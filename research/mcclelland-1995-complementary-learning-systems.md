## Claims
- The organization: "memories are first stored via synaptic changes in the hippocampal system, ... these changes support reinstatement of recent memories in the neocortex, ... neocortical synapses change a little on each reinstatement, and ... remote memory is based on accumulated neocortical changes."
- Why two systems: networks "discover the structure in ensembles of items if learning of each item is gradual and interleaved with learning about other items". Fast learning of new material in the same weights causes "catastrophic interference". The hippocampus exists "to provide a medium for the initial storage of memories in a form that avoids interference with the knowledge already acquired in the neocortical system".
- Why consolidation is slow: "Incorporation takes a long time to allow new knowledge to be interleaved with ongoing exposure to exemplars of the existing knowledge structure."
- Three principles: (1) structure is found by "a gradual, interleaved learning process"; (2) "Attempts to learn new information rapidly ... lead to catastrophic interference"; (3) new material can be added without interference "if new material is incorporated gradually, interleaved with ongoing exposure to examples of the domain".
- Offline reinstatement: reinstatement occurs in task contexts and also "in off-line situations, including active rehearsal, reminiscence, and other inactive states including sleep". The hippocampus is "not just ... a memory store but ... the teacher of the neocortical processing system", in the sense that it "provides training trials". It does not choose cortical representations.
- Context is consolidated only if it is replayed: "If the previous reinstatements had specifically included information about the time and place of initial learning, then this information, too, would gradually become incorporated ... If, however, the reinstatements occur in many different contexts, and if these reinstatements do not include other aspects of the original context of encoding, no reliable memory for any particular context would remain."
- Schematic memory comes from a race: "If the rate of decay is relatively rapid in comparison with the rate of consolidation, much of the idiosyncratic content of individual episodes and events may not be consolidated at all." Shared aspects consolidate first, or are "already" in the weights.
- Self-reinforcement of reinstatement: hippocampal plasticity may be "suppressed during some phases of sleep". "If task-relevant reinstatements were self-reinforcing but spontaneous reinstatements were not, ... memories that remain relevant would tend to persist longer in the hippocampal system."
- Learning rate should fall with experience. The optimal online mean estimate uses step 1/n, so "begin life using relatively large neocortical learning rates ... and then to reduce the learning rate gradually". This is offered as a functional account of critical periods. Infantile amnesia follows if the cortical code changes fast early on: old hippocampal traces "would no longer make sense in terms of the more mature system of neocortical representations."
- Simple first-order cue-outcome relations can be learned fast outside the hippocampus, because they "are generally apparent in much smaller samples of data than higher order relationships". Evolution may add special fast systems, for example taste aversion.
- Salience scales the trace: hippocampal learning "depends on the salience or importance of the original episode", and differences "might be induced by neuromodulatory systems activated in strongly motivated or emotionally charged situations (Gold & McGaugh, 1984)".

## Evidence
- Retrograde gradients (Figure 1). In Kim & Fanselow 1992, rats got 15 tone-shock pairings and lesions at 1, 7, 14 or 28 days. Lesions within a day left context fear near zero, and fear rose with lesion delay over the full 28 days. In Winocur 1990, a lesion at 0 days left "virtually no preference", and a lesion at 5 days was close to control. In Zola-Morgan & Squire 1990, monkeys learned 100 object discriminations. Lesioned animals scored in the "low 60s" at 1 week and peaked at about 70% at 11 weeks, while controls fell from about 80% to about 70%. Lesioned animals can do better on older than on recent material, which the authors take as evidence of real consolidation rather than two stores decaying at different rates.
- Interleaving versus focused learning: a replication of Rumelhart's 1990 semantic network, trained 500 epochs with ε = 0.1, weights initialised uniformly in [-0.5, 0.5], and targets 0.95/0.05. Coarse distinctions appear by epoch 200 (plants versus animals) and fine ones by epoch 500. Adding "penguin can swim, not fly" by focused training learns it fast but makes the net treat all animals, and to a lesser extent plants, as swimmers. Interleaved training learns it slowly "without ever producing more than a slight hint of interference". McCloskey & Cohen's AB-AC network loses AB "before AC performance rises above 0% correct", while humans keep more than 50% AB recall.
- Neocortical simulations of consolidation. Kim & Fanselow: a 16-16-16 network with 20 background pairs, each shown once per simulated day, plus one new environment-tone-shock pair. One epoch equals one day, and the fit falls within the error bars. Zola-Morgan & Squire: a 50-15-1 network with 250 background items each sampled at p = 0.2 per day, and hippocampal traces decaying at D = 0.025/day ("one sixth of its original value in 10 weeks"). Reinstatement probability is r = 0.1 per trial per day, so each discrimination is replayed about 1.5 times a day when fresh and about 0.15 times a day at 10 weeks. Cortical ε = 0.03, retrieval probability R = 0.07, and each condition was run with 200 simulated subjects.
- Table 1, the fits of the two-compartment model (rates per day; columns D_h, C, D_c, S_h(0), S_c(0)): Winocur 0.250, 0.400, 0.075, 0.900, 0.100. Kim & Fanselow 0.050, 0.040, 0.011, 0.800, 0.030. Zola-Morgan & Squire 0.035, 0.020, 0.003, 1.000, 0.100. Squire & Cohen (humans) 0.001, 0.001, 0.001, 0.500, 0.000. The three rates covary across studies.
- LTP decay falls into two groups: about 0.28/day after weak induction and about 0.05/day after strong or repeated induction (Abraham & Otani 1991). These match the fitted D_h for the passive food-preference task (0.25) and for the shock task (0.05).
- Wilson & McNaughton 1994: the correlation structure of 50-100 simultaneously recorded CA1 cells during exploration reappears in later sharp-wave activity during rest and sleep, and is absent before exploration. Sharp waves last about 100 ms.

## Mechanism
- A simple covariance (Hebbian) rule, Eq. 1: Δw_ij = ε(a_i a_j − w_ij). w_ij is then a running average whose effective sample size is about 1/ε. Smaller ε gives a more accurate population estimate.
- The two-compartment consolidation model (Eqs. 3-9):
  - Hippocampal trace: S_h(t) = S_h(0)·e^(−D_h t).
  - Offline reinstatement probability: p(t) = r_h·S_h(t).
  - Cortical trace: dS_c/dt = C·S_h(t)·[1 − S_c(t)] − D_c·S_c(t), with C = ε·r_h.
  - Retrieval: b_h = R_h·S_h, b_c = R_c·S_c, and b_hc = b_h + (1 − b_h)·b_c. Overall correct responding is b_hc + (1 − b_hc)·b_p, where b_p is the chance level.
- The hippocampal code is a compressed "summary sketch" passed through entorhinal cortex. Sparse conjunctive coding keeps the hippocampus from itself suffering catastrophic interference. The authors suggest the encoding and decoding pathways belong to the slow system, so that they stay stable while traces are years old. As a result, the hippocampus remembers material that fits familiar structure far better than random patterns.
- The source of interleaved old material is left open: the environment, reminiscence, or spontaneous reactivation. One proposal is that slow-wave-sleep hippocampal replay primes cortical patterns that are then active in REM, "so that both new and old information" are "played back in closely interleaved fashion".

## Implications for the engine (our inference, not the paper's)
- **Two stores, as two equations to fit.** An episodic buffer holds the day's feature-stream episodes with strength S_h. A slow parametric model, the latent dynamics that would carry temperament and the operator model, learns only from replay drawn with probability ∝ S_h and interleaved with replay of older material. The two-compartment model gives a quantitative baseline: with a few hours of data per day, the consolidation curve of a given episode class is set by (S_h(0), D_h, C, D_c). These can be estimated from the engine's own logs and compared across ablations.
- **Salience sets S_h(0) and D_h; it does not set which weights move.** The paper's 0.28 versus 0.05/day split is a 5-6x difference in persistence between weak and strong events. In the engine, a scalar from the core, for example the size of the interoceptive or homeostatic error at encoding, would set initial strength and decay. Principle 10 then needs that scalar to come out of the core's equations and not be a hand-set "emotional weight".
- **This paper is the clearest derivation of the S6 ablation.** Partner-specific knowledge reaches the slow store only if replays carry the context. If replayed episodes include the tag (who, and the internal state at the time), the slow model learns a context-conditioned map. If tags are stripped, consolidation produces the schematic, context-free average, as in the paper's "no reliable memory for any particular context would remain". So "removing state tagging from replay removes specificity" is predicted directly. A matched control is needed: tag-free replay with the same number and timing of replays.
- **Protecting other relationships.** The penguin result says that focused nightly training on today's operator episodes would overwrite the shared structure, the general model of people and of the engine's own body. Each offline batch must mix today's episodes with replay of older episodes and of background material, at a ratio set by C and the decay rates. A partner-specific mapping that departs from the general schema is the slow, "inconsistent" case (see the Kumaran 2016 note).
- **Waking reinstatement versus sleep replay.** If reinstatement during live interaction refreshes S_h and offline replay does not, traces that keep being relevant with the operator persist and the rest fade. That is a cheap, explicit rule. It is ablatable by letting offline replay also refresh S_h, which should produce runaway rehearsal of early episodes.
- **An annealed learning rate and "infancy".** The 1/n argument supports high early plasticity that is reduced as experience accumulates, which fits the brief's infant-like start. The infantile-amnesia point is a warning for S6's "survive model upgrades": buffered traces stored as latent codes of an old encoder become unreadable after the encoder changes. Principle 7 already stores feature streams rather than latents. The buffer should keep features plus tags and re-encode them on replay, so that its index never points into a dead code.
- **Fast first-order learning.** Simple cue-outcome contingencies with the operator (this voice feature predicts that touch) can be learned directly by the slow store with a larger rate, because they need few samples. Higher-order, configural relations wait for replay.

## Verdict: adopt (the architecture and the two-compartment equations as the quantitative baseline for consolidation)
- The paper gives a principled reason for the split, from stochastic estimation and gradient interdependence, rather than a module choice. That suits Principle 10: if the core is a slowly estimated generative model, the buffer and replay are the core's sampling scheme, not an extra component. That is our framing, not theirs.
- Caveats. The paper does not implement the hippocampus; it is a "black box" source of training trials. Replay frequency and timing are fitted, not derived. "No special distinction" is drawn between semantic and episodic consolidation, and Kumaran 2016 revises the "neocortex is slow" claim.
