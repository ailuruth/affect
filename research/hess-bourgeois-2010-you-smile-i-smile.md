# Hess & Bourgeois 2010, "You smile - I smile: Emotion expression in social interaction" (Biological Psychology 84:514-520, doi 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2009.11.001)

Source read: full article PDF (author-hosted manuscript, ursulakhess.com/resources/HB10.pdf), Study 1 in full, Study 2 results and the general discussion.

## Claims
- In live, face-to-face dyads of strangers telling each other a happy or an anger episode, smiles (mostly Duchenne) were mimicked, frowns were not.
- Mimicry depended on emotional context and on sex composition: men in same-sex dyads, and both sexes in mixed dyads, mimicked smiles less during anger narratives than happy ones; women in same-sex dyads mimicked equally in both.
- Status (manipulated by bogus test scores) did not change mimicry.
- Photos and videos "are not the same as a real life human interaction partner".

## Evidence
- Study 1: 96 same-sex dyads (48 male, 48 female), strangers, 18-30. Study 2: 72 mixed-sex dyads. Each partner narrated one happy and one anger event; role, status (same / higher / lower) and order counterbalanced; experimenter left the room.
- EMG on both partners at once: orbicularis oculi, zygomaticus major, corrugator supercilii, levator labii; 200 ms integration.
- Mimicry = correlation between speaker's and listener's mean EMG across 15-s epochs (z-transformed correlations).
- Study 1: muscle site F(3,492) = 43.71, p < .001. Mean speaker-listener correlations: corrugator r = .08, levator r = .44, orbicularis oculi r = .59, zygomaticus r = .54. Muscle × emotion × sex F(3,492) = 4.11, p = .007. Men: orbicularis oculi r = .68 happy vs .49 anger; zygomaticus .66 vs .50. Women: orbicularis .58 vs .62; zygomaticus .46 vs .53. No effect involving status.
- Study 2: muscle site F(2,232) = 34.09, p < .001; smiles mimicked in both narratives, less in anger narratives; frowns not mimicked.
- Speakers rarely frowned or sneered even when telling anger stories; the authors cite their own finding that people show anger to friends but not strangers.

## What it implies for the design
- In the setting the project targets (live dyad, conversation), the dominant coupled channel is the smile, and the brow channel is close to uncoupled (r = .08). A symmetric copy gain across channels misdescribes live behavior.
- The coupling strength of the same channel depends on what the partner is talking about (anger story vs happy story), at least for some people: content of the episode modulates the face-to-face coupling.
- 15-s epochs are far too coarse for timing; this paper establishes which channels couple, not when.
- Strangers only. The anger result may change with familiarity (the authors' own aside), which is exactly the operator case and is not tested here.

## Verdict: adopt (as the live-dyad baseline for strangers)
