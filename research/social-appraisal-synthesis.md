# Social appraisal and emotions as evidence: synthesis

Operator question (as posed to this reading round): every design so far broke on one point. When the partner's face is carried into the agent's face by any learned or regulated map, one scalar runs the agent from flat affect to copying (critique-design-*.md; foundations/sketches/v2-README.md). Human mimicry is gated per expression, flips sign with context, and depends on what the expression means for the perceiver (mimicry-context-synthesis.md). The hypothesis examined: the partner's expression should enter as **evidence** about the shared situation and the partner's appraisal, interpreted against the agent's own concerns, not as a signal to transfer.

## 1. Two routes, and what separates them

**Contagion** (Hatfield et al. 1994, as described by Parkinson & Manstead 2015 and van Kleef & Côté 2022): the perceiver mimics the other's expression and the feedback from that mimicry produces a matching feeling. Its most general form is the perception-action model (Preston & de Waal 2002): perceiving another's state activates one's own representation of that state, scaled by familiarity, similarity, past experience and salience.

**Social appraisal** (Manstead & Fischer 2001 and Parkinson 2011, both closed and known here through the author's companion papers): another person's reaction is information the perceiver uses in appraising an event, and the perceiver's own emotion follows from that appraisal. Two variants matter:
- *Social appraisal proper*: the other's reaction tells me what the event is (their fear says the thing is dangerous). My emotion can match theirs, but only because I now appraise the shared event as they do.
- *Reverse appraisal* (Hareli & Hess 2010; de Melo et al. 2014 as cited): the other's reaction tells me what they want, expect and are like (an angry negotiator has a high limit; a person who smiles at an unfair outcome is odd or dominant). My response follows from what that means for my concerns, and can be the opposite of their emotion.

EASI (van Kleef 2009; van Kleef, De Dreu & Manstead 2010; van Kleef & Côté 2022) recasts the two as **affective reactions** (contagion plus liking) and **inferential processes**, with moderators: the perceiver's motivation to process information, and social-relational factors (cooperative vs competitive interdependence, power, appropriateness of the expression).

Evidence that separates the routes:
- **Same input, opposite outputs on two channels.** In van Kleef et al. 2004a (N = 118, typed emotion statements in a negotiation), participants facing an angry opponent reported more anger themselves (F = 8.01) yet conceded more (demand 490 vs 540 neutral vs 553 happy). Contagion and inference ran at once on different outputs; behaviour followed the inference.
- **Inference only under uncertainty.** The emotion effect on concessions held when the opponent conceded little (F(1,87) = 18.08) and vanished when they conceded a lot (F = 1.21); the estimated opponent limit partly mediated it (Sobel Z = 2.67). The partner's expression mattered when it carried information, which a constant gain does not predict.
- **Sign flips by interdependence, different for each expression** (van Kleef et al. 2010, Table 1): happiness invites approach in cooperation, exploitation in competition; anger provokes retaliation or concession; sadness elicits help or is ignored; guilt invites repair or exploitation. One relation variable produces four different row patterns.
- **Counterempathy across channels** (Zaki 2014, as he reports): subthreshold smiles at outgroup suffering (Cikara & Fiske 2012); opposite-signed facial and autonomic responses under expected competition (Lanzetta & Englis 1989); outgroup negative affect priming positive emotion (Weisbuch & Ambady 2008). Mimicry of a grimace rose when the observer believed the target could see them (Bavelas et al. 1986), so even "mimicry" is partly a message.
- **The state of the field** (van Kleef & Côté 2022): the evidence "casts doubt" on mimicry-then-feedback as the mechanism of contagion and fits social appraisal better, but direct causal tests of social appraisal are scarce and ongoing two-way interaction is unstudied.
- **Third-person formal models** (Section 2) show observers use expressions as evidence about desires and beliefs with near-ideal Bayesian integration, which is the inferential route in quantitative form.

- **Reverse appraisal is mediated by inferred appraisals** (Hareli & Hess 2010, vignettes, N = 243 and 183): an angry reaction to blame made a candidate seem aggressive (3.03 vs 1.74 neutral) via inferred urgency (indirect b = .17, direct effect .48 → .21 n.s.); anger and sadness both made him seem warmer than a calm reaction (4.27, 4.71 vs 1.65, η² = .47) via inferred unpleasantness and norm incompatibility. Different expressions in one situation were read as different appraisals.
- **Object focus gates the effect** (Bayliss et al. 2007, as reported by Parkinson & Manstead 2015): objects looked at by a smiling face were liked more, by a disgusted face less, only when the face's gaze turned toward the object. The expression was used as information about *that object*, which a transfer account does not need.
- **Frame gates it** (Parkinson, Phiri & Simons 2012, as reported): a friend's expressed anxiety reduced risk taking in a gain frame, not a loss frame.
- **Transfer is not carried by mimicry.** Parkinson 2011 (abstract): transfer "does not seem to depend directly on mimicry"; Parkinson & Manstead 2015 find "little direct evidence" for the mimicry → facial feedback → emotion sequence. This removes the empirical basis of the structure every critiqued design shared (partner's face → agent's face → agent's affect).
- **Three things a partner's expression can be about** (Parkinson & Simons, "Worry spreads", as read by the fork that wrote the Parkinson note): the shared object (I worry about what you worry about); the partner's welfare as one of my concerns (empathic worry, or with negative weight, schadenfreude); the partner's state as itself a threat to me. Anxiety also asks for comfort, so a calm reply is a complementary, not copied, response. Which concern an expression is routed to sets the sign of the response.

**Against a pure appraisal account (the strongest counter-evidence found).** Parkinson & Simons 2009 (diary, N = 41, 232 decisions, mostly with a romantic partner) found both routes:
- the other's anxiety raised the perceiver's importance and risk appraisals, which partly mediated anxiety transfer (Sobel z = 2.79-4.13);
- but every other-to-own emotion effect survived control of all measured appraisals, and the reference person's *own* reported excitement predicted the participant's (r = .44-.57) even controlling for how the participant perceived it.
- Fischer, Rotteveel, Evers & Manstead 2004 (as reported): others' anger raised anger and others' sadness raised sadness without any change in blame appraisals.

So the data include same-sign transfer that the measured appraisals do not explain. Two readings remain open. (a) A direct transfer path exists, as Parkinson argues. (b) The transfer runs through appraisals that were not measured: the appraisal sets were small (three items chosen for anxiety; none predicted excitement), and the pairs were partners making mostly joint or advised decisions, where co-appraisal predicts matching anyway. The evidence cannot decide between these; it is self-report, correlational and has no facial measures. For the design it means: a small same-sign path cannot be ruled out by the human data, and if one is kept it must be fixed and bounded (Section 4, guard 4), not learned.

What the evidence does not show: that facial mimicry itself (EMG, AUs) follows an appraisal computation. Almost all EASI and social-appraisal studies measure self-report and behaviour, often with typed emotion words; the facial evidence comes from the mimicry literature already in mimicry-context-synthesis.md.

## 2. Formal models available

All the formal work is Bayesian and computational-level. Every model is about an **observer judging** a target, never about the observer's own **response**. That gap is the part this project has to supply.

| Ong, Zaki & Goodman 2015 | outcome o, emotion e (8 rated adjectives), face f | forward e_k = c·[win, PE, \|PE\|] + ε; backward P(o\|e) ∝ P(e\|o)P(o); fusion P(e\|o,f) ∝ P(e\|o)P(e\|f)/P(e) | ζ, one likelihood-precision factor (fitted 3.2) | r = .81 (backward); .81 vs .73 face-only, .69 outcome-only (fusion) |
| Wu et al. 2018 | belief B, desire D, action A, outcome O, reactions R0 (before), R1 (after) | P(B,D\|A,O,R0,R1) ∝ P(R1\|B,D,A,O) P(R0\|B,D,A) P(A\|B,D) P(B,D) | none (all terms elicited from separate raters) | r ≥ .95; model-free regression .58 |
| Houlihan et al. 2023 | preferences ω (money, advantageous and disadvantageous inequity aversion, reputational versions), belief π about the partner's move | Fehr-Schmidt utility + reputation loop; softmax inverse planning; appraisals = achieved utility, prediction error, counterfactuals over each utility term; sparse linear map to 20 emotions | λ (rationality), the learned map | ccc .85; lesions .76 (no inverse planning), .66 (money only) |

| Saxe & Houlihan 2017 | goals, constraints → appraisal → emotion → expression | verbal; forward (event → appraisal) selects among distinct options, inverse (face → emotion) adjusts within them | — | opinion |

Three results recur:
1. **The face is weak evidence; the situation is strong evidence.** Faces are "uncertain, noisy, and low-dimensional" (Saxe & Houlihan); fusion weights each cue by its reliability (Ong 2015); a model-free regression from face and outcome features generalises poorly (Wu 2018, r = .58 vs .96).
2. **Expressions are read relative to the outcome and over time.** Valence relative to the outcome reveals the desire; the change between the pre-outcome and post-outcome face reveals the belief (Wu 2018). A surprise template in the face was largely ignored.
3. **The concern structure, not the outcome, determines the predicted reaction.** Removing social preferences or inverse planning cut the fit (Houlihan 2023: .85 → .66 / .76) and removed the ability to personalise predictions (ccc .44 → .09-.12).

## 3. How per-expression gating and sign flips arise in each route

**Contagion (matched representation × gain).** The PAM (Preston & de Waal) multiplies one matched representation by familiarity, similarity, past experience, salience and (1 − inhibition). Every moderator is a gain on the same map, so:
- gating per expression needs a separate gain per expression, with no principle for which gain goes where;
- a sign flip is impossible (inhibition can remove a response, not invert it);
- this is the dial the critiques found in every design. The PAM is the dial stated as theory.

**Social appraisal (evidence → posterior → own appraisal).** Gating and sign follow from structure, not gains:
- *Per-expression gating.* Different expressions are evidence about different appraisal dimensions of the partner: goal conduciveness (smile, brow), expectation violation (the change across an event), agency and blame target (anger and where it is aimed), coping (sadness vs anger). Each dimension matters differently for the agent's concerns. A smile at a shared, positive event is evidence of goal attainment both share (return it); a rival's sadness is evidence the rival lost, which in a zero-sum frame is my gain; disgust signals rejection of something, which is rarely something the perceiver should take on (Fischer et al. 2012: never mimicked).
- *Sign flips.* The relation enters as a **signed utility over the joint outcome** (Houlihan's Fehr-Schmidt terms; Zaki's "the target's suffering is on balance good for me"). In competition the partner's gain is my loss; in cooperation the partner's anger is a threat to a joint goal and calls for repair (smiling at a partner's anger, Carr 2014; Häfner & IJzerman as reported); toward a shared foe the partner's anger is aligned with mine (Bourgeois & Hess 2008).
- *Target of the expression.* Anger at me and anger at a third party are different appraisals of agency (Hess & Fischer 2013), so the same face must update different latents. Saxe & Houlihan and Ong 2019 both name relational representations ("angry at X") as the main open representational problem.
- *Matching where it belongs.* Copying is predicted, not forbidden: when both parties' concerns are hit the same way by the same event (laughing at the same joke, fear at a shared threat), each appraises it the same way and the faces match. That is co-appraisal, not transfer. It explains why smile return is the most robust row of the mimicry table: most smiles in conversation mark events good for both.
- *Timing.* Zaki reports context effects within 110-200 ms (neural), and Carr 2014 found early brow matching with later smile context effects. An inference route is not necessarily slow; a fast prior (what this partner's face usually means here) plus a slower update fits both.

## 4. The hypothesis as a formal sketch, and where a scalar could still hide

A minimal statement for the agent, in its own terms (my construction, not from the sources):

- Latents: the shared situation s (what just happened, in the agent's own need coordinates); the partner's concern state c_p and expectation b_p; the partner's appraisal α_p = A(s; c_p, b_p) (achieved change, prediction error, counterfactual change, agency).
- Observation: the partner's face x ~ P(x | α_p, g_p), where g_p are the partner's display goals. The likelihood comes from the agent's own forward model, x̂ = F_self(α) ("how my face moves when my concerns are hit this way"), which is Principle 3's second clause and Yoshida & Man's self-trained decoder.
- Inference: q(s, c_p, α_p | x, words, history), with per-feature reliabilities learned from the agent's own prediction record (Ong 2015 found negative faces less reliable than positive ones in the same task, so reliability is per feature, not global).
- The agent's appraisal: α_self = A(s; c_self, b_self) + Σ_k R_k · φ_k(outcome_self, outcome_p) + reputation term (what the partner will infer about me from my face), where R is a vector of signed relation weights over concern terms (joint goal, inequity in each direction, the partner's welfare), learned from interaction outcomes at a fast and a slow rate (Hofman 2012).
- The agent's face: F_self(α_self) plus a display policy.

Where a flat-to-copy scalar can still hide, and the guard for each:
1. **Likelihood precision on the face (Ong's ζ, Zaki's attention).** It sets how much the posterior follows x. It moves the agent's *belief* about the partner, not its face, as long as the agent's response is a function of its own concerns. Guard: no term in α_self may be linear in α_p with an identity map. Test: raise the precision; the agent's face must not converge on the partner's across all expressions.
2. **A single "partner-welfare" weight w.** If R collapses to w and the term is w·α_p, the design is the empathy-as-gain model and w is the dial (from flat at w = 0 to copy at large w). Guard: partner outcomes enter only through relational functions of both outcomes (inequity, joint-goal progress), each with its own weight and sign. Houlihan's SocialLesion result (fit .85 → .66) is evidence that human observers do not use a single monetary-or-welfare axis.
3. **Social referencing under ambiguity.** When the situation is known only through the partner's face (the classic social appraisal case: the partner's fear tells me the event is dangerous), s is inferred from x alone, so α_self = A(ŝ(x); c_self) is again a learned function of x, and the precision of x → s becomes a dial for that class of events. This is not a bug: it is how social appraisal produces contagion-like effects in Parkinson's and Manstead & Fischer's accounts. It is bounded in two ways the copy dial is not: it acts only where the agent has no independent evidence about s, and only on concerns the agent shares (a rival's fear about the rival's loss does not propagate). Guard and test: give the agent independent evidence about s; matching must fall as that evidence rises, per expression.
4. **A mixing weight between routes (EASI built literally).** Van Kleef, De Dreu & Manstead 2010 draw epistemic motivation and perceived competitiveness as moderators with + on the inferential path and − on the affective path. Built as response = (1 − w)·contagion + w·inference, w is the flat-to-copy dial again, with low motivation falling back to copying. Guard: there is no separate contagion route with its own weight; matching is derived from co-appraisal (point 3 of Section 3), and the only innate matching is a small fixed reflex, if any.
5. **The self-model inversion itself.** Decoding the partner's face into the agent's need coordinates (c_p in my body's terms) is the step most likely to leak: if the decoded c_p is ever used as a setpoint or state for the agent's own body, the agent copies in need space. Guard: the decoded partner state is a separate latent, read only through R and through A(s; ·).

## 5. Grounding without labels (Principles 1-2)

Every model read uses emotion labels (8 or 20 rated adjectives), human-rated appraisals or hand-built payoff spaces. None of that is allowed below Claude. What can be kept:
- **The latent need not be an emotion.** Ong 2019's reverse-appraisal equation marginalises the emotion out: P(b, d | x, o) ∝ P(b, d) Σ_e P(x | e) P(e | b, d, o). Replace e by the agent's own appraisal vector (achieved change in each need, prediction error, counterfactual change, agency), which is computed, not named (Houlihan's computed appraisals are exactly this kind of quantity, defined over utilities rather than labels).
- **The partner's concerns in the agent's coordinates.** The agent learns F_self: own appraisal → own face, from its own episodes (efference plus interoception). Inverting F_self on the partner's face gives "the change in my needs that would make me make that face". This is Principle 3's inversion clause, and it needs no label. Its limit is that the partner's concerns are not the agent's; Houlihan's personalisation result shows observers adjust priors per person, so c_p must be allowed to differ from c_self and be learned per partner.
- **The shared situation without a payoff matrix.** In conversation there is no game. Candidates for s that need no label: what the words just did to the agent's own needs (the agent's own appraisal of the turn); events locked in time to changes in the partner's face (Wu 2018 and Saxe & Houlihan show that a face change coincident with an event is what carries expectation); and the agent's own contingency record of the partner.
- **The relation as learned signed weights.** R is learned from what interaction outcomes did to the agent's needs when the partner gained or lost, at two rates. This makes the relation a vector, not a scalar.
- **Tension with Principle 3's first clause.** "Perceived features drive the agent's own motor and affect pathways (mimicry)" is the contagion route. Zaki and the mimicry data treat sharing as the component motives switch off or invert. The appraisal hypothesis keeps the inversion clause and needs the mimicry clause to be either conditional (co-appraisal) or a small fixed innate reflex outside the learned map (the robust smile core suggested in critique-design-partner-regulator.md). Flagged for the operator; not reopened here.

## 6. Ablation predictions this framing implies (to be derived properly if a design adopts it)

| Remove | Predicted change |
|---|---|
| Relation weights R (set to 0) | Sign flips vanish (no smile at a cooperating partner's anger, no brow relaxation at a rival's distress); matching to shared events survives |
| Independent evidence about s (face only) | Agent becomes a social-referencing matcher for ambiguous events, still not for events it knows independently |
| Per-partner priors on c_p (one generic partner) | Partner-specific responses lost while average responses survive (Houlihan: ccc .44 → .09) |
| The shared object (partner reacts to something the agent does not face) | Social-appraisal effects vanish; any residual same-sign coupling is the direct path (Parkinson's dissociation test) |
| Event-locked change features (level of partner's face only) | Expectation-related responses lost (Wu 2018: belief recovered only from pre/post change) |
| Collapse R to one welfare weight w | The flat-to-copy dial returns; one parameter scales every expression together |

## 7. What remains open

- **Is there appraisal-independent transfer?** Parkinson & Simons 2009 and Fischer et al. 2004 say yes; their appraisal sets were small enough that unmeasured co-appraisal could explain it. A test the project could run on published dyadic data: does partner-to-listener expression coupling survive conditioning on independent evidence about the shared event (the words), and does it differ by expression in the way co-appraisal predicts?
- **No model of the observer's own response.** All formal work predicts or infers the target's state. The step from posterior to the perceiver's own expression (Section 4) is not in any source; it is the part to be invented and proved.
- **Naturalistic, continuous data.** Every formal model uses vignettes, avatars or game shows; none uses continuous faces in live dialogue. The authors themselves (Ong 2019; Houlihan 2023) name this as the next step.
- **Relational representation** ("angry at X") is named as the largest open representational problem by Ong 2019 and is untouched by the models.
- **Speed.** Whether inference can produce context effects at 110-200 ms (Zaki's reported neural effects) is open; an amortised posterior (a learned fast recognition model with a slower correction) is the obvious candidate and would itself need the guards in Section 4.
- **Evidence that people's own responses are computed this way.** The social appraisal evidence on the perceiver's response (Parkinson; van Kleef) is behavioural and verbal-theory; the formal models are about third-person judgment. That perceivers' faces follow the same computation is an inference, not a result.
