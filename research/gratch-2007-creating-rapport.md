# Creating Rapport with Virtual Agents (Gratch, Wang, Gerten, Fast, Duffy; IVA 2007)

Gratch, J., Wang, N., Gerten, J., Fast, E., Duffy, R. "Creating Rapport with Virtual Agents." Intelligent Virtual Agents (IVA 2007), LNCS 4722, 125-138. doi:10.1007/978-3-540-74997-4_12. Author PDF: people.ict.usc.edu/gratch/public_html/GratchIVA07-rapport.pdf.

## What was read
- Full text of the author PDF (10 pages): introduction, Rapport Agent description, method, measures, results with Table 1, discussion and future work. Table 1 is jumbled in the text extraction, so I quote only numbers that also appear in the prose.

## Claims
- The Rapport Agent produces listener feedback "by realtime analysis of acoustic properties of speech (detecting backchannel opportunity points, disfluencies, questions, and loudness) and speaker gestures (detecting head nods, shakes, gaze shifts and posture shifts)", "driven according to predefined behavior-mapping rules". In this study "Facial expressions were not generated."
- Four between-subjects conditions with N = 131: Face-to-face (20 speakers), Mediated (a real listener's tracked head and posture rendered on an avatar; 20), Responsive (Rapport Agent; 24), Non-contingent (the Responsive feedback stream replayed to a different speaker, yoked for frequency; 24).
- "Results suggest that contingency matters when it comes to creating rapport and that agent generated behavior was as good as human listeners in creating rapport."

## Evidence
- Contingency (paired by feedback tape): with the non-contingent agent, speakers produced more words (M = 458.83 vs 299.17, t(11) = 2.17, p = .05), more pause fillers (15.75 vs 8.00, t(11) = 3.06, p = .01), a higher pause-filler rate (6.11 vs 3.80, t(11) = 2.44, p = .03) and more disfluencies (22.17 vs 11.75, t(11) = 2.30, p = .04).
- The Responsive agent's self-reported rapport did not differ from face-to-face. However, "The responsive agent ... was found to be less trustworthy than human listeners." The non-contingent agent "was also found to be more distracting than a real human listener."
- The Mediated avatar (real human motion, no face) elicited less rapport and was "less helpful and more distracting" than either the human or the responsive agent. The authors suggest this may be because "human listeners often responded with facial feedback that would be seen in the face-to-face condition but was not recognized or displayed".

## Failures documented
- **Feedback that ignores content.** "The Rapport Agent responds without attending to the content of the speaker's narrative. Such feedback has been called envelope feedback". Citing Bavelas et al., the authors say envelope feedback "can be woefully inadequate in certain contexts": when speakers told personally emotional stories, "storytellers expected emotional feedback to key events in the story and found it hard to construct effective narratives without it."
- **Timing-free feedback costs fluency.** The same nods at the same rate, decoupled from the speaker, produced about twice the disfluencies (22.17 vs 11.75).
- **No face.** Neither the agent nor the mediated avatar showed facial expression, and the mediated condition's deficit is attributed partly to the missing face.
- **Rapport is a single-session construct.** "within the virtual human's community, rapport has been conceptualized as short-term construct that arises in a single interaction ... or as a deepening sense of interdependence that arises over time". The study tests only the first.
- **Lower trust than a human** despite equal rapport ratings.
