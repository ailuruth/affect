# van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg 1990, "Cross-Cultural Consistency of Coding the Strange Situation" (Infant Behavior and Development 13:469-485)

Source read: the PDF on three-mode.leidenuniv.nl (17 pp.). Read: abstract, introduction, the Classifications method paragraph on the Richters, Waters & Vaughn (1988) functions, and the Discussion opening. Tables 1-4 and the principal component analysis were NOT read in detail. Richters et al. 1988 itself was NOT read; its figures below are as quoted here.

## Claims
- "Infants' behavior during reunion with the parent after a short separation is classified into three main categories"; "Some resistant children are unable to settle within the 3-min reunion episodes".
- "except for distance interaction, the interactive scales in the two reunion episodes were scored in accordance with the original coding rules", across seven investigators in six countries.

## Evidence
- Scales checked: "proximity seeking, contact maintaining, avoidance, resistance, and distance interaction". "Coding these scales is the first step in classifying Strange Situations."
- Sample means were tested against Ainsworth et al. 1978 Tables 13, 32 and 33 by single-sample z tests, with Bonferroni critical z = 2.78.
- Richters et al. (1988) classification functions use "weighted scores for proximity seeking, contact maintaining, avoidance, resistance, and crying in the reunion episodes". They were developed on "255 Strange Situations conducted and coded by Ainsworth and her colleagues". Reported agreement: "approximately 91% agreement on B vs. non-B decisions, and 94% on A vs. C decisions; ... kappa values were .82 and .88". Full A-B-C agreement is "88% and .79". Without crying it is "85%" and "kappa is .73".
- This multinational set: "mean percentage of (A-B-C) agreement ... is 83%, and the mean kappa is .66."
- Distance interaction diverged: "All samples show more distance interaction than the original sample", and the authors suspect "coding rules may have been described somewhat more loosely than those for the other scales."

## Verdict: watch
It shows that the Strange Situation's categorical judgment can be reproduced from four reunion-episode scale scores by published discriminant functions (kappa about .7-.8). If an embodied Bot were ever scored on Ainsworth's scales as published, those functions would give a non-holistic classification. For the current screen-bound Bot the scales themselves do not apply (see ainsworth-1978-strange-situation.md), so this is reference only. It also warns that distance interaction, the one scale a screen agent could plausibly show, is the least reliably coded.
