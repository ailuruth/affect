## Claims
- Functional flexibility: "a single vocal category expresses positive, negative, and neutral emotional states on different occasions." It is the property that lets the same sentence celebrate, complain or describe. Classical ethology's "fixed signals" and nonhuman primate calls (which vary by context but have not been shown to vary in valence) lack it.
- Three protophones, the infant's speech-like precursors, defined acoustically relative to the individual infant: squeals (very high pitch), vocants (vowel-like, mid-range pitch), growls (harsh voice quality or low pitch). Cry and laugh are treated as species-specific fixed signals.
- Data: longitudinal recordings of 9 infants at three ages in the first year; 6,995 utterances located. Vocal type coded from audio only; facial affect (positive = smiling or grinning, negative = frowning or grimacing, neutral = neither) coded from video only, by different passes.
- Protophones were mostly neutral (mean 64%), cries and laughs almost never (4%). All three protophones occurred with substantial positive and negative faces; the six contrasts (protophones more positive than cry, less than laugh, more neutral than both, less negative than cry, more negative than laugh) were all significant by odds ratio. Protophones were far more frequent than cries and laughs.
- Not random either: independence failed, chi^2(4, n = 6,535) = 148, p < .001; vocants were 9.1 SD more likely than expected to be neutral, squeals 10.0 SD less likely (more positive and more negative), growls more positive than expected.
- Individual differences: each of the 9 infants had its own pattern of protophone-affect pairing; cry and laugh showed no individual differences.
- Illocution and perlocution: over 80% of positive-affect protophones were coded as taking part in protoconversation; about 90% of negative ones as complaints or pleas for help; neutral ones mostly indeterminate. Caregivers encouraged after 88% of positive protophones and tried to change the situation after about 75% of negative ones (all p < .001; replicated by a second coder working from audio-only caregiver speech).
- Flexibility held across all five situational contexts, including the infant playing alone.
- Reliability on 21% of data: kappa .60 for protophone category (read as fuzzy, emerging categories), .91 for cry and laugh, .73 for facial affect.
- Flexibility is present by 3-4 months; at 10-12 months protophone qualities ride on canonical babbling with the full affect range kept. The authors place flexibility before syntax, words, joint attention and canonical babbling as a foundation of language, and suggest it as an early hominin divergence from other apes.

## Evidence with numbers
n = 9 infants, 6,995 utterances; chi^2 = 148; 64% vs 4% neutral; 80-90% illocution and 75-88% perlocution consistency; kappas .60/.73/.91.

## What it implies for an engine whose voice comes from an innate vocal body and is learned by living with people
- Two kinds of innate vocalization, with different coupling to state:
  - Fixed signals (cry, laugh): hard-wired to valence, no individual differences. In the engine these are reflexes with a fixed sign, part of the innate body (Principle 2 allows a few reflexes).
  - Protophones: produced by the vocal body's basic settings (pitch extreme high, mid, low or harsh) with no fixed valence; their affective use is learned and individual. In the engine, the vocal body's parameters (pitch level, roughness, tract openness) must not be hard-wired to valence; the mapping from internal state to vocal settings should be partly free and shaped by experience, which is what lets an individual manner of speaking emerge.
- The data argue against a fixed state-to-voice table (e.g. Scherer's predictions applied as a lookup). The innate part is a coarse bias (squeals at affective extremes, vocants near neutral), and the rest is learned use. This reconciles banse-scherer-1996-acoustic-profiles.md (systematic adult profiles) with the long-term goal (manner acquired by living).
- The face disambiguates the voice: valence was read from the face while the sound stayed the same category. The engine's face and voice should be driven from the same state but through different maps, so that the face can carry the valence the voice leaves open.
- Caregiver responses differed by the infant's affect (encouragement vs repair), giving the contingency structure that goldstein-schwade-2008-social-feedback-babbling.md shows drives vocal learning. The operator's differential responses to the engine's vocalizations are the training signal.
- Vocalizing alone (solo play) still occurs with affect: the engine's vocal output should not be only a response to the operator; spontaneous vocalization driven by its own state is part of the innate behaviour.
