## Claims
This is a conceptual review with almost no numbers. The critique of homeostatic, drive-based motivation is stated in the source as follows, as strongly as the source states it.

1. Drive reduction is false as a theory of reward. "The drive reduction concept of reward is so intuitive that it was thought to be self-evident for decades ... All the more pity, perhaps, that the idea turns out not to be true. Drive reduction is not really a chief mechanism of reward." On the brain-stimulation tests: "the theory was not true, and nearly all of the predictions based on it turned out to be wrong. In many cases, the opposite results were found instead." Drive reduction is listed among "outdated concepts (such as drive reduction), whose primary use now is to delineate how brains might have motivated behavior (but do not)".
2. The same manipulation both creates the drive and rewards. Lateral hypothalamic sites "where the stimulation caused eating behavior were almost always the same sites where stimulation was rewarding ... Eating electrodes were not punishing electrodes. Instead, the eating electrodes were reward electrodes." Therefore "reward could not be due to drive reduction. The reward electrode increased the motivation to eat, it did not reduce that drive."
3. Reducing the deficit without the sensory incentive does not satisfy. Dogs "intravenously fed the full amount of nutrients they would ordinarily eat ... still consumed their normal meals by mouth"; "They quickly become overweight, but still continued to eat." In Miller & Kessen, "Rats quickly learned to run to their tasty drink, but merely walked for the drive reduction of intragastric intubation." The fistula patient Tom chewed and spat food because "introducing (food) directly into his stomach failed to satisfy his appetite".
4. Incentive motivation without deficit. "rats avidly drank saccharin solutions that had no nutrients", and male rats "worked to gain brief access to a female for copulatory intromission, even if no time was allowed for the subsequent ejaculation". People overeat "despite no homeostatic deficits and even in the face of a body-weight surplus."
5. Expressing a motivation often increases it rather than reducing it. "behavioral expressions of motivation often do not reduce the motivation but, instead, actually primes or enhances its subsequent intensity" (the "'cocktail peanut' phenomenon"). Priming "is explained better by incentive motivation concepts".
6. Anticipatory motivation is not homeostatic in mechanism. It occurs "before a deficit ever occurs", so "the homeostatic comparison and error detector mechanism ... cannot be the mechanism that triggers anticipatory prevention response". "In real life, most instances of eating and drinking may occur in the absence of classical homeostatic deficits." Berridge concedes that the anticipatory thirst "is real" and may use "some of the same brain mechanisms".
7. Setpoints may be fictions. Stability can come from settling points, "a stable state caused by a balance of opposing forces, but without any setpoint or error detection". "The trend in thinking now seems to be that setpoints are misleading for understanding how brain systems of hunger or thirst really work."
8. 'Wanting' and 'liking' separate. "'Liking' is essentially hedonic impact"; "'Wanting', or incentive salience, is the motivational incentive value of the same reward ... not a sensory pleasure." "'Liking' without 'wanting' can be produced, and so can 'wanting' without 'liking'." Under lateral hypothalamic stimulation, rats' reactions to sweet taste "became more aversive, if anything ... although the same electrode made them eat."
9. Cue-triggered wanting. Incentive salience "can tag certain stimulus representations" and makes them "attractive, attention grabbing". "Cue-triggered 'wanting' does not require understanding of causal relations about the hedonic outcome." It "may lead to irrational 'wants' for outcomes that are neither liked nor even expected to be liked." In addiction, sensitization "can last for years", so cues trigger relapse in people who "have been drug-free and out of withdrawal for months or years".
10. Physiological state modulates incentive value; it does not drive behaviour directly. Following Toates, "physiological depletion states could enhance the incentive value of their goal stimuli", an "essentially ... multiplicative interaction between physiological deficit and external stimulus". The interaction is "three-way", including predictive cues (alliesthesia; Cabanac).
11. Dedicated drive neurons and peptides. Valenstein's work "effectively put an end to the plausibility of the dedicated drive neurons in lateral hypothalamus". The modern version ("dedicated hunger peptides ... neuropeptide Y or agouti-related protein") "may turn out to be as misleading". NPY can produce "a conditioned aversion to the taste of the food it made a rat eat."

## Evidence
A narrative review of the classic literature: Miller, Olds, Valenstein, Sheffield, Young, Bolles, Bindra, Toates, Cabanac, and the author's own taste-reactivity work. The only date and time spans given are historical ("between 1930 and 1970", "in the past 50 years", the 1960s brain-stimulation era). The review reports no new data and no effect sizes.

## Verdict: adopt (as the critique our design must answer, point by point)
This is the strongest published case against a drive-based account of affect. For (c), the claims our design must answer, in order of threat:
- Points 2 and 8: activating the deficit circuit can itself be rewarding, and wanting can rise while liking falls. A single scalar costate broadcast as "the affect" cannot express wanting without liking. At minimum the design needs a separate incentive channel attached to cues, distinct from the shadow price of the need.
- Points 4 and 9: incentives and cues motivate without any deficit, and the motivation can outlast it for years. A costate ∂V/∂H is zero wherever V does not depend on H. So anything the agent pursues that is not tied to a body variable (social contact, novelty) falls outside it. Keramati & Gutkin 2014 concede this explicitly (keramati-gutkin-2014-homeostatic-rl.md).
- Point 6 is partly answered by a value-gradient rather than a drive signal. A learned V gives a nonzero gradient before the deficit exists, and the AgRP data (chen-2015-sensory-detection-arcuate.md, betley-2015-agrp-negative-valence.md) show a need signal moving on cues before the body changes. Berridge's text grants that anticipatory thirst is "real" and shares mechanisms. His claim is that the trigger is not error detection, which a forward-looking value does not use either.
- Point 7: our "setpoint" should be treated as a modelling convenience. Keramati & Gutkin argue that a settling-point system can be rewritten with an energy function, so the drive form survives as notation.
- Point 11 cuts against reading AgRP as "the hunger neuron". Betley's own finding of a positive-valence lateral hypothalamic counterpart is consistent with this point.
- Point 5 (priming) is a falsifiable check: after a first small intake, a pure drive account predicts lower motivation, while incentive accounts predict higher.
