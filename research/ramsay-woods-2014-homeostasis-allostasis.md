# Ramsay & Woods 2014, "Clarifying the roles of homeostasis and allostasis in physiological regulation" (Psychological Review 121(2):225-247)

## Claims
- Bernard and Cannon did not posit set points: "neither Bernard nor Cannon actually considered the value of a regulated variable to be maintained at an invariant level or set point." The authors continue: "whereas Sterling and Eyer (1988) and many others claim that homeostasis implies an invariant set point, it was not in fact included in Cannon’s definition; rather ... the application of engineer control theory into consideration of biological regulation incorporated the existence of a set point".
- Anticipation does not distinguish the two models: "Adjusting the homeostatic canon to recognize the ability of organisms to take advantage of past experience in order to preempt likely perturbations simply shifts the activity of inevitable corrective responses to an earlier time point." Table 1: "Anticipatory responses do not distinguish allostasis from contemporary views of homeostasis."
- Adjustable set points predate allostasis: "25 years prior to Sterling and Eyer’s (1988) assertion that allostatic regulation was needed because homeostasis is limited to having an invariant set point, others had already modified the model of homeostatic regulation to include an adjustable set point".
- The set point itself is rejected in favour of a balance point (Romanovsky, Kanosue):
  - "the stabilized level of a regulated variable is better conceived as a ‘balance point’ that reflects the summed action of all of the influences on that variable".
  - Each effector loop has "its own threshold (or ‘set point’) for activation", and there is "no central integrator that coordinates effector activity".
  - Table 1: "‘Balance point’ theory is better able to account for physiological regulation. There is no regulated variable, no set point, and no error signal."
- Their own pushback on balance-point theory: it is "cumbersome" because "simply knowing that the level of the balance point has increased is uninformative about underlying effector activity" (citing Kanosue).
- Allostasis redefined as dysregulation: "we suggest that allostasis be considered a dysregulatory (or disordered) form of physiological regulation that involves effector loops that over-respond in magnitude or duration and/or that compete concurrently with other effectors." The three signatures, verbatim:
  1. "an effector response that eventually overcorrects an initial perturbation (i.e., causes a sign-reversal) despite the continued presence of the disturbance"
  2. "an effector response that remains active even though the disturbance that elicited it is no longer present and the continued effector activity is causing a persistent shift in the balance point"
  3. "concurrent effector competition caused by triggering events that occur in a manner or situation not anticipated in an evolutionary sense"
- Measurement rule: "distinguishing homeostasis and allostasis cannot be accomplished by evaluating the balance level of the regulated variable and instead requires measurement of the underlying effectors."
- Robustness and fragility (citing Csete & Doyle 2002 and Kitano 2004): "while highly complex regulatory systems can be quite robust to cope with naturalistic disturbances, this increase in robustness comes at the cost of an equal increase in fragility". They add: "many instances of allostatic regulation may be the result of exposed fragilities in homeostatic regulation."
- Self-assessment: "we realize that these suggestions are speculative and likely to be revised."

## Evidence
A theoretical review. Its empirical anchors are thermoregulation physiology (independent thermoeffector loops) and sign-reversal studies (opioid-induced hyperalgesia; nicotine and ethanol hyperthermia in rats; osmoregulation). No new data and no formal model.

## Verdict: adopt (as the pushback on Sterling and as an evaluation rule)
For (d):
- This paper undercuts both sides of the set-point debate. Against Sterling, anticipation and movable references are already part of contemporary homeostasis, so they cannot be what makes allostasis new. Against classical homeostasis, the physiology shows no central comparator or set point at all, only a balance point of independent threshold loops.
- For the project:
  - Do not claim "allostasis" because the regulator anticipates or moves its reference. Those are homeostatic by this account.
  - Any claim of an allostatic or pathological state should be tested on effector activity, using the three signatures: sign reversal under a sustained disturbance, persistence after the disturbance ends, and concurrent opposing effectors. The level of the regulated variable alone is not evidence.
- Tension with francis-wonham-1976-internal-model-principle.md: the IMP's linear form assumes an explicit error signal and controller, which Ramsay & Woods say physiology lacks. Sontag's nonlinear IMP needs no plant/controller split, so it is the version that fits a balance-point body.
- The robustness-fragility trade-off they cite (Csete & Doyle) is the same conservation idea as the Bode integral (stein-2003-respect-the-unstable.md). The Csete & Doyle source was not read.
