# Meltzoff, Murray, Simpson, Heimann, Nagy, Nadel, Pedersen, Brooks, Messinger, De Pascalis, Subiaul, Paukner & Ferrari 2018, "Re-examination of Oostenbroek et al. (2016): evidence for neonatal imitation of tongue protrusion" (Developmental Science 21(4):e12609, doi:10.1111/desc.12609, PMC6710010)

Source read: the full text of the publisher-typeset accepted article (8 pages, from the NSF Public Access Repository), including Figure 2, the endnotes and the recommendations. Not read: Oostenbroek et al.'s 2018 reply ("Re-evaluating the neonatal imitation hypothesis", Developmental Science), and the authors' further response (PMC6659722). This note covers one side of a live dispute. See oostenbroek-2016-neonatal-imitation.md (abstract only) and jones-2009-development-of-imitation.md.

## Claims
- Oostenbroek et al.'s design had "11 flaws ... that biased the results toward null effects":
  1. 11 models within subjects in one ~11-minute session (prior positive studies used 1-4).
  2. Some modelled acts are impossible for neonates (the vowel "ee", a tongue click).
  3. Stimulus periods of at most 30 s, some 15 s. A review of 23 studies found modelling of 60 s or more "yielded positive evidence for imitation in all studies", 40 s or less "in only 31% of studies" (Anisfeld 1991). Response periods were 15-60 s against Meltzoff & Moore's 2.5-4 min.
  4. Response criteria deviated (e.g., responses after a >2-s look-away were not counted; mouth-opening duration not scored).
  5. The experimenter's face behind the hand during finger models.
  6. Some infants tested while drowsy (Brazelton state 3).
  7. Uncontrolled exposure to the experimenter (walking and rocking the infant between trials).
  8. Post-hoc selection: 64 of 106 in the longitudinal analysis; "Only 25 of 106 infants actually completed the pre-specified longitudinal design (11 models x 4 ages)".
  9. Trial length decided in situ; partial trials averaged per 15 s.
  10. Order not counterbalanced; tongue protrusion and mouth opening always adjacent.
  11. Neonates balanced on the experimenter's lap with poor postural support.
- Re-analysis of the authors' raw data: compare tongue protrusion (TP) to the TP model against the mean TP across the 10 control models.
  - Cross-sectional, paired t tests: 1 week t(74) = 2.75, p = .008, d = .32; 3 weeks t(80) = 2.16, p = .034, d = 0.24; 6 weeks t(84) = 2.78, p = .007, d = .30; 9 weeks t(88) = 3.79, p = .0003, d = .40. Also significant with GLMM.
  - In Oostenbroek et al.'s own Table S4, 39 of 40 pairwise TP-vs-control comparisons go in the predicted direction; at 9 weeks, 9 of 10 are significant (p < .001 to .004).
  - Longitudinal (same n = 64), contrast TP vs mean of controls: beta = .45, SE = .09, p < .0001.
  - Complete-data subset (n = 25): effect of model F(10, 240) = 5.74, p < .0001, partial eta^2 = .19; TP to TP model M = 0.75 (SD 0.52) vs pooled controls M = 0.41 (SD 0.28) per 15 s, F(1, 24) = 15.62, p = .0006, partial eta^2 = .39.
- Against the arousal account: the 10 controls were all "arousing dynamic stimuli", including an object protruding from a tube (mimicking tongue protrusion) and a hinged box opening and closing, so more TP to the TP model than to their mean "contradicts the arousal account" (Oostenbroek et al. acknowledge this in their supplement, per the authors).
- Mother-infant contingencies "can interfere with a strictly imitative response"; the experimenter should look unlike the primary caretaker; parents should be kept blind to the gestures to avoid practice ("The effects of parental training and contingent responding are interesting to investigate in their own right; Murray et al., 2016").
- Five recommendations: few models (or independent groups), 1.5-4 min response periods, a homogeneous backdrop and a spotlit face, control of social exposure to the tester, piloting.

## Evidence
The re-analysis numbers above. The effect that survives is tongue protrusion only; the paper makes no claim about mouth opening, happy/sad faces or finger movements in these data (endnote 6 says the design flaws "may have undermined" them). The effect size is small to moderate: d = .24 to .40 at each age, roughly 0.75 vs 0.41 TP per 15 s in the complete subset.

## What it implies for the engine
- Even on the most favourable reading, what survives is one orofacial act (tongue protrusion) matched above a range of arousing controls, with small effects, and parental practice and contingent responding are flagged as confounds that could create matching. Nothing here shows innate matching of emotional expressions.
- The two readings give different design consequences, and the engine should take the one that does not depend on resolving the dispute: a minimal innate orofacial response to a protruding/approaching mouth-region stimulus may be allowed as a reflex (it exists in both readings; they disagree on whether it is selective to the matching model), but any general visual-to-motor correspondence for facial expressions must be learned.
- The paper's own methodological point is useful for evaluation: matching tests need controls that are equally arousing and equally similar in low-level motion (the tube and box controls). An engine claimed to "mimic" should be tested against a partner's control movements matched for motion energy and location, not just against a still face.
- "Mother and her familiar facial games" interfere with imitation of a stranger: early matching is shaped by the dyad's history. That fits an engine whose mimicry is operator-specific.
