# Matsumoto & Lee 1993, "Consciousness, volition, and the neuropsychology of facial expressions of emotion" (Consciousness and Cognition 2:237-254), read as the accessible stand-in for Rinn 1984, "The neuropsychology of facial expression: a review of the neurological and psychological mechanisms for producing facial expressions" (Psychological Bulletin 95(1):52-77)

Source read: Matsumoto & Lee 1993, scanned PDF from the first author's website, pp. 237-248 read in full through OCR (some words garbled; quotes below were checked for sense). The remaining pages (facial feedback and the ANS-posing studies, conclusions) were skimmed. Rinn 1984 was NOT read: no free copy was found (APA PsycNet is paywalled). Everything attributed to Rinn here is Matsumoto & Lee's account of Rinn, so it is secondary. Read Rinn in the original before citing his specifics.

## Claims
- Anatomy (pp. 238-240): the face has "over 40 anatomically separate muscles", most attached to bone on one side and skin or fascia on the other; most "do not move bone and ligaments", and Rinn suggested they are "specialized for communication and expression". Jaw muscles (trigeminal, CN V) and mimetic muscles (facial nerve, CN VII) "use different neural tracts" (Rinn). Some muscles act only in combination; one anatomical muscle can produce several functional actions (inner vs outer frontalis), so measurement should use functional units (FACS AUs), each with "different degrees of intensity or laterality and with different timing characteristics (i.e., onset, apex, and offset)".
- The facial nucleus receives pyramidal (corticobulbar) and extrapyramidal input, direct fibres from the contralateral red nucleus and limbic connections. Basal ganglia act on the face "via the premotor and motor areas of the cortex, rather than by descending projections to the brain stem"; limbic and extrapyramidal systems "include large areas of the neocortex, and are not necessarily subcortical" (p. 240).
- Rinn's two-route thesis, as reported: "Voluntary facial expressions are thought to emanate from the cortical motor strip and course to the facial nucleus through the pyramidal tract ...: impulses for involuntary facial expressions result from innervations along the extrapyramidal tract (Rinn, 1984)" (p. 240). Rinn's four lines of evidence (p. 240):
  1. Motor strip or corticobulbar lesions: patients "cannot contract the facial muscles on command, but can produce spontaneous expressions".
  2. Extrapyramidal (basal ganglia) lesions: patients "can move their facial muscles on command, but lose all spontaneous movements".
  3. Facial nerve anastomosis (accessory nerve spliced onto the facial nerve): volitional control returns, but "spontaneous emotional expressions will not recover on the affected side".
  4. Pseudobulbar palsy (corticobulbar lesions): involuntary laughing or weeping "virtually indistinguishable" from normal, but with "no emotional experience, or an incompatible experience".
- Laterality: left-face superiority is found mainly in posed or conversational (partly voluntary) expressions; spontaneous expressions recorded unobserved are "nearly symmetrical", with asymmetries equally often on either side (Ekman et al. 1981). Rinn proposed that apparent right-hemisphere dominance is really left-hemisphere superiority in inhibiting emotion, via language (pp. 241-242).
- Corticobulbar input is contralateral to the lower face, "with increasing bilateral influence to the mid- and upper-face areas", so posed asymmetry should differ by face region (p. 243).
- The authors' main argument: the voluntary-involuntary dichotomy "is not adequate ... and may actually be misleading" because of learned display rules that become automatic. They propose three dimensions: degree of display-rule modification; degree of learning and rehearsal (unlearned to "totally learned and automatic"); degree of conscious, volitional effort (p. 245). Display rules "are learned early in life and are functional by mid- to late-childhood"; the gain in regulating expression in mid-to-late childhood "appears to be related to the completion of frontal lobe development" (p. 246). Hence "the cortical (i.e., pyramidal) motor system frequently works with subcortical systems".
- Release of inhibition: emotional smiles were "more exaggerated on the paralyzed side" after motor-strip lesions (Brodal 1981), "suggestive of release of inhibition by the ever-present influence of corticobulbar connections" (p. 247).
- Innate evidence they cite: "infants born with no brain structures higher than the midbrain show some normal facial expressions, such as crying and disgust (Guyton, 1976; Steiner, 1973)", and "Congenitally blind children display a full range of spontaneous facial expressions of emotion, which cannot be explained by visual learning or imitation" (p. 247).
- Smiles: genuine felt happiness recruits zygomatic major plus orbicularis oculi, other smiles usually zygomatic major only (Ekman & Friesen 1982; Ekman, Davidson & Friesen 1990). A social smile without the eye component may come from lack of orbicularis drive, its cortical suppression, or both (p. 248).
- Facial feedback: the face "may not be the endpoint in the emotion process"; feedback from facial muscles may regulate subjective experience (Tomkins) (p. 248).

## Evidence
A review; no new data. The cited lesion and anastomosis findings are clinical observations; no counts or effect sizes are given in what I read.

## What it implies for the engine
- The two-route structure is classical and rests on double dissociations (lesion 1 vs lesion 2), which is exactly the ablation style Principle 5 wants. The engine's face should have a deliberate route and an affective route converging on the same muscle layer, with predictions: cut the deliberate route and posed/requested expressions fail while spontaneous ones survive; cut the affective route and the reverse.
- The routes are not independent in adults: the cortical route continuously inhibits and reshapes the affective one (display rules, release of inhibition after lesions). For the engine, the conceptual layer (Claude) or a learned regulation policy should act on the face mainly by modulating and masking the affective route's output, not only by writing its own expressions. Masking leaves a signature (zygomatic without orbicularis), which the engine's face should be able to produce and its perception should be able to read.
- Display rules are learned and become automatic over years. That is learning-by-living in the form the brief wants: a regulation layer that starts empty and is shaped by social contingencies, while the affective route's patterns exist from the start.
- Innate floor: anencephalic infants cry and show disgust; congenitally blind children show the spontaneous repertoire. So some expression patterns need neither cortex nor visual learning. That supports giving the engine's body innate brainstem-level expressive reflexes (cry, disgust/gape) rather than learning them from seen faces.
- Regional laterality (bilateral upper face, contralateral lower face) matches Gothard's anatomy and gives the engine's muscle layer a structural prior.
