# Ray & Heyes 2011, "Imitation in infancy: the wealth of the stimulus" (Developmental Science 14(1):92-105, doi:10.1111/j.1467-7687.2010.00961.x)

Source read: the full text of the published PDF (pp. 92-102) from the second author's Oxford page, including Table 1 and Figure 1. The online Supporting Material (the gesture-by-gesture review of 37 experiments) and the commentaries published with this target article were NOT read. This is the clearest statement of the associative sequence learning (ASL) model of imitation; Heyes 2016 ("Homo imitans? Seven reasons why imitation couldn't possibly be associative", Phil Trans B) was not read.

## Claims
- The correspondence problem: imitation needs "a neurocognitive mechanism that relates the seen and unfelt to the felt and unseen". Facial gestures are "perceptually opaque": when performed they are felt, not seen, so imitation cannot be guided by comparing how the two look.
- Poverty-of-the-stimulus view (Meltzoff & Moore's AIM): an "innate common code" detects equivalences between observed and executed acts. AIM predicts range, specificity, developmental continuity, intentionality (successive responses converge on the target) and a human-only distribution. None holds:
  - Range: 37 experiments on 18 gestures. Half the gestures (9) never produced a positive report; five more (mouth opening, hand opening/closing, lip protrusion, sequential finger movement, blinking) have more negative than positive reports ("twice as many failures to find imitation of mouth opening as successful attempts"). Only lateral head movement, facial expressions of emotion and tongue protrusion have more positive than negative reports. Head movement is attributed to perceptual tethering; "the facial expression effect, when present, may be an artefact associated with the method used to score infant behaviour". Tongue protrusion: "leave no doubt that when newborn infants see tongue protrusion they are likely to protrude their own tongues" (Table 1: 21 positive, 9 negative experiments, as I read the table).
  - Specificity: tongue protrusion responds to non-topographic features (Jacobson 1979: pen or ball approaching the mouth; Jones 1996, 2006: lights, music). Jacobson's alternative is an innate releasing mechanism (the stimulus resembles an approaching nipple, eliciting feeding responses).
  - Continuity: matched gestures "drop-out" at about 3 months and return around 6 months, and the decline tracks declining spontaneous rates (Maratos 1982).
  - Intentionality: claimed improvement over trials (Nagy et al. 2005; Meltzoff & Moore 1994 sideways tongue) is confounded with response vigour and perceptual learning.
  - Phylogeny: birds imitate; newborn rhesus match only tongue protrusion and lip smacking, only on day 3 (Ferrari et al. 2006). So any homologous newborn mechanism is a releasing or arousal mechanism, not a supramodal matcher.
- ASL model: to imitate action x, one must have "seen x and performed x close together in time (contiguity)" and contingently, meaning P(seeing x | doing x) exceeds P(seeing any other single action | doing x). Associative learning then forms a "matching vertical association", "a bidirectional excitatory link between a sensory (typically visual) representation of x and a motor representation of x". Crucially, ASL has no built-in bias toward matching: experiencing see-x with do-y forms "a non-matching vertical association", giving counter-imitation. "If people are more inclined to imitate than to counter-imitate, this is because our developmental environments have exposed us to more matching ... than non-matching ... sensorimotor relationships."
- Adult evidence that the mapping is retrainable: brief incompatible training (open hand to closing stimulus) abolished automatic imitation (Heyes et al. 2005); in little vs index finger abduction it reversed automatic imitation and TMS motor-evoked potentials (Catmur et al. 2007: after training, watching index-finger movement gave larger MEPs in the little-finger muscle); fMRI hand-vs-foot mirror responses reversed after crossed training (Catmur et al. 2008). Expertise (ballet) increases mirror responses; motor or sensorimotor experience matters more than visual experience alone.
- Five sources of "imitogenic" experience: direct self-observation (only for transparent actions; neonates keep their hands in view), mirror self-observation (85% of 6-12-month-olds treat the mirror image as a playmate, Amsterdam 1972), synchronous action (both caregiver and infant opened their mouths in 55% of spoon-feeding sequences, O'Toole & Dubin 1968), acquired equivalence (a word or sound paired at different times with seeing and doing; actions with sounds are imitated earlier), and being imitated.
- Being imitated is the richest source: infants spend much waking time face to face, "65% of this time is spent actively interacting"; an imitation episode occurs "roughly once a minute" in mother-infant face-to-face play, and "79%" involve the mother imitating the infant (Pawlby 1977); mothers are "roughly five times as likely to imitate their infants as the other way around", still at 11-12 months (Uzgiris et al. 1989). Mothers reward matches and mark their imitations with exaggerated "motionese"; salience speeds associative learning (Rescorla-Wagner). Infants "do not need to detect or recognize that they are being imitated" for this to work.
- Developmental predictions confirmed: imitation frequency rises linearly from 7.5 months and doubles by 12 months; transparent gestures (waving bye-bye) imitated up to 6 months earlier than opaque ones (tongue protrusion) despite being motorically harder (Jones 2007); among opaque actions, those with sounds or that adults often imitate (mouth opening with a pop at ~6 months, lip smacking ~8 months) come long before ear touching (a year later).
- Individual differences: at 2 years, imitation variance in twins was 42% shared environment, 28% unique environment, 30% genetic (McEwen et al. 2007). Most imitative mothers have the most imitative infants by 12 months (Masur 1987). Infants with physical disabilities whose parents "almost never imitated" them showed imitation deficits at 19 months for movements within their capability (Cress et al. 1998). Infants of high-anger mothers, who imitated them less, imitated less.
- For artificial agents: robot work has focused on self-observation, "whereas our review suggests that imitation by adults is an especially rich source of imitogenic experience in human infancy".

## Evidence
Review with a large table of neonatal experiments, plus the adult training studies above. Numbers above are as reported by Ray & Heyes.

## What it implies for the engine
- This is the mechanism the brief's "mimicry and reading of others learned from contingencies during living" needs, stated precisely: a see-x/do-x link forms when the engine's own motor act x is contiguous and contingent with seeing x. No innate similarity metric is required, and the same machinery will learn counter-mimicry if the environment pairs differently. That gives the engine a falsifiable ablation: train the engine in a world where the partner's displays are crossed with its own, and its mimicry should reverse.
- For the face (perceptually opaque), the engine needs a source of see-x/do-x pairs. Candidates, in ASL's order of importance for infants: being imitated by the partner; a mirror (the engine seeing its own rendered face while proprioceiving its muscle commands, available for free in software); synchronous reactions to shared events (both startle to the same sound); acquired equivalence through words (Claude naming "frown" both when the operator frowns and when the engine does). The mirror source is the cheapest and most controllable for an artificial agent; but ASL predicts it learns only self-appearance, and the operator-specific mapping requires the operator's face.
- A real constraint: an adult operator will not imitate the engine once a minute with exaggerated marking. Plan for sparse being-imitated data; lean on self-observation and shared-event synchrony; and measure the contingency P(see x | do x) actually available in the operator's sessions before expecting mimicry to form.
- Contingency is defined as a conditional probability over action-observation pairs. This matches the sufficiency and necessity indices in gergely-watson-1996-social-biofeedback.md and can be computed over AU streams.
- Innate floor that ASL allows: releasing mechanisms (tongue protrusion to an approaching object near the mouth) and arousal-driven oral exploration. Emotional-expression "matching" in neonates is judged a scoring artefact here; this disagrees with Jones's suggestion that emotional displays are reflexively matched.
