# Van Kleef 2009, "How Emotions Regulate Social Life: The Emotions as Social Information (EASI) Model" (Current Directions in Psychological Science 18(3):184-188, doi 10.1111/j.1467-8721.2009.01633.x)

Source read: the full published article (UvA-DARE copy, pure.uva.nl file 75196_309689.pdf), all sections and both figures. It is a five-page theory paper. The studies it cites are described here as Van Kleef describes them; the 2004a negotiation numbers below come from the primary report (see van-kleef-2010-easi-social-decision-making.md, where I read the thesis version of Van Kleef, De Dreu & Manstead 2004a).

## Claims
- An observer's behaviour toward someone who shows an emotion is driven by two distinct, mutually influencing routes (Fig. 1):
  - **Inferential processes.** The expression tells the observer something about the expresser's appraisal of the situation (anger = goal blocked and someone else to blame; happiness = things going well; sadness = irrevocable loss, low coping potential). The observer draws a conclusion about the situation or the expresser's intentions and acts on that conclusion.
  - **Affective reactions.** Two kinds: (a) emotional contagion via "mirror-neuron activity, mimicry, and afferent feedback"; (b) impressions and liking of the expresser.
- The two routes can converge (a loved one's distress signals need for help and elicits compassion; both lead to helping), compete (an angry negotiation opponent makes me angry, which pushes me to compete, while the inference "their limit is reached" pushes me to concede), or feed each other (inferring disapproval makes me angry).
- Which route predicts behaviour is set by two classes of moderators:
  - **The observer's information processing.** "The more thorough the information processing, the stronger the predictive power of inferences; the shallower the information processing, the stronger the predictive power of affective reactions." Proxies are need for cognitive closure, time pressure and power (low power raises processing motivation).
  - **Social-relational factors.** The relationship, display rules, and whether the emotion is aimed at the person or at the situation. These act mainly through the perceived appropriateness of the expression. Inappropriate displays produce stronger affective reactions.
- The model is about discrete emotions, not valence: "each discrete emotion conveys specific information."
- Open questions the author names: close relationships, noisy settings, ambiguous or mixed expressions. He also predicts that observers "should respond similarly to emotional expressions regardless of whether they perceive them accurately", and calls this untested.

## Evidence
No new data. The cited evidence, as the paper reports it:
- Social referencing: infants cross a visual cliff more often when the mother smiles than when she looks fearful (Klinnert et al. 1983).
- Computer-mediated negotiation with text emotions (Van Kleef, De Dreu & Manstead 2004a): angry opponent → larger concessions; happy opponent → smaller concessions; the effect is mediated by the inferred opponent limit. In the primary report (Exp. 2.1, N = 118), mean demand was 490 with an angry opponent, 540 with a non-emotional one and 553 with a happy one, F(2,115) = 5.66. In the same participants, contagion of feeling also happened: those facing anger reported more anger, F(2,115) = 8.01. Their behaviour still followed the inference, not their own feeling.
- Guilt or regret from an opponent → higher own demands; disappointment or worry → lower demands (Van Kleef et al. 2006).
- Leader anger vs happiness, delivered by a trained actor on video with identical words, to four-person teams (Van Kleef et al., then in press, 2009). Teams high in information-processing motivation performed better under an angry leader. Teams low in it performed better under a happy leader. Fig. 2 shows the crossover; the y-axis spans roughly 20,000–30,000 points, and the paper gives no means.
- Anger directed at the offer → concessions (mediated by the limit inference). Anger directed at the person → less conceding (Steinel, Van Kleef & Harinck 2008).
- Anger that breaks a display rule → retaliation instead of concession, especially for high-power targets (Van Kleef & Côté 2007).

## Formal model
None; the model is verbal only. Fig. 1 is a box-and-arrow diagram: the expression feeds two mediators (inferences, affective reactions), both feed behaviour, and two moderator boxes point at both paths. The paper states no functional form, weights or equations.

## What it implies for the design
- **The two routes map onto the project's two options.** "Affective reactions / contagion" is the transfer route every one of the 15 designs used. "Inferential processes" is the evidence route under examination: the partner's expression updates the agent's model of the shared situation and of the partner's appraisal, and the agent's own response follows from its own concerns. EASI says humans do both. It also says the inferential route can drive the opposite act to the one contagion would drive (concede to anger, exploit happiness).
- **The two routes can run at once on different outputs.** In 2004a, felt anger converged with the partner's (contagion), while the act diverged (conceding). So "contagion vs inference" need not be one mixture weight on one output. A design can let a bounded reciprocal component act on the felt/arousal channel while the act and the face are computed from the inferred situation. The paper does not measure faces, so whether the face follows the feeling or the inference is open. Seibt 2015 and Carr 2014 (mimicry-context-synthesis.md) suggest that the face carries both, at different latencies.
- **A hidden dial in EASI itself.** If EASI is implemented literally as behaviour = (1 − w)·Affective(x) + w·Inference(x, situation), with w set by "information-processing motivation" and "appropriateness", then w is exactly the flat-to-copy-style dial the critiques found. At w → 0 the agent reverts to contagion (a copy, if contagion is implemented as transfer). At w → 1 it is purely inferential. In EASI as written, both moderators set this one relative weight. The design should not inherit the mixture. The way out is to compute the reciprocal component *inside* the inference (see the 2010 note: convergent feeling is what the inference produces when the agent's concerns are aligned with the partner's), not as a separate route with its own gain.
- **Appropriateness as prediction error.** "Appropriateness" can be read without labels as: how well the partner's expression is predicted by the agent's own model of the situation. An expression that the agent's situation model explains is used as evidence about the situation (concede to anger about the offer). One the model cannot explain (anger aimed at me, or against a display rule) shifts the update to the partner model itself (this partner is hostile), which flips the act. This interpretation is mine, not the paper's.
- **Labels.** EASI is written entirely in discrete emotion labels, which Principles 1–2 forbid below Claude. What the agent can use without labels is the *informational role* each expression plays: what it predicts about the partner's next behaviour toward the agent and about the shared outcome. That can be learned from contingencies (partner's face → partner's later act → agent's own need change). The open question is whether a few hours of one partner's data suffice to learn it.

## Verdict: adopt (as the frame for "two routes, moderated")
This is the shortest statement of the contagion-versus-inference split, with the claim that the inferential route can reverse the act. Its moderators are verbal and should not be implemented as one route-mixing weight, because that weight is a flat-to-copy dial. Use the primary negotiation studies (2010 note) for numbers.
