# Camras & Shutter 2010, "Emotional facial expressions in infancy" (Emotion Review 2(2):120-129, doi:10.1177/1754073909352529)

Source read: the full text of the published PDF (pp. 120-128) from a course-readings page at the University of Miami; the reference list was not read. It is a review; the primary studies it summarises (Oster, Hegley & Nagel 1992; Camras 1992; Camras et al. 1993, 2007; Bennett, Bendersky & Lewis 2002, 2005; Hiatt, Campos & Emde 1979) were NOT read in the original. This note stands in for both Camras and Oster on the question of whether infant expressions match adult prototypes; Oster's own Baby FACS manual and chapters were not available.

## Claims
- Target: differential emotions theory's (DET; Izard) "principle of innate concordance", that infants have discrete basic emotions each indexed by a pre-specified facial expression identical to the adult one, coded with Izard's MAX system. Conclusion: "current evidence fails to support its proposal regarding a set of pre-specified facial expressions that invariably reflect a corresponding set of discrete emotions in infants."
- Morphology does not match adult prototypes for negative emotions. Oster et al. 1992 (Study 3) recoded MAX training photographs (infants 2 weeks to 21 months) with Baby FACS and submitted them to FACSAID: all happiness slides and 2 of 3 surprise slides matched, but "only three out of 19 negative expressions were identically interpreted by both MAX and FACSAID"; "none of the four MAX-specified anger slides" read as anger. Comparing formulas: MAX anger includes orbicularis oculi (AU6), absent from every FACS anger formula, and lacks lip tightening/press (AU23/24), present in every FACS anger formula; MAX disgust includes AU6 and tongue showing (AU19), absent from FACS disgust; only one of four MAX sadness formulas matches a FACS prototype.
- Judges do not read infant negative expressions as discrete emotions. Izard et al. 1980: forced-choice accuracy above 60% only for joy, surprise and sadness (chance 12.5%). Oster et al. 1992: over 70% agreement for five of six joy/surprise/interest slides, but the predicted emotion was chosen by over 60% for only one negative slide; on rating scales the predicted emotion was highest for 2 of 17 negative stimuli, and "distress" was rated higher than the predicted emotion for all four anger pictures. Camras et al. 1993: with face, voice and body visible (4-9-week-olds), distress was rated highest regardless of whether the face was MAX pain, anger or sadness.
- Expressions occur without the emotion and fail to occur with it:
  - Neonatal smiles in REM sleep; "surprise" faces at 4 weeks to excitement at familiar stimuli and when bringing an object to the mouth (confirmed at 5-7 months); a brow-raise "interest" variant depends on looking up rather than down (Michel et al. 1992); "pain" faces when pleasant swinging stops or in face-to-face play.
  - Surprise and fear faces are rarely seen on the visual cliff or in expectancy violation at 10-12 months, while pain faces sometimes are.
  - During crying at 4-9 weeks, MAX pain, anger and sadness faces cycle "as the intensity of her crying waxed and waned", tracking distress intensity (Camras 1992). Oster proposes some "sadness" faces are attempts to regulate crying.
- Situational specificity (Hiatt et al. 1979 criteria: inter- and intra-situational) is met only for happiness at 4 months, and happiness and disgust at 12 months. Bennett et al. 2002 (4 months): sour taste produced mainly sadness faces; more babies showed anger than fear faces in both the anger and fear situations. Camras et al. 2007 (11-month-old European American, Japanese and Chinese infants; arm restraint vs a growling toy gorilla head; Baby FACS): non-facial behaviour confirmed anger vs fear, but neither specificity criterion was met; anger-related patterns dominated both, and the rank orders of expressions in the two situations correlated r = .50. "Although the babies clearly experienced anger and fear (as indicated by their nonfacial behaviors), they produced similar facial responses."
- Contingency loss: 4-6-month-olds who controlled an audiovisual stimulus by arm movement showed more positive and less negative expression than yoked infants; after contingency loss, "anger" faces rose; anger faces went with raised heart rate and "sadness" faces with cortisol at 4 months (Lewis et al. 2006).
- Alternative: Bridges' differentiation (diffuse excitement -> delight and distress -> discrete emotions) plus dynamic systems. The MAX "surprise" face may first mean intense excitement and the "anger" face moderate general distress, later becoming tied to discrete emotions, possibly with small morphological changes toward adult forms; this has "not yet occurred by 12 months for surprise, anger, and fear", while disgust may be specific by 12 months. "The development of emotions themselves can be independent of the development of their corresponding prototypic facial expressions." Socialising adults may act as "control parameters" that reorganise expression-emotion links; even in adults, expressions "may never come to be associated with emotion states in a unique one-to-one invariant relationship".

## Evidence
Numbers above as reported. The strongest primary evidence is Oster et al. 1992 (FACS-level morphology and judgment) and Camras et al. 2007 (three cultures, behavioural validation of the elicited emotions, r = .50 between situations).

## What it implies for the engine
- The innate expressive repertoire is coarse: a smile (positive), a cry-face (distress, with intensity carried by eye constriction and mouth opening), a few reflexive orofacial patterns (disgust/gape to sour or bitter). Adult-prototype "anger", "fear" and "surprise" faces are not innate in their adult form and not specific in infancy. An engine whose innate face has six basic-emotion presets contradicts the infant evidence (and Principle 2); see mimo.md's rejection for the same reason.
- Innate infant configurations are driven partly by non-emotional factors (gaze direction raises the brows, oral exploration opens the mouth, crying intensity cycles the brow and mouth). The engine's face should be driven by such body and attention variables, not only by affect.
- Differentiation is learned and socially shaped, with emotion-specific behaviour preceding emotion-specific faces. An engine that learns by living should show the same order: distinct coping behaviour first, distinct faces later, as its face-state mapping is shaped by the operator's responses. This is a testable developmental prediction for S6.
- For reading others: adult observers read infant negative faces as "distress" unless context is given. Likewise, the engine's reading of a face should be context-dependent and graded, not a lookup.
- Contingency loss produces a distinct negative pattern with its own physiology (anger with heart rate, sadness with cortisol at 4 months). This is a body-level signature that the engine's homeostatic layer could reproduce when a controllable contingency is lost.
