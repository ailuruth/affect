## Claims (abstract only)
- "In natural behaviour, animals actively interrogate their environments using endogenously generated 'question-and-answer' strategies. However, in laboratory settings participants typically engage with externally imposed stimuli and tasks, and the mechanisms of active sampling remain poorly understood."
- "We distinguish between information sampling, in which organisms reduce uncertainty relevant to a familiar task, and information search, in which they investigate in an open-ended fashion to discover new tasks."
- "We review evidence that both sampling and search depend on individual preferences over cognitive states, including attitudes towards uncertainty, learning progress and types of information."
- "We propose that, although these preferences are non-instrumental and can on occasion interfere with external goals, they are important heuristics that allow organisms to cope with the high complexity of both sampling and search, and generate curiosity-driven investigations in large, open environments in which rewards are sparse and ex ante unknown."

The highlighted references carry one-line author annotations. These are the authors' descriptions of cited work, not results we have checked:
- Foley et al. 2017 (PNAS): "oculomotor neurons encode expected information gains in monkeys".
- Blanchard, Hayden & Bromberg-Martin 2015 (Neuron): "single-neuron encoding of non-instrumental information value in the monkey orbitofrontal cortex".
- Baranes, Oudeyer & Gottlieb 2014 (Front. Neurosci. 8:317): "a novel laboratory task for examining intrinsically motivated exploration based on difficulty in humans".
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014 (Neuron): "the effects of curiosity on memory and the hippocampus in humans".
- van Lieshout et al. 2018 (J. Neurosci.): "non-instrumental information value and its neural correlates in humans".
- Also cited without annotation: Bromberg-Martin & Hikosaka 2009 (dopamine neurons and advance information), Daddaoua, Lopes & Gottlieb 2016 (monkey oculomotor exploration "guided by uncertainty reduction"), Kidd & Hayden 2015, and Oudeyer, Kaplan & Hafner 2007.

From the parts we could read, we cannot tell whether the review reports any human or animal study in which subjects prefer learnable over unlearnable stimuli, or in which behaviour tracks learning progress rather than uncertainty or information gain. The abstract lists learning progress as one of several "preferences over cognitive states". Most of the annotated neural evidence is about information gain or the value of non-instrumental information, not learning progress.

## Verdict: watch (needs the full text)
- This is the source most likely to answer question (c), but we have not read it. Before citing it for "biological curiosity tracks learning progress", get the full text through institutional access. The most direct primary candidate for learnable-vs-unlearnable preference in humans appears to be Baranes, Oudeyer & Gottlieb 2014 (Front. Neurosci., open access), going by its annotation. The open-access precursor review Gottlieb, Oudeyer, Lopes & Baranes 2013 (Trends Cogn. Sci. 17:585-593; PMC4193662) is another route. Neither was read here.
- Nothing read here treats learning progress as satiable or set-point regulated. The abstract's word is "preferences", and they are "non-instrumental".
