# Betley, Xu, Cao, Gong, Magnus, Yu & Sternson 2015, "Neurons for hunger and thirst transmit a negative-valence teaching signal" (Nature 521:180-185)

Source read: the NIH author manuscript in PMC (PMC4567040), fetched as full-text XML on 2026-11-24 and read in full: abstract, introduction, all Results sections, Discussion, Methods, the five main figure legends and the Extended Data figure legends. Not read: Extended Data Table 1 (the statistics table; its contents were not in the XML text I extracted), the supplementary videos, and the figures themselves. Most effects are reported in the text only as significance in figures. The few numbers quoted below are all that the text and legends give.

## Claims
- AgRP neuron activity is aversive, meaning it has negative valence: "Mice avoided activation of AGRP neurons, indicating that AGRP neuron activity has negative valence."
- Reducing that activity is a teaching signal: "AGRP neuron inhibition conditioned preference for flavours and places." The general claim: "these need-sensing neurons condition preference for environmental cues associated with nutrient or water ingestion, which is learned through reduction of negative-valence signals during restoration of homeostasis."
- The valence is mild and Pavlovian rather than instrumental. AgRP activation does "not appear to elicit strong aversion or disgust". The signal "can mediate Pavlovian learning, but this property does not readily extend to instrumental conditioning."
- AgRP neurons "predictively encode the receipt of nutritive food by rapidly reducing activity, and this process involves learning". But "sustained reduction of AGRP neuron activity requires nutrient ingestion".
- The authors set this against the opposite valence of lateral hypothalamic stimulation. They conclude that "homeostatic need states regulate behaviour through a combination of negative and positive valence signals", operating "in a concerted push-pull manner".
- A second need system shows the same property. Activating thirst neurons (SFO Nos1) is also avoided, demonstrating "a negative-valence signal that can condition learning from a second homeostatic neuronal cell type".

## Evidence
Optogenetic activation (ChR2) and chemogenetic silencing (PSAM-GlyR) of AgRP neurons, plus deep-brain single-cell calcium imaging through a GRIN lens.

- Flavour conditioning: a flavour paired with AgRP photostimulation in fed mice became less preferred ("EGFP, n=6; ChR2, n=8"). A flavour paired with AgRP silencing in food-restricted mice ("85-90% body weight") became more preferred. The shift in preference "correlated with reduction of chow re-feeding by AGRP neuron silencing" ("n=29 mice").
- Place conditioning by silencing worked in food-restricted mice ("n=20") but not in fed ones ("n=9"), so it depends on the need state. Passive pairing with AgRP activation did not shift place preference ("ΔOccupancy time: 28.4 ± 72 s; p=0.70 paired t-test, n=10"). A closed-loop protocol, with stimulation whenever the mouse entered the paired side, did produce avoidance (second half of each session: "group: F(1,154)=6.4, p=0.019"), and in extinction mice preferred "the side that had been associated with cessation of AGRP neuron photostimulation".
- Instrumental tests failed. Attempts to train lever-press or nose-poke "to either shut off AGRP neuron photostimulation in well-fed mice or to optogenetically silence AGRP neurons in food-restricted mice were unsuccessful".
- Lever pressing for food under AgRP stimulation (PR7, "n=11" per group) declined over 15 sessions in fed mice, because "nutrient ingestion would not be capable of reducing exogenously elevated AGRP neuron activity". The authors read this as "reduced value of nutritive food when AGRP neuron activity remains elevated".
- Single-cell imaging: food restriction raised baseline activity "in 54/61 AGRP neurons". Chow delivery reduced activity "in 106/110 neurons (96%, 4 mice)". Activity fell "prior to food consumption". A visible but inaccessible pellet reduced activity "nearly to the same level as during a subsequent food consumption trial". A false-food object "only transiently reduced AGRP neuron activity, which rapidly recovered after the mouse contacted the object". When food was removed after "less than 50 mg had been consumed", activity rose gradually back toward, but "slightly below", baseline.
- Learned cue: a "200 ms auditory and visual compound CS" initially caused "a slight increase". After pairing with food it "elicited reduction of AGRP neuron activity (prior to spout extension, p < 0.001 ...), and food consumption had little additional effect".
- Ghrelin raised activity in "81% of AGRP neurons", with return to baseline at "population t1/2: 19 min, individual neuron t1/2 range: 5-46 min".
- Thirst neurons: SFO Nos1 stimulation increased drinking with "latency, 20 Hz: 3.8 ± 0.5 minutes, n=8", and the paired side was avoided ("group: F(1,112)=26.2, p<0.001").

## Verdict: adopt (as evidence that a need signal has negative valence and that its reduction teaches)
- For (a), the costate as the broadcast affective signal: this is direct causal evidence that a need-tracking signal is aversive in itself, and that its decrease reinforces whatever cue or place preceded the decrease. That is the sign convention we want: a high shadow price of a need is felt as bad, and a fall in it is felt as relief and teaches. Two limits from the paper should carry into the design. The valence is weak ("not a strongly aversive stimulus, such as a shock"). It also conditioned Pavlovian preferences but did not support learning an action to turn it off. So treat it as an affective tone and a Pavlovian teacher, not as the sole reward for instrumental learning.
- For (b), anticipation: single-cell data confirm Chen et al. 2015 (chen-2015-sensory-detection-arcuate.md) at 96% of neurons. They add the key fact that the anticipatory drop is learned: a neutral cue acquires it by pairing, after which eating adds "little". The signal behaves like a prediction of the post-intake need, not a reading of the current body. The drop only lasts if nutrients arrive. With false food it recovers at once, which the authors say "is expected to reduce preference for non-food objects".
- Implication for drive versus anticipatory signals: the paper itself splits the two. There is a fast, cue-driven, learned drop, and a slow, nutrient-dependent baseline. An agent that broadcasts only the body-derived drive would miss the first. One that broadcasts only the predicted value would miss the requirement that relief is confirmed by the body. Both components need to be represented.
- Note that the paper pairs this with positive-valence systems (lateral hypothalamus) for the same behaviour. Negative-valence drive reduction is offered as one half of a push-pull, not as the whole of motivation. That concedes part of Berridge's critique (berridge-2004-motivation-concepts.md).
