# Facial mimicry in context: does it only scale, or does it change shape?

Operator question: when a person faces a partner, does their facial response to the partner only scale with relationship, affiliation or familiarity (one gain from flat to copy), or does it change shape (which expressions are returned, which are countered, and when)?

Sources read for this synthesis (one note each): Hess & Fischer 2013; Bourgeois & Hess 2008; Weyers et al. 2009; Seibt et al. 2015; Fischer, Becker & Veenstra 2012; Fischer & Hess 2017; Hess & Bourgeois 2010; Carr, Winkielman & Oveis 2014; Hofman et al. 2012; Mauersberger et al. 2022; Likowski et al. 2008 (abstract only). Related notes already in the index: Dimberg & Thunberg 2012 and Lundqvist 1995 (scoring of rapid facial reactions).

Not read in the original, cited below only as other papers report them: Häfner & IJzerman 2011; Lanzetta & Englis 1989; Likowski et al. 2011a; Seibt et al. 2013; Heerey & Crossley 2013; Likowski et al. 2011b; Sims et al. 2012; Aguado et al. 2013; Mauersberger et al. 2015 (abstract read only); Herrera, Bourgeois, Cheung & Hess 1998.

## Answer: it changes shape

The literature's answer is that context changes the shape of the response, not only its amount. Seibt et al. 2015, reviewing the field, conclude that the moderation "is not just a matter of more or less mimicry", and that the intensity and the type of response depend on the expression and on gaze. Hess & Fischer 2013 model two routes: shared emotion (mimicry), used when the stance is affiliative, and a reactive emotion that can be congruent or incongruent. Carr et al. 2014 title their result "power fundamentally changes facial responding". All the primary studies I read fit this answer. None found a pure scaling pattern when more than one expression was tested.

The shape changes in four ways. Each needs a separate structure in an agent.

### 1. Gating per expression: the relation acts differently on each expression

| Partner expression | Strangers / neutral relation | Close, in-group or liked | Rival, disliked or out-group |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smile / happiness | Returned in labs and in live dyads; most robust (Bourgeois & Hess 2008, both studies; Hess & Bourgeois 2010) | Returned; in the one live friends-vs-strangers study, smiles were coupled only between intimates (Fischer et al. 2012) | Zygomaticus return absent under competition in all three Würzburg experiments (Seibt 2015, Table 1); absent right after an unfair offer (Hofman 2012). Corrugator relaxation to smiles often survives |
| Sadness | Mimicked with photos; costly in social settings | Mimicked for in-group (Bourgeois & Hess 2008 Study 2, sport in-group only); for liked characters (Likowski 2008, abstract) | Not mimicked; brow relaxes instead (negative characters, competitors: Likowski 2008 abstract and Seibt 2015) |
| Anger | Mimicked with context-free photos; not mimicked from unknown men (Bourgeois & Hess 2008 Study 2) or in live conversation (corrugator r = .08, Hess & Bourgeois 2010) | Mimicked when aimed at a shared adversary (Bourgeois & Hess 2008 Study 1). Partners and cooperators answer it with a smile (see 2) | Brow relaxation under competition (Seibt 2015); more anger mimicry toward a partner with an unfair reputation (Hofman 2012) |
| Disgust | Not mimicked in live dyads, by friends or strangers (Fischer et al. 2012) | Not mimicked; congruent responses to disgust predicted worse daily interactions (Mauersberger 2015, via Fischer & Hess 2017) | — |
| Laughter | Mimicked when the laughter is appropriate to its cause; little after disgust scenes (Mauersberger 2022) | Closeness predicts laughter mimicry (Mauersberger 2022) | — |

A single relation gain moves every row the same way. The data move rows in different directions: smile return barely moves with group membership while sad return switches on or off (Bourgeois & Hess 2008); after an unfair reputation, anger return goes up while smile return stays at baseline (Hofman 2012).

### 2. Sign changes: countering instead of copying

- Smile to a partner's anger: high-power perceivers to high-power targets' anger (d = 0.78), low-power perceivers to all anger (d = 0.69) (Carr 2014, read). Romantic partners with communal orientation (Häfner & IJzerman 2011 Study 1, as reported by Seibt 2015 and Hess & Fischer 2013), and subliminally cooperation-primed participants (Seibt 2013, as reported).
- Smile to a partner's sadness: explicit cooperation, mediated by the goal of a smooth interaction (Likowski 2011a, as reported).
- Brow relaxation, a positive-valence response, to a rival's or disliked person's sadness or anger: Likowski 2008 (abstract) and Likowski 2011a / Seibt 2013 (as reported); in Likowski 2011a it was mediated by joy.
- Smiling at a competitor's pain, frowning at a competitor's pleasure: Lanzetta & Englis 1989, as reported by Weyers 2009 and Bourgeois & Hess 2008. Weyers 2009 (read) found reduced mimicry but no significant counter-mimicry under subliminal competition, so countering may need an explicit competitive frame.
- Reading of these: close and cooperative relations can produce a complementary, regulating response (soothing, encouraging). Antagonistic relations produce a reactive response to what the expression means for the observer (the rival's loss is my gain). These are opposite departures from copying, and both appear in the data.

### 3. Timing: early matching, later context

- Carr 2014 (read): corrugator responded before 1,000 ms and was more stimulus-matching; zygomaticus responded at 2,000-4,000 ms and carried the power effects. The two muscles did not co-activate. The authors propose that early responses follow direct matching and later ones reflect context.
- Weyers 2009 (read): the competition effect on corrugator to smiles appeared from the 4th second of an 8-s face and grew; early seconds were similar across groups.
- Against a simple early/late rule: Mauersberger 2022 (read) found context-dependent laughter mimicry within the first second, and Likowski 2011b found mood effects in the second half of the first second (as reported by Seibt 2015). Context can act early on the smile channel when the context is already known.
- Anticipation: genuine smiles are reciprocated before they appear, polite smiles only after (Heerey & Crossley 2013, as reported by Seibt 2015 and Fischer & Hess 2017). Response timing depends on how predictable and rewarding the partner is, which is itself a property of the relation.

### 4. The relation is learned from the partner's behavior, at two time scales

- Within a game, a partner's unfair offer removes smile return immediately; after the game, an unfair reputation increases anger return and a fair reputation decreases it (Hofman 2012, read).
- One pairing of trait words with a face changes later responses (Likowski 2008, abstract); reward paired with a face scales later smile return linearly (Sims et al. 2012, as reported); a face's typical expression transfers to its neutral face (Aguado et al. 2013, as reported).

## What follows for the agent (my inference, not a claim of the sources)

- Replace "gain × copy" with a learned, partner-conditioned linear map from the partner's continuous facial channels to the agent's own, plus a latency per channel. The data require off-diagonal entries (smile to anger, smile to sadness) and negative entries (brow relaxation to a rival's distress). A low-dimensional version (one scalar gain) predicts uniform scaling across expressions; the ablation test is whether the agent can reproduce the Bourgeois & Hess pattern (smile flat, sadness switching) and the Hofman pattern (opposite changes in two expressions).
- Keep a robust, context-poor core (smile to smile, brow relaxation to smile, fast brow response) and let context act mainly on the slower and the negative-expression components. This matches both the timing data and the robustness of smile return.
- The map's inputs must include where the partner's expression is aimed (gaze, head) and what it is about (the shared episode), not only the partner's face, because identical faces get different responses (anger at a common foe vs at me; laughter at a funny vs a disgusting scene).
- The partner model is learned from interaction history, not labelled (fits Principles 1-2): a fast component (what just happened) and a slow one (reputation, closeness).
- None of this rewards the partner's smiles (Principle 6): the sign changes come from the relation and the shared situation, not from maximising the partner's positive expressions.

## What remains open

- Close, long-term relationships are barely studied. Seibt 2015 found only two publications with long-standing partners (Fischer et al. 2012; Häfner & IJzerman 2011), and I could read only the first. Whether mimicry between partners of months or years differs in shape from mimicry between new friends is open. This is the case that matters for S6 (living with the operator).
- Live interaction is rare. Most evidence is from photos, avatars or recorded videos; the live studies (Hess & Bourgeois 2010; Fischer et al. 2012) used 15-s epochs or few-second clips with correlations across dyads, which cannot resolve lag. Timing of context effects in live, two-way interaction is open.
- The early-matching / late-context split rests on two small studies (Carr 2014, Weyers 2009) and is contradicted for laughter (Mauersberger 2022). Whether it is a general rule is open.
- Whether people mimic discrete expressions at all, beyond valence, is unsettled (Hess & Fischer 2013 found little evidence; most studies measure only corrugator and zygomaticus). The literature cannot say which AU-level channels are coupled in which relation.
- Replication: many key results come from one lab group (Würzburg), with women only, avatar stimuli and cells of about 15-25 people. Effect sizes for the shape changes are not pooled anywhere I read. Holland et al. 2021 (meta-analysis of mimicry-empathy correlations) does not address context; I did not read it.
- Dynamics within an exchange (does the response change as the partner's expression changes, and how fast does a relation update) are, in Seibt 2015's words, a "next step" that had not been studied in detail.
