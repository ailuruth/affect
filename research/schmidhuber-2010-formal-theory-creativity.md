# Schmidhuber 2010, "Formal Theory of Creativity, Fun, and Intrinsic Motivation (1990-2010)" (IEEE Trans. Auton. Mental Dev. 2(3):230-247)

Source read: the author's PDF (people.idsia.ch/~juergen/ieeecreative.pdf, 18 pages, author pagination 1-18, not the journal layout). Read on 2026-11-25. Read in full: §I (introduction), §II.A-B (agent, intrinsic reward, model quality), §III.A-C (the 1990, 1991 and 1995 implementations), §IV.A (beyond traditional information theory), the part of §IV.D that responds to Oudeyer et al. and Friston et al., §IV.E.1-2 (psychology, aesthetics), §V item D of the typology (vanishing rewards), §VII (concluding remarks). ## Claims
- The intrinsic reward is model improvement, not model error: "rint(t+1) = f[C(p(t), h(≤t+1)), C(p(t+1), h(≤t+1))]" (eq. 2), with "most obvious ... f(a, b) = a − b. This corresponds to a discrete time version of maximizing the first derivative of the model's quality." Both models are scored on the same data: "the old one is trained only on h(≤t) and the new one also gets to see h(t ≤ t+1)."
- Quality C is ideally compression: Cl(p, h(≤t)) is "the number of bits needed to specify both the predictor and the deviations of the sensory history from its predictions"; with runtime, "Clτ(p, h(≤t)) = Cl(p, h(≤t)) + log τ(p, h(≤t))" (eq. 4). The simpler prediction-error measure Cxry is a sum of squared prediction errors over the whole history.
- Noise and darkness are both boring: "A vision-based agent that always stays in the dark will experience an extremely compressible, soon totally predictable history ... In front of a screen full of white noise ... it will experience highly unpredictable and fundamentally incompressible data. ... in both cases the data is not surprising but boring ... as it does not allow for further compression progress". The red/blue box example: blue boxes hold "a generator of unpredictable, incompressible white noise", so the learned policy is "open the next unopened red box". (The paper does not use the phrase "noisy TV".)
- The 1990 error-based version fails on noise: it "encourages the controller to focus its search on those parts of the environment where there will always be high prediction errors due to noise or randomness, or due to computational limitations of the predictor."
- On Friston's dark room (§IV.D): "their agents really want to stabilize and make everything predictable", whereas "the theory of creativity has no problem whatsoever with dark rooms—the latter get boring as soon as they are predictable; then there is no learning progress no more, that is, the first derivative of predictability / compressibility is zero, that is, the intrinsic reward is zero".

## Evidence
Theoretical and review paper. The 1991 implementation (§III.B) learns a network H "to approximate the expected first derivative of the model's prediction error"; "The absolute value of H's output is taken as the intrinsic reward". On noise: "although noise is unpredictable and leads to wildly varying target signals for the predictor, in the long run these signals do not change the adaptive predictor's parameters much, and the predictor of predictor changes is able to learn this." The 1995 version (§III.C) uses information gain, "the Kullback-Leibler distance between the learning predictor's subjective probability distributions on possible next events before and after new observations". Experimental claims are stated only as "experiments show the advantages ... over conventional random exploration"; no numbers in the parts read. In §IV.D he rebuts Oudeyer et al.'s criticism of single-step progress estimates: the 1991 system "is in fact precisely designed to deal with stochastic fluctuations".

No animal or human data. Psychology is cited (Berlyne, "nonhomeostatic drives [25]", Piaget) without new evidence.

## Runs on this Mac without a GPU?
n.a. for the theory. The universal (compression-optimal) version is not computable in practice, by the author's own account ("theoretically optimal (but not necessarily practical)"). The 1991-style approximations are small networks and would run on a CPU (our judgment).

## Verdict: adopt (as the argument and the noise/dark-room framing), reject (as a satiable drive)
- It gives the cleanest single statement that one quantity, learning progress, scores both the dark room and white noise at zero. Cite it for that.
- Satiability: Schmidhuber treats LP as a reward to maximize, never as a regulated variable. It goes to zero only when the world is exhausted: "intrinsic reward for learning progress eventually vanishes in environments where after some time nothing new can be learnt any more". He contrasts this with "traditional regularizers with a priori fixed relative weights". He files curiosity under Berlyne's "nonhomeostatic drives". Nothing here supports a set point on LP; a set point is our design choice (D62) and has to be justified on our own grounds.
- Two details to carry into D62: (1) progress should be scored on a fixed evaluation set (old and new model on the same data), not on whatever the agent sees next, or switching inputs looks like progress; (2) the 1991 version rewards the absolute value of H's output, i.e. the predicted size of the model's change, whatever its direction. That is our reading of the sentence quoted above. A set-point LP should state its sign convention.
