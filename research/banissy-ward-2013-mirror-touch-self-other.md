# Mechanisms of self–other representations and vicarious experiences of touch in mirror-touch synesthesia (Banissy & Ward)

Citation: M. J. Banissy, J. Ward. "Mechanisms of self-other representations and vicarious experiences of touch in mirror-touch synesthesia." *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 7:112, 2013. doi:10.3389/fnhum.2013.00112. PMC3615185 (CC BY).

Source read: the PMC full text (an opinion article of about 1,800 words of body text), read in full including the footnote. It is an opinion piece: its empirical claims are citations, two of them to work then "under revision" (Holle et al.). I report those claims as the authors' report, not as results I have read.

## The argument
- **Everyone has vicarious somatosensation; almost no one feels it.** Observing touch on another person recruits primary and secondary somatosensory cortex, premotor regions and STS (Blakemore et al. 2005, as reported). "For most of us, these vicarious representations are implicit and do not lead to overt sensations."
- **Mirror-touch synaesthesia (MTS)** (~1.6% of people) is the exception: seen touch evokes felt touch. Two spatial subtypes exist, mirrored (more common) and anatomical, matching mirrored and anatomical frames of bimodal visual–tactile cells in macaque parietal cortex (footnote, citing Ishida et al.).
- **Two accounts.** (1) *Threshold*: the shared somatosensory map is over-excitable. (2) *Self–other*: the shared map is normal but the mechanism that keeps vicarious activity tagged as "other" is faulty, so it is disinhibited.
- **Evidence the authors give against a global threshold account:** in their group fMRI study, synaesthetes showed *hypo*-excitability to touch on dummy faces (which do not evoke synaesthesia). "It is not the case that the somatosensory system is hyper-excited in some global (i.e., context-free) sense … the activity within the somatosensory network seems to be differently modulated (or gated)."
- **Similarity gates vicarious touch in everyone.** Visuo-tactile interference from seen touch on a face is largest when the other is more similar to the self in appearance "or even political opinions" (Serino et al., as reported).
- **Candidate gate regions:** anterior insula (the only region distinguishing synaesthete "C" from 12 controls in Blakemore et al. 2005) and right TPJ (reduced grey matter in synaesthetes, as reported), both linked to self–other processing; S2 excitability differences possibly mediated by insula connectivity.
- **A cost predicted, not shown:** heightened emotional empathy in MTS (Banissy & Ward 2007), but possibly worse agency discrimination; MTS is expected to be one of "a constellation of traits associated with atypical mechanisms of self-other representation."

## What it means for the design
1. **Shared body map plus a gate, not two maps.** The brain keeps one somatosensory map for "touched me" and "touched you"; what keeps the other's touch from becoming one's own state is a context-dependent gate (similarity, object vs person) that lives elsewhere (insula, TPJ). This matches the project's self-other-control synthesis and adds a second axis: the gate is *graded by self–other similarity*.
2. **The failure mode has a name and a cost profile.** Disinhibiting the gate gives more empathy and more self–other blurring. That is the biological picture of the copy attractor: not stronger coupling everywhere, but a gate that no longer separates. The gate must be a structural part of the core, not a learned gain that can drift open.
3. **Similarity-dependent gating is a hazard for a relationship that grows.** If the gate opens with perceived similarity, and living together makes partner and agent more alike, the gate opens further: a positive feedback a design must bound.

## Verdict
- **watch (opinion; frames the gate, gives no mechanism).** Useful for the claim that vicarious activity is kept from becoming one's own state by a gate graded by context and self–other similarity, and that loss of the gate (not a higher gain) is what produces felt copying.
