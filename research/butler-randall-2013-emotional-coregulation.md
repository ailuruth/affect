# Butler & Randall 2013, "Emotional Coregulation in Close Relationships" (Emotion Review 5(2):202-210, doi 10.1177/1754073912451630)

Source read, 2026-11-25: the full article (all 9 pages, including the Figure 1 caption and the closing recommendations), from a PDF copy of the published version hosted on a Squarespace site (static1.squarespace.com/.../butler.randall.2013.pdf). It appears to be an author-posted copy; that was not confirmed. The reference list was skimmed only.

## Claims
- The term is used loosely: "the term coregulation has been used to refer to numerous potentially distinct phenomena", including day-to-day affect covariation, reunion transmission and conflict-driven cortisol correlation.
- Proposed operational definition: "a bidirectional linkage of oscillating emotional channels (subjective experience, expressive behavior, and autonomic physiology) between partners, which contributes to emotional and physiological stability for both partners".
- Three criteria for evidence of coregulation:
  1. "bidirectional statistical dependence between relationship partners over time on some measure of emotion";
  2. "emotions oscillating in a coupled fashion around a fluctuating optimal level that is globally stable", that is, the joint state is "morphostatic, meaning that it is dominated by negative feedback processes";
  3. after a perturbation, "dampening oscillations converging on a more optimal level given internal and external demands".
- Morphostatic versus morphogenic (from Butler 2011): "Morphogenic processes are dominated by positive feedback and entail amplified deviations resulting in an altered emotional state", while "morphostatic processes depend on negative feedback to dampen or reverse deviations". Negative-affect reciprocity, emotion transmission and emotional contagion are classed as morphogenic, so they are not coregulation.
- Covariation alone is not diagnostic: Figure 1's caption says "both coregulation and the morphogenic process show emotional covariation ... and coupling ... The processes are distinguished, however, by the presence of a linear trend in the case of morphogenesis and the presence of coupled dampened oscillations following a stimulus in the case of coregulation."
- Stress buffering is distinguished from coregulation by direction. A stranger's presence may calm "in a fairly unidirectional way" (Panel C: no correlation between the two people's variations, but a quicker return to baseline). A close partner should show "a more bidirectional pattern of dampening oscillations". The authors say no data yet compare the two temporal patterns.
- Interpersonal affect regulation (support, guilt induction, distraction) is a behaviour that may or may not produce coregulation, not coregulation itself.
- Open questions the authors list:
  - whether coregulation exists in adults at all ("there does not exist any conclusive evidence to date");
  - its time scale ("seconds, minutes, hours, or days");
  - which subsystems carry it (autonomic over minutes, cortisol over hours);
  - its cost to the regulating partner;
  - its relation to attachment security.

## Evidence
A conceptual review; no new data. The evidence it cites, as the authors report it:
- Feldman 2003: "Mother–infant synchrony was inversely related to infant negative emotionality, whereas father–infant synchrony was positively related to the degree of positive arousal". This is role-specific: mothers modulate negative states, fathers coordinate high-intensity positive arousal.
- Butner, Diamond & Hicks 2007 (daily diaries, coupled oscillator): coupling "was only found for positive emotion, and the frequency of oscillations was in the range of one affective cycle every 5 days". Covariation was "greater on days when couples spent more time together".
- Saxbe & Repetti 2010: covariation of negative but not positive mood, and cortisol linkage, were both stronger in less satisfied couples. The authors read this as negative reciprocity, "the results clearly do not indicate a morphostatic emotion regulatory pattern".
- Schoebi 2008 (6 reports a day for 7 days): partner influence on hard affect at reunion was greater in insecure individuals. The authors argue this is transmission, but note the analysis lacked a linear trend term, so it cannot be classified.
- Coan, Schaefer & Davidson 2006: holding a stranger's hand reduced threat-related neural activity; the husband's hand reduced it further; the highest-quality marriages showed the least threat activity.
- Feldman 2006: higher infant parasympathetic tone predicted later behavioural synchrony.

## Formal models
No equations in the paper. It recommends the coupled-oscillator models of Boker and colleagues and Butner et al., and an analysis with four parts: "(a) average emotional levels (to establish the presence of a nonextreme set point), (b) linear trends ..., (c) covariation and coupling ..., and ideally (d) dampening in response to a perturbation". The operational form, with equations, is in research/butler-2011-temporal-interpersonal-emotion-systems.md (rties).

## What it implies for a dyadic affective core
- Partner influence has at least three distinguishable signatures, and one gain cannot tell them apart:
  - morphostatic coupling: negative feedback on the joint deviation, so damped oscillation back to a set point;
  - morphogenic coupling: positive feedback, so a trend away from it (contagion, reciprocity);
  - unidirectional buffering: a faster return without any correlation between the partners.

  A single scalar gain on a "copy the partner" map yields covariation plus a trend toward the partner's state. That is the morphogenic class, and here it is explicitly not regulation.
- The useful target is "coupled damping": the partner's influence enters the agent's damping (rate) term as well as its level term. The criterion is whether the joint deviation returns toward a set point after a perturbation, not whether the agent's face correlates with the partner's. This fits Principle 6 (homeostasis, satiable). Coregulation is defined by both partners' stability, not by matching.
- Role dependence is built into the cited evidence (mother versus father; regulator versus regulated). The paper's open question about the regulator's cost implies the coupling need not be symmetric.
- Multi-scale: the authors explicitly expect different time scales for different channels (seconds to days). One gain cannot be right at all of them.
- What is NOT shown: the authors concede there is no conclusive evidence that adult coregulation exists in the defined form. Butner et al. showed coupling only for positive affect on a ~5-day cycle, with no perturbation design. The criteria are a test specification, not a result.

## Verdict: adopt (as test criteria and vocabulary)
Its three criteria (bidirectional dependence, a stable non-extreme set point with no trend, damped return after a perturbation) are a ready specification for scoring an agent-human dyad. They also separate "regulates with the partner" from "copies the partner", which correlation-based synchrony scores cannot. Evidence for adult coregulation is admittedly thin, so it is not a claim about what humans do.
