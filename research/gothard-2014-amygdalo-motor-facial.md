# Gothard 2014, "The amygdalo-motor pathways and the control of facial expressions" (Frontiers in Neuroscience 8:43, doi:10.3389/fnins.2014.00043, PMC3958699)

Source read: the full text of the publisher PDF (Frontiers, CC-BY), pp. 1-4, including Figure 1 and its caption. The reference list was skimmed only. It is a 4-page perspective article with no new data; every empirical claim below is Gothard's summary of cited work (mostly macaque tract-tracing by Morecraft et al. and single-unit work from her lab), not something I checked in the primary sources.

## Claims
- A facial expression is "the reflection of a decision", and that decision "depends on the emotional state of the agent, the sensory-motor state of the agent's face, and the evaluation of the ongoing social situation" (p. 1).
- Against a one-way perceive -> evaluate -> act pipeline: the decision "does not take place at an anatomically distinct decision node; rather it emerges from the activity of the entire circuit", which runs as "closed processing loops that carry out parallel reciprocal transformations between sensory and motor processes" informed by visceral input (p. 1).
- Three kinds of facial movement: "(1) voluntary, coordinated by cortical pathways, (2) reflexive, or (3) driven by central pattern generators coordinated by subcortical motor pathways, located mainly in the brainstem" (p. 2). Periaqueductal gray and hypothalamus orchestrate species-typical displays but are "hardly sufficient ... to voluntarily direct a facial expression toward an individual of interest".
- Two cortical routes, from stroke dissociations: middle cerebral artery strokes (M1, premotor) leave patients unable to make "a symmetrical, voluntary smile" but able to "smile normally in response to jokes" (Monrad-Krohn 1924; Hopf et al. 1992 and others). Anterior cerebral artery strokes affecting midcingulate cortex leave voluntary movement but abolish spontaneous emotional expression ("amimia") (p. 2).
- Five cortical face motor areas per hemisphere (Morecraft et al. 2001, 2004, 2007), with an upper/lower face split (Figure 1):
  - Lower face: M1 and ventrolateral premotor (PMCvl) project to the lateral facial nucleus, contralaterally; M4 (caudal midcingulate face area) targets lateral facial nucleus, "especially the motor neurons that supply the upper lip".
  - Upper face: SMA projects to the medial facial nucleus; M3 (rostral midcingulate face area) projects bilaterally to the medial facial nucleus and also to brainstem reticular formation with autonomic centres, so "M3 is in position ... to coordinate both the overt (behavioral) and covert (autonomic) expression of emotions" (p. 3).
  - PMCvl initiates externally cued movements (the partner's face could be the cue) and is where mirror neurons were found for hands; "A full mirror neuron system for facial expression ... has not been experimentally confirmed" (Ferrari et al. 2003 is suggestive) (p. 2). SMA handles self-initiated movement.
- M4 damage impairs emotional smiling but not voluntary smiling (Hopf et al. 1992), the reverse of M1/PMCvl damage, so "M4 is mostly involved in the emotional control of facial expressions" (p. 3).
- The cingulate is "the site where the limbic system gains access to the motor system": the cingulate syndrome (variant of akinetic mutism) brings flat affect, and recovered patients explain their non-response by "a complete lack of desire to interact with others" (p. 3).
- The amygdala is probably not needed to form expressions but "critical for selecting the expressions that are most appropriate for a given social context". Basal amygdala projects directly to PMCvl, M3, M4 and SMA (Figure 1). Bilateral amygdala lesions make monkeys and humans "less reserved" with strangers and more affiliative. Amygdala and M3 form a closed loop.
- Self-monitoring: amygdala and midcingulate neurons respond both when seeing and when producing expressions (Livneh et al. 2012); activity becomes more synchronous during production, amygdala leading. In the amygdala, neurons respond "primarily after the onset of facial activity" measured by intramuscular EMG (Fuglevand et al. 2012), so the amygdala "might be responding primarily to the sensory consequences" of one's own expression (p. 3).
- Gaze: amygdala "eye cells", some active only during mutual eye contact; eye-contact duration "is a strong predictor of facial-expression reciprocation" in monkeys and humans (p. 3).
- Viscera: amygdala and midcingulate receive visceral input (NTS, parabrachial, insula), fire in phase with cardiac and respiratory cycles; 80% of vagal fibres are ascending; perception of emotional faces depends on cardiac phase (Gray et al.) (p. 4).

## Evidence
No new data. Numbers given: five cortical face motor areas; 80% ascending vagal fibres. All anatomy is macaque tract-tracing as summarised; human dissociations are clinical case series as summarised.

## What it implies for the engine
- It gives the motor architecture of a face "driven from inside": not one controller but several parallel motor sources converging on one final common path (the facial nucleus), split by face region. An engine with one AU vector written by one policy collapses this. A closer copy: separate drives into the same muscle layer, at least (a) a deliberate/conceptual route (M1, PMCvl, SMA: what Claude's layer or a cued policy would drive), and (b) an affective route (M3, M4) fed by the latent affective state and by interoception, with (c) brainstem pattern generators for reflexes. Lesioning (a) vs (b) is a ready-made ablation with known human outcomes (voluntary vs emotional facial paresis).
- The upper/lower split is concrete: upper face gets bilateral, more limbic-weighted input (M3, SMA); lower face gets contralateral, more voluntary input (M1, PMCvl) plus M4 for the upper lip. That predicts that the engine's deliberate route should be strongest on the mouth and its affective route should reach brows and eyes, which is why posed smiles miss the eyes.
- Expression is a closed loop: the amygdala responds after EMG onset, to the sensory consequence of one's own expression. That supports building a proprioceptive channel from the engine's face back into its affective state (facial feedback), not a one-way writer.
- Expression is gated by social context and gaze (eye-contact duration predicts reciprocation). Mimicry in the engine should be conditioned on mutual gaze, not fire on any seen face.
- A "decision" in Gothard's sense is not a hand-named emotion; it is the distributed state selecting among motor options, compatible with Principle 2.
- It does not say what is innate vs learned; it is anatomy. Facial mirror neurons are explicitly unconfirmed.
