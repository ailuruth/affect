## Claims
- Initial state: infants do not at first perceive their own emotion states as distinct categories. "a belső ... jegyeket, amelyeket egy érzelmi állapot megjelenése és kifejezése aktivál, kezdetben a csecsemő nem fogja fel tudatosan, vagy legalábbis ... nem rendeződnek még olyan kategoriális csoportokba" [the internal cues activated by an emotion state are at first not consciously perceived by the infant, or at least are not yet organised into categorical groups] (p. 63). The perceptual system starts out oriented to the outside world (exteroceptive), citing Colombo et al. 1990.
- Core proposal: "a szülői érzelemtükrözés egyfajta természetes szociális biofeedback tréningként funkcionál" [parental affect-mirroring functions as a kind of natural social biofeedback training] (p. 72). The mechanism is "a válasz–inger kontingenciák detekciójára illetve a percipiált kontingencia maximalizálására specializálódott kontingenciadetekciós modul" [a contingency detection module specialised for detecting response-stimulus contingencies and maximising perceived contingency] (p. 72).
- How the infant learns its own states: by running the contingency analysis on the parent's mirroring, the infant finds the set of internal and behavioural cues that best predicts it. Because the mirroring tracks the state the parent attributes to the infant, that cue set "megfelelést fog mutatni a csecsemő érzelmi állapotával" [will correspond to the infant's emotion state] (p. 78). Outcome (p. 88): the infant (1) becomes able to perceive and group the internal cues of a categorical emotion, (2) builds "másodlagos reprezentációi[t]" [secondary representations] of its primary states, used to become aware of and self-attribute them, and (3) learns a general "jelölt" [marked] communicative code.
- Markedness: mothers "ösztönösen száliens módon 'megjelölik' affektustükröző megnyilvánulásaikat" [instinctively and saliently 'mark' their affect-mirroring displays] so they are perceptually distinct from their own real emotions, typically by "valódi érzelemkifejezésüknek valamilyen eltúlzott, transzformált változatát" [an exaggerated, transformed version of their real emotion expression] (p. 84). Markedness causes "referenciális lekapcsolás" [referential decoupling]: the emotion is not attributed to the parent (p. 85). High contingency with the infant's own behaviour then anchors it to the infant: "a csecsemő a jelölt tükröző ingert referenciálisan úgy fogja értelmezni, mint ami az ő saját állapotának kifejeződése" [the infant will interpret the marked mirroring stimulus as an expression of its own state] (p. 86).
- Self vs other by contingency level: mirroring is never perfect, and this matters "hiszen tudjuk, hogy a csecsemő rendkívül érzékeny a tökéletes, valamint a magas, de nem tökéletes szintű inger–válasz kontingenciák közötti különbségre" [since we know the infant is extremely sensitive to the difference between perfect and high-but-imperfect stimulus-response contingency] and uses it to sort stimuli into self vs social objects (p. 84, citing Bahrick & Watson 1985; Watson 1979, 1994). A real (unmarked) parental emotion is under far less contingent control of the infant than a marked mirror (pp. 86-87).
- Pathology, stated as hypotheses: unmarked but category-congruent mirroring means the emotion is attributed to the parent, no secondary representation forms, and the infant's negative affect escalates; they link this to projective identification and borderline development (pp. 91-92). Marked but category-incongruent mirroring anchors a wrong category to the self, giving a distorted self-representation (pp. 92-93).

## Evidence
Theoretical paper; no new data. The contingency claims rest on Watson's work as the authors summarise it:
- Two-month-olds kicked more when kicking moved a mobile, not when the mobile moved non-contingently (Watson 1972). Detecting control came with smiling and cooing, so "az ezáltal generált oksági kontroll élménye pozitív arousalt indukál" [the experience of causal control so generated induces positive arousal] (p. 72).
- Two indices (Watson 1979, 1994). The "elégségességi index" [sufficiency index] looks forward: P(stimulus | response). The "szükségességi index" [necessity index] looks backward: P(response preceded | stimulus). Worked example: a mobile tied to the right leg but also moved half the time by wind gives sufficiency 1.00 and necessity 0.5 (pp. 73-74). Note: one sentence on p. 76 labels the forward-looking test "szükségességi index"; this looks like a slip in the translation, because pp. 73 and 78 define it the other way.
- Maximising rule: if necessity exceeds sufficiency, narrow the response class; if sufficiency exceeds necessity, widen it. Response rate is highest "amikor mind az elégségességi, mind a szükségességi index értéke elég magas és egyenlő" [when both indices are fairly high and equal] (p. 75).
- Regulation by contingency: an attuned parent mirrors in bursts, so necessity is high but sufficiency is low. Following the maximising rule, the infant narrows and dampens its negative displays, and this "a negatív érzelmi állapot szabályozásához (mérsékléséhez) vezet" [leads to regulation (moderation) of the negative state] (p. 80). Felt control adds positive affect (pp. 80-81). The infant experiences itself as the agent of the soothing (p. 81).
- Age shift, as the authors report Bahrick & Watson 1985 and Watson 1994 (secondary here; see bahrick-watson-1985-proprioceptive-visual-contingency.md): "a fiatalabb, három hónapos csecsemők inkább nézték lábmozgásuk teljesen kontingens képét ..., míg az öt hónaposokból az ilyen tökéletes válasz–inger kontingencia averziót váltott ki" [the younger, three-month-olds preferred to watch the perfectly contingent image of their leg movements, while in five-month-olds such perfect contingency produced aversion], and after 3 months infants prefer "a magas, de nem tökéletes" [high but not perfect] contingencies typical of a "'elég jó' anya" ['good enough' mother] (p. 73).

## Verdict: adopt (as the developmental model for "contingently answered" and for self/other by contingency level), with caveats
Why adopt:
- It is the primary source for three of the foundation's commitments: contingent response is what the infant seeks and is rewarded by (positive arousal from detected control); self vs other is told apart by perfect vs high-but-imperfect contingency; and an agent learns to read its own states from another's contingent, marked reflection of them. The last one is a mechanism for "one sign map both expresses and reads": the parent's marked display is the same sign as a real emotion but decoupled from the sender.
- It gives operational quantities: two directional conditional probabilities and a maximising rule. These are simpler and more inspectable than transfer entropy, and they split "the partner answers me" (sufficiency) from "only I cause the partner" (necessity). Consider them alongside TE.
- It gives testable failure modes: unmarked mirroring, which escalates, and category-incongruent mirroring, which builds a wrong self-model.

Caveats:
- Satiability is NOT claimed here. The paper says high contingency regulates and pleases, and that infants maximise it. It does not say the need is satiable. "Maximising" suggests the opposite, so satiation must come from elsewhere.
- Markedness, decoupling and the pathology chain are hypotheses with no data in this paper.
- *[Checked 2026-11-25 against the primary: the claim that 3-month-olds preferred the perfectly contingent view is wrong. The 1985 data show a bimodal split (14 of 29 infants below .4, 12 above .6), with no group preference.]* The age-shift claim is Gergely & Watson's summary of Bahrick & Watson. A secondary account (Rochat 1998) reports 3-month-olds as split. Check the 1985 primary before relying on "3-month-olds prefer perfect contingency".
