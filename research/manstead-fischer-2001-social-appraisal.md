# Manstead & Fischer 2001, "Social appraisal: The social world as object of and influence on appraisal processes" (in K. R. Scherer, A. Schorr & T. Johnstone (Eds.), Appraisal Processes in Emotion: Theory, Methods, Research, pp. 221-232. New York: Oxford University Press; no DOI for the chapter)

## Claims (as others describe them)
- Origin: Campos & Stenberg (1981) distinguished individual appraisal from "social appraisal of how another individual is reacting to the event" (quoted in Parkinson & Manstead 2015, p. 373). Manstead & Fischer 2001 "formalized the concept of social appraisal to cover situations in which relational meanings depend on other people's perceived evaluations" (P&M 2015).
- Definition (Hareli & Hess 2019): "a social appraisal is an appraisal whose object of evaluation is others' response to an emotion eliciting situation"; the result is an emotional reaction shaped by it.
- Two aspects, as in the chapter's title (Manstead 2005, Psychologist):
  1. **The social world as influence on appraisal:** "we also appraise the reactions of others, including the emotional responses of others to the same event". Example: "A comedy viewed in the company of a friend who seems not to find it funny is much less amusing."
  2. **The social world as object of appraisal:** "how we think others would react to our emotional behaviours", that is, the anticipated social consequences of one's own expression shape whether and how it is expressed.
- Social appraisal is not only about resolving ambiguity about the event; others' reactions also set **norms** for emotion and action. Their example: gauging a companion's reaction to a potentially offensive joke (P&M 2015).
- Developmental root: social referencing in infancy (Manstead 2005; Sorce et al. 1985 visual cliff).
- 2017 commentary (abstract only): at one level of analysis social referencing and social appraisal are functionally equivalent; at another there is "an important difference" between them. I could not read what that difference is.
- Hareli & Hess 2019 on the relation to reverse engineering of appraisals: social appraisal concerns how the perceiver's own reaction to a situation is reshaped by the other's emotion; reverse engineering concerns what the perceiver infers about the expresser (often beyond the here and now). Social appraisal "can" be based on reverse engineering but need not be.

## Evidence (all as reported by others)
- Timmers, Fischer & Manstead 1998 (via Manstead 2005): women tend to express anger to a third party rather than to the person who angered them; men show the reverse. No numbers in the summary.
- Evers, Fischer, Rodriguez Mosquera & Manstead 2005 (via Manstead 2005): in a hot-sauce allocation paradigm men allocated more than women; the gap was largest after negative feedback when participants expected to meet the other later; women's lower expression was partly mediated by anticipated negative social consequences. No numbers in the summary.
- Fischer, Rotteveel, Evers & Manstead 2004 (via Parkinson & Simons 2009): imagined poor grade; more anger when others reacted angrily, more sadness when others reacted sadly; blame appraisals did not change. This is transfer without the mediating appraisal change the theory predicts.
- Later social-appraisal studies listed in P&M 2015: van der Schalk et al. 2015 (seeing another regret a fair allocation led to anticipated regret and less fair allocations); Lelieveld et al. 2013 (higher ultimatum offers to a disappointed in-group member, mediated by guilt). Bruder, Dosmukhambetova, Nerb & Manstead 2012 (abstract via search): in direct interaction friends converged strongly in expressions, appraisals and feelings, except fear-related appraisals. Not read.

## Formal model
None known. The chapter is a conceptual extension of appraisal theory.

## What it implies for the design
- The concept is the core of the hypothesis under test: the partner's expression enters as an **appraisal input** (evidence about how the partner evaluates the shared event), and the agent's own appraisal integrates it with the agent's own concerns. The comedy example is a pure case: the friend's non-amusement lowers my amusement at the same film; it is not that I copy their flat face, it is that the film is re-evaluated.
- The second aspect gives a separate, action-side component the critiqued designs lack: **the agent's own expression is evaluated by its anticipated effect on the partner** (display regulation). In the agent this is a policy over the agent's own face scored by predicted partner response, distinct from the feeling. It can produce the human pattern of withholding or redirecting an expression (anger expressed to a third party rather than the target) and complementary displays (smiling to calm an angry partner), without any coupling from the partner's face to the agent's face.
- Norm-setting means the partner's reaction is evidence about what is appropriate here, not only about what is out there; this is information about the shared social situation and accumulates over time with a given partner (a slow, relation-level variable).
- Hidden scalar check: the "influence" aspect reduces, in any probabilistic implementation, to how strongly the partner's perceived evaluation moves the agent's own appraisal (a social-evidence weight). At full weight and with identical concerns, the agent feels what the partner shows. The theory's own content keeps that from being the whole story: the "object" aspect (anticipated consequences of my display) and the agent's own concerns are independent of that weight. A design built from this concept must show that the evidence weight and the display policy are separate parameters, or it collapses back to one gain.
- Weakness for this project: the empirical base for the influence route is mostly vignettes and self-report, and the best-known direct test (Fischer et al. 2004) found transfer without the predicted appraisal change.

## Verdict: watch (unread original); adopt the concept as the frame for the "evidence, not transfer" hypothesis
The two-aspect structure (others' reactions as input to my appraisal; others' anticipated reactions as an object of my appraisal) is worth adopting. Read the chapter and the 2017 commentary before citing any specific wording.
