# Bourgeois & Hess 2008, "The impact of social context on mimicry" (Biological Psychology 77:343-352, doi 10.1016/j.biopsycho.2007.11.008)

Source read: full article PDF (author-hosted manuscript, ursulakhess.com/resources/BH08.pdf), both studies, methods and results.

## Claims
- Mimicry depends on both the relation to the expresser and on which expression is shown: "participants mimicked happiness displays regardless of the expresser's group membership, negative emotions were either not mimicked or only when shown by an ingroup member."
- Anger is mimicked only when it is plausibly aimed at a shared adversary (Study 1: politicians in a debate, filmed in 4/5 profile, looking at each other). Unexplained anger from strangers was not mimicked (Study 2).
- Sadness mimicry is "costly" (it signals readiness to help) and so is reserved for in-group members.

## Evidence
- EMG, left side: corrugator supercilii, zygomaticus major, orbicularis oculi (Study 2 also levator, dropped for missing data). Integrated with a 200 ms time constant, sampled at 20 Hz, z-scored within participant. Mimicry tested as a planned contrast across muscles (corrugator vs the two smile muscles).
- Study 1: N = 54 Quebec students, mostly sympathetic to one politician (Bouchard) over the other (Charest). Twelve silent debate clips (3 smiles and 3 frowns each, mean 13.2 s). Anger: politician × muscle F(2,51) = 10.55, p < .0001, η² = .29; more corrugator to Bouchard's anger, F(1,52) = 10.67, p = .002, η² = .17, and less orbicularis oculi, F(1,52) = 9.09, p = .004, η² = .15. Happiness: smile pattern present, but no politician × muscle interaction, F(2,51) = .72, p = .491. The two politicians are different people; the authors raise a baby-face confound for Charest's smiles.
- Study 2: N = 60 French-Canadian men, half basketball players. Photos of happy, sad and angry faces of Quebecois and Sub-Saharan African men (MSFDE), each labelled as basketball player or not, labels counterbalanced, so the same face was in-group for some participants and out-group for others. Anger: no significant effects. Happiness: smile contrast F(1,59) = 18.80, p < .0001, η² = .24, with no interaction with either group factor. Sadness: contrast F(1,59) = 26.34, p < .0001, η² = .31, qualified by player group × ethnic group × muscle, F(2,58) = 7.23, p = .002, η² = .20; on the per-person contrast, main effect of player group F(1,59) = 5.86, p = .019, η² = .09; double in-group M = .33 (SD 1.13), player-in/ethnic-out M = .24 (SD .70), both player-out-group cells "close to 0". The salient category (sport) overrode ethnicity.

## What it implies for the design
- This is direct evidence for "changes shape", not only "scales": the relation acts per expression. Smile return is flat across in-group and out-group; sad return switches from present to absent; anger return depends on whom the anger targets. One relation gain multiplying a copy cannot produce this pattern (it would scale all three together).
- Which relation is salient is itself a learned variable (sport beat ethnicity here). For an agent living with one operator, the relevant "group" is likely shared goals and history, not appearance.
- Effect sizes are modest and samples are small (54, 60); the 20 Hz, 200 ms-integrated signal and whole-clip averaging say nothing about timing within the first second.

## Verdict: adopt (as primary evidence for expression-specific gating by relation)
