# Parkinson 2011, "Interpersonal Emotion Transfer: Contagion and Social Appraisal" (Social and Personality Psychology Compass 5(7):428-439, doi 10.1111/j.1751-9004.2011.00365.x)

Source read: **abstract only** (via Crossref). The article is closed access (Unpaywall and Semantic Scholar list no open copy; the Wiley PDF needs a subscription). To report its argument I read in full three papers by the same author that cover the same ground and cite it: Parkinson & Simons 2009 (PSPB, accepted manuscript; separate note), Parkinson & Manstead 2015 "Current emotion research in social psychology: thinking about emotions and other people" (Emotion Review 7(4):371-380, doi 10.1177/1754073915590624; published PDF from Oxford ORA), and Parkinson & Simons "Worry spreads: interpersonal transfer of problem-related anxiety" (Cognition & Emotion 2012, submitted manuscript from ORA). Statements below marked [abstract] are from the 2011 paper; everything else is from those companion papers and is labelled.

## Claims
- [abstract] Exposure to someone else's emotion can produce similar feelings (interpersonal emotion transfer, IET). Two processes may contribute: emotion contagion and social appraisal.
- [abstract] People automatically mimic others' perceived movements, including emotion expressions, but **IET does not seem to depend directly on mimicry**, so other processes must underlie contagion.
- [abstract] Social appraisal is supported by studies where IET depends on changes in explicit interpretations and evaluations of events, and on **implicitly registered cues about the direction of attention**.
- [abstract] Future work must focus on the **object-focus** of emotion expressions and their contextual meaning to explain the variety of IET effects.
- [P&M 2015, citing Parkinson 2011b] "There is little direct evidence for operation of the specific mimicry–feedback–emotion sequence"; most evidence for contagion is indirect, obtained by ruling out explicit mediators (Parkinson & Simons 2009), which "does not confirm the specific nature of the nonexplicit process that is ruled in".
- [P&M 2015] Embodied effects of seeing expressions "do not seem to involve direct mimicry but rather reflect the implicitly perceived meaning of the expressions" (citing Hess, Houde & Fischer 2014).
- [P&M 2015] Gaze-cued appraisal as a distinct, non-inferential route: a face's gaze directs attention to an object and its expression then colours the evaluation of that object.
- [P&M 2015] Against treating emotions only by their category: "do the separate components of their growing anger (the raised voice, focused stare, or bodily agitation) induce more direct lower level adjustment that need not depend on their perceived status as symptoms of emotion?" Infants and early hominids were affected by others' emotions before they could categorise them.
- [Worry spreads] Three anxiety-transfer routes: object-directed social appraisal (I worry about what you worry about), empathic worry (I worry about you because you are worried; person-directed), and interpersonal meta-worry (your worry itself is the threat to me). Anxiety has two interpersonal functions in tension, alerting and comfort-seeking, which produce "mutually incongruent" responses: a partner may answer worry with calm.

## Evidence
All as reported in the companion papers, not read in their originals unless noted.
- Bayliss, Frischen, Fenske & Tipper 2007: objects looked at by a smiling face were liked more, and objects looked at by a disgusted face liked less, **only when the face's eyes turned toward the object**, not away; participants did not report noticing the gaze (P&M 2015; Worry spreads).
- Mumenthaler & Sander 2012: an angry face gazing at a target face made participants more likely to label the target "fear"; with peripheral, brief presentation the effect persisted (P&M 2015; Worry spreads).
- Parkinson, Phiri & Simons 2012: pairs of friends, Balloon Analogue Risk Task over a soundless two-way video link. When the watching friend was covertly told to express rather than hold back anxiety, the player pumped less, **but only under a gain frame, not a loss frame**; the watcher's trait expressivity (Berkeley Expressivity Questionnaire) correlated negatively with the player's risk-taking in both frames (Worry spreads; no effect sizes given there).
- Sorce et al. 1985 visual cliff: almost all infants crossed toward a smiling mother, none toward a fearful one (Parkinson & Simons 2009).
- Fischer, Rotteveel, Evers & Manstead 2004: participants imagining a poor grade reported more anger when others were described reacting with anger, more sadness when others reacted with sadness, without changes in blame appraisals, which makes social appraisal unlikely there (Parkinson & Simons 2009).
- Parkinson & Simons 2009 diary data (read in full; see that note): both routes present, transfer survives control of appraisals and of perception of the other.

## Formal model
None. The framework is a verbal two-route (later three-route) process distinction tested by mediation.

## What it implies for the design
- **Mimicry is not the carrier of transfer.** If IET does not depend on mimicry, then the agent's face copying the partner is neither necessary nor sufficient for the agent's affect to move with the partner's. This is a direct argument against the shared structure of the critiqued designs, where the partner's face enters the agent's own face through a map and the agent's affect follows its own face. The partner's expression can change the agent's affect without the agent's face matching it, and the agent's face can match without its affect changing.
- **Object-focus is the gate.** The same expression means different things depending on what it is directed at (gaze toward vs away; anger at me vs at a third party, already in Hess & Fischer 2013). In an inference design, the partner's gaze and head direction select which part of the shared situation the expression is evidence about. This is a content-dependent selection, not a gain: the expression updates the appraisal of the attended object, and the agent's own concerns about that object decide the sign of its response.
- **Three objects of concern, three routes, three signs.** Following Worry spreads: (1) the shared object (agent's appraisal of the world), (2) the partner as someone whose wellbeing matters to the agent (empathic), (3) the partner's state as itself a threat (meta-worry). A rival's distress is good news on route 2 with a negative weight (schadenfreude, Seibt 2015's brow relaxation); a partner's worry answered with calm is route 2 producing a regulating, complementary display. This maps the sign flips in the mimicry-context synthesis onto where the expression is routed, not onto a coupling sign.
- **Hidden scalar check.** Each route has its own weight: precision of the partner's expression as evidence about the object (route 1), the signed weight of the partner's wellbeing in the agent's concerns (route 2), and the cost the agent attaches to the partner's state (route 3). Route 2's signed weight is a one-dimensional dial from schadenfreude through indifference to empathic distress. It is not a flat-to-copy dial on the face, because what it scales is the inferred partner appraisal passed through the agent's concerns, and route 1 is unaffected by it. Whether these three weights collapse to one in practice (a single "closeness" learned from history) is the risk to check.
- The gain-frame vs loss-frame result says the agent's own current orientation (already attending to costs) reduces the impact of the partner's anxiety: the evidence weight depends on the agent's own prior state, as in precision-weighted cue combination.

## Verdict: watch (unread original); adopt the two-route distinction and the object-focus gate
The distinction and the "IET does not depend directly on mimicry" claim are supported by the author's own full papers I read. Read the 2011 original before quoting any of its specific evidence counts.
