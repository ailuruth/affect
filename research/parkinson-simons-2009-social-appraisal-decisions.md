# Parkinson & Simons 2009, "Affecting Others: Social Appraisal and Emotion Contagion in Everyday Decision Making" (Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 35(8):1071-1084, doi 10.1177/0146167209336611)

Source read: the authors' accepted manuscript (Oxford ORA copy, 9,712 words), in full, including Tables 2-7 as extracted by pdftotext (table layout partly scrambled; coefficients below are only those I could match to their rows). Figure 1 (the two-route path diagram) is described in the text; the figure itself did not extract.

## Claims
- Two routes of interpersonal emotion transfer, separated by the role of appraisal:
  - **Social appraisal:** the other's perceived emotion is information that changes my appraisal of a shared object (risk, importance); the changed appraisal then changes my emotion. Other → my appraisal → my emotion.
  - **Emotion contagion:** I catch the other's affect without registering its significance (Hatfield et al. 1994 mimicry-feedback, or direct activation of action codes, Neumann & Strack 2000). Other → my emotion, not mediated by appraisal; the caught emotion may then colour appraisal (other → my emotion → my appraisal).
- Operational test: social appraisal predicts that appraisals mediate other→own emotion, and that other→own appraisal survives control for own emotion. Contagion predicts a residual other→own emotion after appraisals are controlled, and that other→appraisal effects vanish once own emotion is controlled.
- Both routes operate in everyday decisions. Transfer survived control for all measured appraisals and for the participant's own perception of the other's emotion, so transfer "does not require explicit registration of someone else's feelings".
- A third route the authors cannot exclude: the expression as an **emotion elicitor** rather than an emotion messenger (Ruys & Stapel 2008): an anxious face or voice is itself an unpleasant stimulus. Proposed discriminating test: elicitor effects should be the same whatever the relationship; contagion is assumed to depend on power and warmth.
- Explicit verbal communication of emotion is more likely to work through social appraisal than contagion.

## Evidence
- Design: event-contingent diary on handheld computers, 3 weeks, N = 41 (15 m, 26 f, age 18-52, M = 26.8). 377 entries, 349 complete, 232 in which the participant was aware of the other's feelings (39 participants) were analysed with multilevel models (HLM, decisions within persons, predictors person-centred). Pilot N = 24 gave similar results (not reported).
- Measures (0-100 scales): own anxiety and excitement at T1 (first considering the decision) and T2 (deciding); other's anxiety and excitement as perceived; appraisals of importance, risk of the chosen option, probability of negative outcome. 63% of participants were partnered; the "reference person" was the spouse or partner for all but two of them. The other was the reference person in 60% of decisions; 41% were joint decisions, 29% the other advised.
- Validity of perceiving the other: person-centred correlations between the participant's rating and the reference person's self-report (49 decisions, 19 participants): excitement r = .64 (T1), .46 (T2); anxiety r = .33 (T1), .38 (T2). Anxiety is read less accurately than excitement.
- Transfer (single-predictor models, Δ−2LL): other's T1 anxiety → own T1 anxiety 67.06; other's T2 anxiety → own T2 anxiety 40.17; other's T1 anxiety → own T2 anxiety 26.35; excitement analogues 47.01, 22.40, 34.10 (all p < .001). Excitement transfer was stronger in joint decisions (interaction coefficient 9.70, t(38) = 3.06, p = .004).
- Social-appraisal evidence: other's anxiety predicted own importance and risk appraisals (not outcome probability). Importance and risk partially mediated anxiety transfer (Sobel z = 2.79-4.13, all p ≤ .006). Other's T2 anxiety still predicted importance and risk after controlling own T2 anxiety; other's T2 excitement still predicted importance after controlling own excitement.
- Contagion-like evidence: all other→own emotion effects stayed significant after controlling all appraisals. Other's T1 anxiety → own appraisals became non-significant once own T1 anxiety was controlled (the contagion ordering: caught emotion, then appraisal).
- With the reference person's own self-report as predictor (pooled within-person, n = 49): reference anxiety T1 → participant anxiety T1 r = .42; reference excitement T1 → participant excitement T1 r = .57 and T2 r = .44; reference excitement T2 → participant excitement T2 r = .44. None mediated by appraisals; each survived control for the participant's perception of the other's emotion (β = .28 to .57).
- Unexplained: other's T1 excitement → own T2 excitement survived controlling own T1 excitement and other's T2 excitement, and was not mediated by any measured appraisal (none of the appraisals predicted excitement at all). The authors suspect unmeasured positive-expectation appraisals.
- Limits: correlational; T1 recalled retrospectively; verbal channels uncontrolled; one-item emotions; appraisal set chosen for anxiety, not excitement.

## Formal model
None beyond the path diagram and Baron-Kenny mediation. The model is two linear paths: e_self = a·e_other + b·A + ε (contagion, a) and A = c·e_other + ε (social appraisal, c·b), with A the agent's appraisal of the shared object.

## What it implies for the design
- The human data need both routes. A design where the partner's face enters only as evidence about the shared situation would miss a real, appraisal-independent, same-sign transfer of anxiety and excitement that survives even when the perceiver does not register the partner's emotion. In valence-arousal terms this residual is exactly the robust "context-poor core" the mimicry-context synthesis already asked for (smile return, fast brow response).
- The routes predict different signatures, which gives ablation predictions for an agent:
  - Social appraisal acts on the agent's evaluation of a **shared object**; its effect should vanish when there is no common object (partner reacting to something the agent does not face) and should be stronger in joint decisions (it was, for excitement).
  - Contagion acts without an object; it should persist when the object is absent and should not change the agent's model of the situation.
  - An elicitor effect should be the same for every partner; contagion should depend on the relation.
- Does an evidence-only design still hide a flat-to-copy scalar? On this paper's model, yes in one place: the weight c (how much the partner's emotion moves my appraisal of risk) is a likelihood-precision scalar. With c → ∞ and my concerns equal to the partner's, my appraisal equals theirs and my emotion copies theirs; with c = 0 I am flat to them. The difference from the critiqued designs is where the copy appears: it is a copy of the **appraisal of a shared object, passed through my own concern function**. It becomes a copy of the face only if my concerns about that object coincide with the partner's. Where they differ (the object is a threat to them and a gain to me), the same c produces a response of the opposite sign. So the scalar exists, but it no longer spans flat-to-copy by itself; the concern map sets the shape.
- The data do not show sign flips; partners here were mostly spouses sharing a decision, the case where concerns align most and both routes are congruent.

## Verdict: adopt (as the empirical separation of the two routes in live relationships)
The clearest naturalistic evidence that social appraisal and appraisal-independent transfer coexist, with relationship partners. Weaknesses: self-report, small N, correlational, verbal channel uncontrolled. It establishes that a design needs a same-sign direct path as well as an inference path, and offers concrete dissociation tests.
