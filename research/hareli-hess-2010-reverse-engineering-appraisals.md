# Hareli & Hess 2010, "What emotional reactions can tell us about the nature of others: An appraisal perspective on person perception" (Cognition and Emotion 24(1):128-140, doi 10.1080/02699930802613828)

Source read: full article PDF (author-hosted copy, ursulakhess.com/resources/HH10.pdf), all sections, Tables 1-2. Also read in full the authors' later restatement, Hareli & Hess 2019, "The reverse engineering of emotions: observers of others' emotions as naïve personality psychologists" (ch. 6 in Hess & Hareli (Eds.), The Social Nature of Emotion Expression, Springer, doi 10.1007/978-3-030-32968-6_6; ursulakhess.com/resources/HH19-CH6.pdf). Studies the chapter cites are reported as it describes them.

## Claims
- Observers "reverse engineer" the appraisals behind another person's emotional reaction (how that person must have seen the situation) and use the reconstructed appraisals to infer the person's character.
- Anger to blame signals urgency, unpleasantness, norm incompatibility and coping potential → perceived as aggressive and self-confident, less warm. Sadness signals goal obstruction without redress → less confident, warmer. A smile or calm reaction to failure signals that the person is "above the situation" → confident but cold.
- It is the reconstructed appraisal, not the expression's generic signal value, that drives the inference: a smile, usually an affiliative signal, made the person look *less* warm than anger did when it was a reaction to being blamed for a failure.
- Same appraisals have the same effect across different emotions (unpleasantness and norm incompatibility mediated warmth for both anger and sadness), so the inference runs through appraisal dimensions.
- (2019 chapter) The information reaches beyond the moment: from appraisals to goals, values, resources, action tendencies, and stable traits (dominance, affiliation, competence). Context changes the weight: when the observer knows the expresser well or holds a stereotype, the expression's characteristic appraisals may carry "very little if any weight", and the observer reinterprets the situation instead (anger from women attributed to personality, not to the event; Brescoll & Uhlmann 2008). When an expression does not fit the situation, observers add a cause (fear toward a kitten → "cat phobic", Hess & Hareli 2017).
- (2019) Facial actions may be a direct readout of appraisals (Scherer's component process view: brow lowering ↔ goal obstruction).

## Evidence
- Study 1: N = 243 students (Haifa; 141 f, 101 m; age M = 26). Vignette, between-subjects 2 (candidate sex) × 3 (reaction: anger, smile, sadness); job-interview story of a failed project followed by colleagues' blame. Six single-item trait scales, 0-6.
  - Emotion main effect (MANOVA) F(6, 472) = 20.67, η² = .21. Aggressive: anger 3.45 vs smile 2.04 vs sadness 1.93 (η² = .25). Self-confident: anger 3.70, smile 3.67, sadness 2.56 (η² = .14). Masculine: 4.16, 3.96, 3.17 (η² = .15). Emotional/warm/gentle composite (α = .78): sadness 4.35 > anger 3.74 > smile 3.22 (η² = .20).
- Study 2: N = 183 (153 f, 25 m; age M = 25). Same vignette; smile replaced by "reacted calmly"; participants also rated the candidate's presumed appraisals (1-6).
  - Traits: MANOVA F(6, 352) = 15.00, η² = .20. Aggressive: anger 3.03, neutral 1.74, sadness 1.42. Self-confident: neutral 4.33 > anger 3.38, sadness 3.03. Warm composite (α = .81): sadness 4.71 > anger 4.27 > neutral 1.65 (η² = .47).
  - Appraisals: MANOVA F(6, 350) = 31.25, η² = .35. Urgency: anger 4.00, sadness 3.48, neutral 1.98 (η² = .25). Unpleasantness: sadness 5.29, anger 4.97, neutral 2.30 (η² = .58). Norm incompatibility: anger 4.84, sadness 4.34, neutral 2.19 (η² = .42). Goal conduciveness and coping potential: no effect (all low; the authors suspect a ceiling of obvious failure).
  - Mediation (multiple mediators, Sobel): anger→aggressive via urgency (indirect b = .17, z = 2.97; direct effect .48 → .21 n.s.). Anger vs neutral → lower self-confidence via unpleasantness (indirect −.19; direct −.34 → −.04). Anger → warm via unpleasantness (.28) and norm incompatibility (.21); direct .62 → .10 n.s. Sadness → warm via the same two (.19, .13), direct effect reduced but still significant (.73 → .41).
- Limits: written vignettes only, no faces; student samples; one situation (blame for failure); single-item traits; cross-sectional mediation.

## Formal model
None in the paper (verbal model plus linear mediation). The 2019 chapter's Figure 6.1 draws two chains: event × person (goals, values, resources) → appraisal → emotion and action tendency (forward), and observed emotion + event → reconstructed appraisal → inferred goals, values, resources, traits (reverse). This is the same structure that Ong, Zaki & Goodman later write as Bayesian inversion, P(b, d | x, o) ∝ P(b, d) Σ_e P(x | e) P(e | b, d, o) (see the Ong notes).

## What it implies for the design
- **Per-expression gating arises from what each expression says.** Different expressions carry information about different appraisal dimensions (urgency and coping for anger; loss without redress for sadness; low unpleasantness for a smile in adversity). An agent that reads the partner's expression as evidence about the partner's appraisal therefore updates different parts of its model for each expression, and responds differently per expression, with no per-expression gain table. This is a principled origin for the mimicry-context table's rows moving in different directions.
- **Sign flips of meaning are already in the inference.** The smile to blame lowered perceived warmth below anger. The same partner smile means "affiliation" in one situation and "indifference or contempt" in another. A transfer map (smile → my smile) cannot represent this; an inference over situation and appraisal can. In the agent, the shared episode (what just happened to whom) must be an input to reading the face.
- **Slow partner model.** The reconstructed appraisals feed stable traits (dominance, affiliation, competence, coping resources). This is the slow relation variable the mimicry-context synthesis asked for (Hofman 2012 reputation; Likowski 2008), with a stated content: the partner's typical concerns and resources, learned from how they react to events.
- **Prior can override the face.** The 2019 chapter says the expression's appraisal meaning can get "very little if any weight" when the observer knows the person well or holds a strong expectation. In Bayesian terms this is a likelihood precision on the face that varies with how well the partner is known. That is a scalar, and at its extremes the agent either reads every face at face value or ignores faces. But it is a precision on the *inference* of the partner's appraisal, not on the agent's own facial output: even at maximal precision, the agent's response is its own concern function applied to the inferred situation, and copying results only where concerns coincide.
- **Label problem.** The study and the theory are phrased in emotion categories (anger, sadness) and named appraisal dimensions. Principles 1-2 forbid both below the language layer. What carries over is the structure (expression → partner's evaluation of the event → partner's goals and resources), not the vocabulary. Grounding it without labels would need the appraisal dimensions to be the agent's own body-coordinate quantities (for example, expected change in the agent's own regulated variables, urgency as the rate at which that change arrives, coping as the agent's own controllability) applied to the partner through the agent's own generative model (Principle 3). This paper offers no help with that step.

## Verdict: adopt (as evidence for the inference route and its per-expression structure); reject its label vocabulary for the engine
Clean, mediated evidence that people read others' reactions as information about how those others appraise a situation, and that the same expression's meaning depends on the situation. Text vignettes only: whether the same mediation holds for faces in live interaction is not shown here.
