# Goldstein, Schwade & Bornstein 2009, "The value of vocalizing: Five-month-old infants associate their own noncry vocalizations with responses from caregivers" (Child Development 80(3):636-644, doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01287.x, PMC4151607)

Source read: the NIH author manuscript on PubMed Central, in full (introduction, method, results with Tables 1-2, discussion); Figures 1-4 by caption and the text that reports them; references not read.

Read for search pass 9 (which learning signal shapes the infant's own output, and is it approval-closed).

## Key claims
- **Design.** 38 infants, 5.36 months (10 more excluded for crying or fussing), 3-episode still-face with one unfamiliar female experimenter: 1 min interaction (talking, no touch, no toys), 2 min still face (neutral, silent, looking at the infant), 1 min interaction. Vocalizations coded acoustically (Oller's categories; syllable counts, r = .94); smiles coded from the lip-corner raise (zygomatic), r = .91. 95.9 % of vocalizations were quasi-resonant.
- **Premise from the introduction.** Caregivers answer about 30-50 % of noncry vocalizations in natural interaction, far below the 80-100 % schedules of classic operant studies; partial reinforcement predicts a learned association that resists extinction and an **extinction burst** (a rise, then a fall) when the contingency is removed.
- **Vocal extinction burst.** Vocalizations per minute differ by episode, F(2,74) = 10.88, p < .001, ηp² = .23: up from interaction 1 to still face (p < .01), down from still face to interaction 2 (p < .05); 22 of 38 infants show that pattern. Within the still face, vocalizing rises to a peak at a median of 75 s (IQR 45 s; 37 of 38 infants show a peak) and then falls; above the last-15-s baseline (M = .79) at 0-15, 30-45, 45-60 and 60-75 s (Bonferroni-corrected).
- **Smiling does the opposite.** Smiles fall in the still face, F(2,72) = 11.78, p < .001, ηp² = .25, below baseline from 15 s to the end; no burst. Vocalizing and smiling are uncorrelated in every episode (ps > .42). The authors: the effect is "specific to the vocal channel" and reflects prior learning, not an immediate affective reaction.
- **Individual differences.** Learning Ratio (still face / interaction 1) M = 1.92, SD = 2.03; Interaction Ratio (still face / interaction 2) M = 1.68. LR correlates with phrases understood at 13 months, r = .47 (n = 26); IR with phrases .36 and words .37 (n = 30).
- **Interpretation.** "Vocalizing has acquired instrumental value"; the burst is "a transitory attempt to elicit social interaction". Predicted, not tested here: infants of depressed mothers (less contingent) should show a smaller burst.

## What it means for the engine
1. **The infant's output-shaping signal here is the partner's response as an outcome of the infant's own act.** In pass 7's terms the objective scores u (whether the partner answers), so it is Proposition 1(a): open. The extinction burst is the behavioural signature of exactly that route: a channel whose production was credited with partner responses is driven up when responses stop.
2. **The signature is channel-specific and dissociates from the face.** Vocalizing bursts while smiling falls. In the engine the analogue is an audit, not a mechanism: if any channel shows a still-face burst whose size depends on the agent's own **contingency history** (not on arousal alone), an approval route trained that channel. A yoked-history control (same exposure, non-contingent partner) separates the two.
3. **Natural partners are partial (30-50 %).** Partial schedules make the learned elicitation persistent; for an open learner the hazard is durable, not transient.

## Verdict
- **adopt (evaluation probe: the channel-specific extinction burst with a yoked-history control as the behavioural test of an approval route); reject as a learning signal for the engine.** N = 38, one experimenter, no contingent-vs-yoked manipulation within this study (the contingency history is inferred, not controlled).
