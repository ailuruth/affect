# Goldstein & Schwade 2008, "Social feedback to infants' babbling facilitates rapid phonological learning"

Psychological Science 19(5):515-523. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02117.x.

Source read: the published PDF from the authors' lab site, read in full (abstract, introduction, method, results, discussion) except the last paragraph of the discussion and the references.

## Claims
- Design: 60 infants aged 9.5 months (range 8 m 27 d to 10 m 14 d) with their mothers; 31 more excluded (14 fussing, 8 mothers not following instructions, 8 with under 5 vocalizations, 1 equipment). Free play in a lab playroom, infants wearing a hidden wireless microphone. Two 30-min sessions 24 h apart; the second was ABA: Baseline 1 (10 min), social response (10 min), Baseline 2 (10 min).
- Four groups of 15. Contingent mothers, instructed over headphones, answered each babble (within 2 s of its offset) by speaking while moving closer, smiling and touching, with either fully resonant vowels (contingent-resonance) or words with consonant-vowel alternation (contingent-CV), varying the actual phonemes (resonance mothers used on average 10.3 vowel types; CV mothers 17.7 consonant and 11.7 vowel types, 93.5% of words with CV alternation). Yoked controls heard each contingent mother's utterance track through headphones and repeated it with the same touching and smiling: same amount and form of stimulation, timing unrelated to their own infant's babbling.
- Infant vocalizations coded with Oller's infraphonological categories: quasi-resonant (closed throat, creaky or nasal), fully resonant (open throat, clear formants), marginal syllable (CV transition over 250 ms) and canonical syllable (CV transition under 250 ms with a fully resonant vowel). Coder reliability .94; maternal-response coding .95.
- Results:
  - Contingent-resonance infants produced more fully resonant vocalizations across periods, F(2,28) = 3.65, p = .039, eta_p^2 = .21; up from Baseline 1 to the response period, not back down in Baseline 2. Their CV proportion did not change.
  - Contingent-CV infants produced more CV-structured vocalizations, F(2,28) = 4.60, p = .019, eta_p^2 = .25; again retained in Baseline 2. Their resonance did not change.
  - Yoked infants changed neither (Fs < 2.75, ps > .10), though they vocalized more during the response period (yoked-resonance F(2,28) = 3.90, p = .032), which the authors attribute to arousal from non-contingent stimulation. Groups did not differ in fussing, looks or smiles to mother (ps > .25).
  - Not imitation: over 706 mother-infant utterance pairs, infants matched 29.3% of the mother's phonemes in the resonance group vs 26.6% in scrambled pairs (p = .12), and 15.0% vs 13.1% in the CV group (p = .39). Infants took up the pattern (resonant vowel, CV alternation), not the phonemes.
- Mechanism proposed: socially guided statistical learning. Variable exemplars make the abstract pattern salient; contingency places the adult's form right after the infant's own attempt, making the disparity between them salient; production is harder than perception, which is why exposure alone (yoked) does not suffice. Feedback-driven learning implicates basal ganglia (songbird anterior forebrain pathway analogy).
- Context from the introduction: most matching in mother-infant interaction is the mother imitating the infant, not the reverse (Jones 2007); 3-month-olds given contingent turn-taking produce less nasal, more resonant sounds than with random responses (Bloom et al. 1987); contingent responses also raise canonical syllables (Goldstein, King & West 2003).

## Evidence with numbers
n = 15 per group; effects within subject, medium size (eta_p^2 .21-.25); learning within 10 minutes, retained through the next 10.

## What it implies for an engine that learns its manner of speaking by living with people from an innate vocal body
- This is the clearest evidence for the long-term goal's route: an innate vocal body (here, an infant's repertoire of quasi-resonant to canonical forms) reshaped within minutes by the partner's contingent responses. The engine should learn its vocal manner the same way: its own vocalization, the operator's response close in time, and learning from the relation between the two. Exposure to the operator's speech without contingency should not shape the engine's voice; that gives a direct ablation (yoke the engine's training signal to another session's timing; vocal change should vanish while activity rises).
- What is learned is a pattern over the engine's own production space (open vs closed tract, fast vs slow transitions), not the partner's surface features. That supports learning in the coordinates of the engine's own vocal body (Principle 3) and argues against cloning the operator's voice or prosody.
- Contingency, not reward: the mothers' smiles and touch were identical in yoked and contingent groups, and only timing mattered. The learning signal is the temporal coupling between the engine's act and the operator's response, which is compatible with Principle 6 (no reward for smiles or attention) if the engine learns from the predictability of the response to its own act rather than from its valence. This also fits gergely-watson-1996-social-biofeedback.md (contingency detection as the learning signal).
- The 2-second response window and the CV transition threshold of 250 ms give concrete timescales: the engine's contingency detector should credit operator responses within about 2 s of its vocal act.
- Arousal raised vocal output without changing form: the engine's arousal should scale how much it vocalizes, separately from what it learns to produce.
