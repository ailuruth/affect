# Nader, Schafe & LeDoux 2000, "Fear memories require protein synthesis in the amygdala for reconsolidation after retrieval" (Nature 406:722-726; doi 10.1038/35021052), and Nader & Hardt 2009, "A single standard for memory: the case for reconsolidation" (Nature Reviews Neuroscience 10:224-234; doi 10.1038/nrn2590)

## Claims
- Nader 2000: "consolidated fear memories, when reactivated during retrieval, return to a labile state in which infusion of anisomycin shortly after memory reactivation produces amnesia on later tests, regardless of whether reactivation was performed 1 or 14 days after conditioning." The authors propose "that as a first approximation 'active' rather than 'new' memories be viewed as labile".
- Nader 2000: "Reconsolidation may be part of the neural mechanism through which constructed memories are stored for later constructions." The authors suggest that synaptic tagging "might account for lability and reconsolidation".
- Nader 2000 limits its own scope: "It is possible that not all memories require reconsolidation". The parameters may include "the extent of experience ..., the kind of learning system engaged and the motivational state of the subject at the time of learning and retrieval."
- Nader & Hardt 2009: "the same operational definitions and types of evidence underpin the deduction of both reconsolidation and consolidation". Reconsolidation is "a time-dependent associative process that is initiated when a consolidated memory is reactivated."
- Nader & Hardt 2009: during reconsolidation, as during consolidation, memory can be "enhanced", "impaired by amnesic treatments" or "interfered with by new learning". These treatments are ineffective once the process is complete.
- Nader & Hardt 2009: reconsolidation "is not universal". There are proposed "boundary conditions" (extinction, memory age, predictability of the reactivation stimulus, training intensity), all contested.
- Nader & Hardt 2009: stability is itself maintained. Consolidated LTM "crucially depends on constitutively active PKMζ", so "memory permanence requires continuous maintenance".

## Evidence
- Paradigm (2000): one tone-shock pairing (30 s, 5 kHz, 75 dB tone; 2.0 mA, 1 s shock). At 24 h, one CS presentation (reactivation), then bilateral intra-LBA anisomycin at 62.5 µg or 6.2 µg per 0.5 µl per side, or vehicle. Test 2 came 24 h later with 3 CSs.
- High dose produced dose-dependent amnesia at test 2: group effect F(2,18) = 12, P < 0.01. The low dose did not differ from vehicle; both differed from high dose (P < 0.01). n = 8, 7, 6.
- No reactivation (no CS), same infusion: memory normal (F < 1; n = 6 vehicle, 7 anisomycin).
- Infusion delayed 6 h after reactivation: no amnesia (group F(1,13) = 4, n.s.; n = 8, 7).
- Reactivation 14 days after training: amnesia still produced, F(1,9) = 20.6, P < 0.01 (n = 6, 5).
- PR-STM at 4 h was intact (F < 1); PR-LTM at 24 h was impaired, F(1,14) = 14, P < 0.01 (n = 8 per group). This is the same STM-intact, LTM-lost pattern that defines a consolidation block.
- The authors' argument: test 1 is an extinction trial, so the conventional view predicts that blocking protein synthesis would block extinction and strengthen fear. The opposite happened.
- 2009: reconsolidation was reported across species and tasks (Fig. 2): auditory and contextual fear in rat, object recognition and contextual fear in mouse, Hermissenda conditioning, human motor sequences. Interference in the human case appears "only when it was reactivated".
- L-LTP analogue (2009): anisomycin 2 h after L-LTP induction has no effect unless test pulses reactivate the pathway. Then potentiation is intact at first and decays.
- Molecular reversal (2009): blocking ERK after reactivation of place preference removes downstream transcription-factor activation. In C. elegans, post-reactivation treatment returns AMPA-receptor expression to naive levels.
- Destabilization needs machinery (2009): ubiquitin-proteasome degradation (Lee et al. 2008), amygdala NMDA receptors, and hippocampal CB1 receptors are "crucial for rendering consolidated memory labile".
- Parameters matter (2009): with strong 3-shock contextual training, reactivation for 3 or 5 min did not trigger reconsolidation, but 10 min did. Human episodic interference appeared only when interfering material was given in the original learning context.
- Differences from consolidation (2009): reconsolidation "is completed faster", dose-response curves differ, and one study shows a double dissociation of mechanisms.
- Alternatives that the 2009 paper rebuts: lesion or nonspecific effect, retrieval deficit, state-dependent learning, facilitated extinction, new learning. Each fails some datum, most often the intact PR-STM, reactivation by a reinforced CS, or post-reactivation enhancement. The retrieval-versus-storage question is left open for both consolidation and reconsolidation.

## Mechanism
There are no equations. The state model is Lewis's (2009 Fig. 1d): memories are "active" (labile) or "inactive" (stable). New learning and retrieval both make a memory active. An active memory restabilizes over time, within a window of up to about 6 h in LBA fear memory, and needs protein synthesis to do so. While active it can be strengthened, weakened or rewritten. Destabilization is a separate, gated step: proteasomal degradation, NMDA receptors. It needs suitable reactivation, depending on duration, context match and possibly prediction error, and may fail under boundary conditions. In synaptic terms (this is Redondo & Morris's account, not Nader's): retrieval re-tags engram synapses, and plasticity-related products restabilize them.

Our minimal formalization: a stored item k has parameters θ_k and a lability λ_k ∈ [0,1]. Retrieval sets λ_k ← g(reactivation) when a destabilization gate is open. While λ_k > 0, gradients on the current experience update θ_k with step ∝ λ_k, and λ_k decays to 0 over the restabilization window. If restabilization "resources" are withheld during that window, θ_k decays toward its prior.

## Implications for the engine (our inferences)
- **Updating happens through retrieval.** An old operator-specific expectation (how they respond when the agent withdraws) should become plastic only when it is reactivated by a matching situation. It should then be rewritten by what actually happens and restabilized. Memories that are not retrieved stay protected. This is a per-item plasticity gate that falls out of retrieval, and it limits forgetting of other partners and other contexts: only the reactivated subset is open.
- **Replay of the same kind.** Offline replay that reactivates a stored episode should be treated as a retrieval. It opens that episode's parameters to update by the current model (integration with today's learning) and then closes them. Replay therefore both consolidates and edits. An ablation follows: replay that reads out without opening (frozen targets) should keep old episodes verbatim but fail to integrate them with recent ones.
- **The destabilization gate should be derived, not set.** The 2009 boundary conditions suggest that reactivation must be long enough, or must carry mismatch, to destabilize. A natural Principle 10 derivation is that lability ∝ the prediction error at retrieval. This is our inference; Nader & Hardt only list "predictability of the reactivation stimulus" as a contested boundary condition. A well-predicted, routine interaction then leaves the relationship model unchanged, and a surprising one opens it.
- **Temperament as a slowly reconsolidated prior.** Temperament is retrieved every day, so under this account it is re-opened every day too. It stays stable only because each reopening is followed by a small update. A stable temperament needs a small step size while labile, or rare destabilization, for the global slow parameters. This connects to EWC/SI-style importance weighting, which belongs to other notes.
- **State at retrieval matters.** Nader 2000 lists "the motivational state of the subject at the time of learning and retrieval" as a parameter. For state-tagged replay, the internal state when an episode is replayed (not only when it was encoded) should set how much it is opened and how it is rewritten. This is speculative but testable: replay the same episode under two internal states and measure the divergence of what is stored.
- **Risk.** Reconsolidation lets every recall corrupt memory. An engine that replays heavily under a persistent negative state could rewrite neutral operator memories toward negative ones, a rumination analogue. That is a failure mode to monitor. It is also a candidate S6 ablation: replay under a clamped state.

## Verdict: adopt (retrieval-gated lability as a constraint on replay updates); watch (the boundary conditions)
- The phenomenon is well established across species and levels. The 2000 data are clean, with small n (5-8 per group) and large F values.
- For Principle 10, "lability on retrieval" can be stated as a constraint: per-item plasticity is non-zero only after reactivation and decays over a window. The gate, which we propose should be prediction error at reactivation, must come from the core.
- Watch: the boundary conditions are unsettled, and storage versus retrieval failure is unresolved. Do not build anything that depends on a specific boundary condition.
