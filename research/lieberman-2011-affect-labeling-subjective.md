# Subjective responses to emotional stimuli during labeling, reappraisal, and distraction (Lieberman, Inagaki, Tabibnia & Crockett)

Matthew D. Lieberman, Tristen K. Inagaki, Golnaz Tabibnia & Molly J. Crockett, *Emotion* 11(3):468–480, 2011, doi:10.1037/a0023503 (PMC3444304, NIH author manuscript).

## What was read
The full PMC manuscript: introduction (which reviews the fMRI affect-labeling studies, including Lieberman et al. 2007), all four studies with results, general discussion, applications and limitations. Figures not seen. This note stands in for Lieberman et al. 2007 (*Psych Sci*) and Torre & Lieberman 2018 (*Emotion Review*): neither could be reached (UCLA lab hosts and SAGE blocked). The 2007 fMRI result is used only as this paper and its own abstract state it: labeling lowers amygdala response, raises right VLPFC, and the two are inversely related.

## Claims and evidence
- **Task.** IAPS pictures for 5 s each. *Watch:* "look and let yourself respond naturally". *Label:* choose which of two words fits the picture (e.g. "crying" vs "bomb" under a picture of a person crying; both words negative for a negative picture). *Reappraise:* think about it so the emotion decreases. *Distract* (Study 3). After each picture, a 9-point distress (or pleasure) rating within 4.5 s. Separate "predictors" imagined the same trials and predicted their feelings; in Studies 2–4 some predictors had just done the task.
- **Labeling lowers felt distress, a little, within the 5-s trial.** Study 1 (N = 28): watch 6.64 vs label 6.25 (t = 2.96, p < .01), about 6% lower. Study 2 (N = 31): watch 6.03, label 5.81 (p < .05), reappraise 5.24. Study 3: labeling and distraction similar in size.
- **It attenuates positive affect too.** Study 4 (female sample, positive pictures): pleasure watch 6.34 vs label 6.02 (p < .05). "Affect labeling serves as an affect attenuator regardless of the initial valence of one's affective state, rather than an alleviator of negative affect." Proposed mechanism: a coarse right-VLPFC "braking" system that inhibits prepotent responses across domains.
- **Larger for stronger affect** in three of the four studies (extreme vs moderate pictures).
- **People predict the opposite.** Predictors expected labeling to *increase* distress (Study 1: predicted 6.94 vs watch 5.72; predicted benefit −1.22, actual +0.39). Those who had just experienced labeling lowering their distress still predicted it would raise it next time. Reappraisal and distraction were predicted to help, and did. So labeling is "incidental emotion regulation": it is done for other reasons ("updating a friend on what one has been up to") and regulates as a side effect.
- **Shared mechanism with reappraisal:** the per-person labeling benefit correlated with the reappraisal benefit (r = .43; .58 for extreme pictures). Right VLPFC appears in nearly every labeling study and 70% of intentional regulation studies; intentional regulation additionally recruits left VLPFC and pre-SMA (holding the goal).
- **Analytic, certainty-giving processing generally dampens affect in both directions** (Wilson et al.'s gift study; Lyubomirsky et al.: writing about positive events lowered life satisfaction, about negative ones raised it).

**Limits:** small samples; the "label" is a word for the picture's emotional content, chosen from two, not a free self-label of one's own feeling; ratings immediately after a 5-s picture, so nothing on duration; reappraisal effects partly demand.

## What it means for the designs
1. **Naming is a bounded, valence-symmetric brake, which is exactly the shape a Claude-to-state route needs.** The effect is a shrink toward neutral (negative less negative, positive less positive), small (about 4–6% of the rating), and larger when the state is stronger. As a rule for the agent: when the agent's own realised utterance names an affective state that matches the sign of its current deviation, each body variable involved contracts toward the agent's own set point by a small factor, Δb = −κ(b − b*), κ of order 0.05 per naming utterance. It is sign-safe by construction (it cannot cross neutral), it does not need Claude to know any value, and it is bounded by κ times the deviation.
2. **The match is computed on the body side from the words, not by Claude.** Claude writes "I've been a bit worried about you" because retrieval showed it worried-state snippets (P4); the body then hears its own sentence. Whether "worried" matches the current state can be checked inside the slow block with an innate lexical sign (the valence and arousal of the words as heard), so 𝒦 still never writes to S. A mismatched label (saying "I'm fine" while concerned) gets no contraction.
3. **It also damps the agent's positive states.** Revision 2 already lacks positive affect. If the agent names its own pleasure ("I'm really glad you're back"), labeling evidence predicts a slightly shorter-lived pleasure. That is realistic, but the satiable approach drive the critique asks for should not be routed through naming alone, or every expression of warmth will reduce warmth.
4. **The agent's comforting words should lower, not raise, its own concern.** The critique found that comforting acts read as sender distress. Predictors in this paper made the same mistake about themselves: they expected naming negative content to raise distress, and it lowered it. The appraisal of the agent's own utterances must not run its comforting words through the partner-word appraisal that maps "upsetting", "sad" to higher V; naming them should contract.
5. **The attenuation is fast** (within the 5-s trial, before the rating). Its duration comes from fan-2018 (minutes) and Ochsner et al. (up to 30–40 min for reappraisal), not from here.
6. **Labeling is incidental.** People do it while talking, not to regulate. That matches a Claude that writes words for the conversation and never aims to regulate the agent: the regulation follows from the agent hearing itself, and Claude's instruction needs no "regulate the agent" goal (which would require it to know the state).

## Verdict
**adopt (mechanism and magnitude):** affect labeling is a small, fast, valence-symmetric attenuation that scales with intensity and works without the person intending or believing it; it gives the safe form of a route from Claude's words to the agent's state, a contraction toward the agent's own set point triggered by its own naming utterance.
