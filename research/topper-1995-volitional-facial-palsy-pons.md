# Töpper, Kosinski & Mull 1995, "Volitional type of facial palsy associated with pontine ischaemia" (Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery, and Psychiatry 58(6):732-734, doi:10.1136/jnnp.58.6.732, PMC1073556)

Source read: the full text of the publisher PDF (a 3-page short report with two figures). Hopf, Müller-Forell & Hopf 1992 ("Localization of emotional and volitional facial paresis", Neurology 42:1918-1923), the main lesion series on the two facial palsies, was NOT read: it is paywalled and no copy was found. Secondary records disagree on its patient count (seven vs four), so it is not cited here for numbers.

## Claims
- Definitions (citing Monrad-Krohn 1924 and Hopf et al. 1992): in volitional facial palsy, weakness "is most pronounced during voluntary contraction whereas emotionally triggered contractions are preserved or at times even exaggerated on the paretic side"; emotional facial palsy is the reverse. Volitional palsy follows lesions of motor cortex or corticobulbar pathways; emotional palsy follows lesions of basal ganglia, hypothalamus or thalamus.
- The dissociation is not limited to the facial nerve: in the bilateral anterior opercular syndrome it appears in jaw (trigeminal), tongue and swallowing muscles.
- Case: a 57-year-old man with a pure motor stroke (left arm and leg 3/5) from a right upper-pons infarct (MRI at 14 days; the lesion spared the pontine tegmentum). Asked to bare his teeth, the left mouth corner drooped; when laughing, mouth movement was symmetric. Upper face relatively spared.
- Conclusion: "fibres conveying voluntary and emotional commands are still separated in the pons". Voluntary commands run in corticobulbar fibres (direct to the facial nucleus or via the pontine reticular formation). Emotional commands are proposed to come from amygdala and lateral hypothalamus via the medial forebrain bundle and dorsal longitudinal fasciculus to the lateral tegmentum, where interneurons project to the facial nucleus: polysynaptic, not direct.
- Laterality: emotional responses such as laughing and crying "involve stereotyped bilateral innervation"; but because most hemispheric lesions produce both palsies, the authors conclude that both kinds of input follow "a common pattern with bilateral projections to the muscles of the upper two thirds of the face and unilateral projection to the lower third".

## Evidence
One case (n = 1), with MRI localisation and photographs. No quantitative facial measurement.

## What it implies for the engine
- The two routes stay separate down to the brainstem and converge only at or near the facial motor nucleus. For the engine this argues for two separately ablatable inputs into one muscle layer, not one controller with two modes. Ablating the deliberate input should leave laughter-driven movement symmetric and intact, and vice versa.
- The emotional route is polysynaptic through brainstem interneurons (pattern-generating), which fits Gothard's "central pattern generators" and the stereotyped, bilateral form of laughing and crying. The engine's affective route could drive pattern generators (laugh, cry, startle) that recruit muscle synergies, while the deliberate route can address individual muscles.
- Release: emotional movement can be exaggerated on the side where voluntary control is lost, consistent with the cortical route normally inhibiting the affective one (see matsumoto-lee-1993-volition-facial-neuropsychology.md).
- Region prior: lower face mostly contralateral and voluntary-dominated; upper two thirds bilateral.
