# Mirror-touch synaesthesia changes representations of self-identity (Maister, Banissy & Tsakiris)

Citation: L. Maister, M. J. Banissy, M. Tsakiris. "Mirror-touch synaesthesia changes representations of self-identity." *Neuropsychologia*, April 2013 issue (PMID 23391559). PMC3750874 (Europe PMC funders' manuscript).

Source read: the PMC manuscript, HTML text-extracted. Read in full: abstract, introduction, methods (participants, stimulus construction, both experiments), all results including Table 1 (questionnaire medians), discussion and footnote.

## The study
- **Enfacement illusion (background).** Seeing an unfamiliar face touched in synchrony with touch on one's own face makes people accept morphs with more of the other's face as "self". Tajadura-Jiménez et al. (2012, as reported) showed the change is in the *self*-face representation, not the other-face one, and only for other → self morph direction.
- **Design.** Six mirror-touch synaesthetes (MTS; 4.3% of the authors' web-questionnaire respondents; all female) and 10 matched controls watched 2-min videos of a stranger's cheek stroked every 3 s (TOUCH) or not (NO-TOUCH), *without* being touched themselves. Self-recognition was measured on morphs at the participant's point of subjective equality (PSE) ±4% and ±8%, before and after. A second control group (n = 10) received the standard synchronous enfacement (felt + seen touch).

## Results with numbers
- Four-way interaction time × image × condition × group, F(1.78, 24.91) = 3.74, p = .042; no group difference before the videos.
- **MTS, PSE images:** self-response change +0.267 (s.d. 0.260) after TOUCH vs +0.067 (0.178) after NO-TOUCH; Wilcoxon z = −2.06, p = .039. No change at ±4–8% images. Controls: no effect of seen touch.
- **Equivalence:** the MTS seen-touch effect did not differ from the standard felt+seen enfacement effect in controls (U = 28.0, p = .827); questionnaire intensity also equivalent (U = 25.0, p = .587).
- **Direction:** MTS endorsed "my own face began to resemble the other person's face" (median 1.0 vs −2.5 in controls, p = .005) more than the reverse item (p = .098, n.s.): the other is taken into the self, not the self projected onto the other.
- Attention and imagery accounts are argued against by screening: MTS felt touch only for touch on humans, not objects, dummies, or an approaching hand.

## What it means for the design
1. **Self-representation is maintained by multisensory contingency, and it can be overwritten by it.** The self-face model is updated whenever seen and felt events coincide. For controls this needs felt touch; for MTS the vicarious activation itself supplies the "felt" side, so seeing alone updates the self.
2. **That is a precise picture of the copy attractor in learning, not in dynamics.** The danger is not that the agent's face follows the partner's in the moment; it is that the agent's *own-body model* (the model used to read the partner "through its own body") learns from coincidences between its own felt state and the partner's seen face, and absorbs the partner. A partner who mirrors the agent synchronously supplies exactly the enfacement condition.
3. **Design rule (derived):** the own-body model may learn only from pairs whose "felt" side is the agent's own efference and interoception, never from a vicarious activation of the shared map. In the engine this means the inverse model's training targets must exclude any signal that the partner's face can drive, including the agent's own fast reflexive mimicry.
4. **Asymmetry.** The effect is other → self only. A model in which self–other blur is symmetric is wrong for this case.

## Verdict
- **adopt (as a failure mode to design against).** Small samples (n = 6 MTS) and a borderline Experiment 2 test (p = .052), but the direction and the equivalence to enfacement give a concrete mechanism by which the self-model absorbs the other: learning from seen–felt coincidence when the felt side is vicarious.
