# Hess & Fischer 2013, "Emotional Mimicry as Social Regulation" (Personality and Social Psychology Review 17(2):142-157, doi 10.1177/1088868312472607)

Source read: full article PDF (author-hosted copy, ursulakhess.com/resources/HF13.pdf), all sections, including Table 1 and Figure 1. This is a theoretical review; the studies it cites are described here as Hess & Fischer describe them, not as I read them.

## Claims
- Against the "Matched Motor Hypothesis" (perceiving a display automatically entrains the same display), the authors propose the Emotion Mimicry in Context view: people mimic their interpretation of an emotional intention, and only when there is at least a minimal affiliative stance toward the expresser.
- Of 40 published mimicry studies (Table 1), most test only anger and happiness. Evidence for discrete-emotion mimicry beyond these is thin: fear mimicry with the fear-specific muscle (frontalis) in only one of eight studies (Lundqvist 1995); disgust (levator) in one of three; surprise (frontalis) in two. Sadness and fear "mimicry" is mostly indexed by corrugator, which also indexes anger. Their conclusion: "the extant evidence supports only valence-based mimicry".
- Two kinds of facial response to an emotional display (Figure 1): with affiliative intent, a congruent display (mimicry, which implies shared understanding of the event); without it, a reactive emotion that can be congruent or incongruent. A frown to an angry face may be reactive (irritation), not mimicry; "mere congruency is not a sufficient indicator that mimicry ... has occurred".
- Context factors reviewed: attitude toward the target (negative attitude reduces mimicry); competition vs cooperation (reduced or absent smile mimicry under competition, and "divergent" displays such as smiling at a competitor's pain); group membership (in-group mimicked more; contempt AU10 to out-group fear, citing Van der Schalk et al. 2011); gaze direction (anger averted toward a third party is shared and mimicked more than anger directed at the observer); and the expression itself (smiles are affiliative and cheap to return; anger and disgust signal non-affiliation and are rarely mimicked unless directed at a common foe).
- Field count they cite (Hinsz & Tomhave 1991): 53% of smiles but only 7% of frowns evoked a matching expression in strangers in public places.
- Mimicry is "not necessary for recognition" of prototypical expressions; it may help with subtle ones.
- Mimicry can stay automatic while depending on affiliation: "People do not need to think about not smiling at the smile of their enemy."

## Evidence
Narrative review, no new data and no meta-analytic effect sizes. Table 1 lists the 40 studies with emotions and muscle sites. The context section rests mainly on Bourgeois & Hess 2008, Likowski et al. 2008, Weyers et al. 2009, Lanzetta & Englis 1989, Fischer, Becker & Veenstra 2012, Häfner & IJzerman 2011 (as cited), Van der Schalk et al. 2011 (as cited) and Herrera, Bourgeois, Cheung & Hess 1998 (conference paper, as cited).

## What it implies for the design
- The response to the partner is not one gain on a copy operator. The paper's model is a switch between two routes (share the other's emotion vs react to it) gated by an inferred relation and by what the signal means for the observer. Under the reactive route the sign can flip (smile at a rival's pain).
- The literature's own units are valence-level, not per-emotion templates. For a body-first agent (Principle 3), this argues for a learned, relation-conditioned map from the partner's continuous facial features to the agent's own, whose cheap default is valence-congruent and whose departures (gating, sign flips) are learned, rather than a hard-wired AU-to-AU copy.
- Who the display is aimed at (gaze, head orientation) is a first-class input: the same anger face should drive different responses when directed at the agent vs at a third party.
- Ablation prediction this suggests: removing the relation-conditioning (one global gain) should erase sign flips and the expression-specific gating while leaving smile return roughly intact, because smile return is the case the paper says is least context-dependent.

## Verdict: adopt (as the theoretical frame)
The central reference for "mimicry changes shape with relationship, not only amount". It is a review; check every empirical claim against the primary paper before using its numbers.
