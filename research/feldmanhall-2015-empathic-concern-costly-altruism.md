# Empathic concern drives costly altruism (FeldmanHall, Dalgleish, Evans & Mobbs)

Oriel FeldmanHall, Tim Dalgleish, Davy Evans & Dean Mobbs, *NeuroImage* 105:347–356, 2015, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2014.10.043 (PMC4275572, CC BY 3.0).

## What was read
The full PMC text (JATS XML): abstract, introduction, methods, all behavioural and imaging results, discussion, and figure captions. The imaging tables were read only as far as the text reports them. **Batson's empathy–altruism papers were not read.** Batson et al. 1981 (*JPSP* 40:290) and Batson 1991 could not be reached: typeset.io, sdimakis.github.io and users.ssc.wisc.edu are refused by this session's network. This paper is used because it is open, tests the same contrast (empathic concern vs personal distress) under a real cost, and reports the trial-by-trial role of the partner's visible pain. Nothing below is attributed to Batson beyond what this paper states.

## Claims
- Trait empathic concern, not trait personal distress, predicts costly helping.
- Situational distress (felt after watching the partner's reaction to one's own last choice) also predicts the next choice.
- Approach and caregiving regions (VTA, caudate, sgACC) track the outcome of costly helping in people high in concern. Amygdala and midbrain track personal distress and selfish choice.

## Method
- **Pain vs Gain task.** 20 trials. On each, the Decider chooses how much of £1 to keep. Keeping more gives a stronger shock to a Receiver the Decider has met.
- **After each choice the Decider watches a 4-s video of the Receiver's face and body.** It is pre-recorded and graded so that stronger shocks show distinctly more distress. The Decider then rates their own distress on a 13-point scale.
- Subjects "could leave the experiment at any point, without compromising their hourly endowment": the escape option that the empathy–altruism hypothesis needs.
- n = 17 after excluding 2 doubters. Believability was unrelated to behaviour (r = −0.21, p = 0.42).

## Evidence with numbers
- Money kept: £12.71 ± 4.2 of £20 (range £3.80–£19.00). Being watched was unrelated to giving (r = −0.05), so there is no sign of reputation management.
- Trait empathic concern (IRI) × money given up: r = 0.53, p = 0.02, and still r = 0.53 with personal distress partialled out.
- Trait personal distress × money given up: r = 0.16, p = 0.53. The two IRI scales were uncorrelated (r = −0.03).
- Lagged regression: situational distress after trial t predicts a lower shock on t + 1 (β = −0.26, SE 0.06, p < 0.001). Trait concern also predicts it (β = −0.48, SE 0.24, p < 0.05). No interaction (β = 0.007, p = 0.86).
- Only one subject chose "no shock" more than once (6 of 20 trials).
- Imaging (p < 0.001 uncorrected and SVC):
  - watching the outcome of more prosocial choices → OFC and dlPFC;
  - watching more selfish outcomes → dACC;
  - with higher trait concern, the outcome of giving → VTA, sgACC/NAcc and caudate, robust to partialling out distress;
  - trait distress and selfish choice → right amygdala and midbrain.

## Limits
- n = 17. Trait concern is a questionnaire. The videos are pre-recorded, so the partner's face is a deterministic, honest function of the shock.
- The authors say both concern and situational distress contribute: "a complex interplay of more than one emotional state."
- The escape option's uptake is not reported.

## Verdict
**adopt (as evidence on the human form of the motive, with the caveat it exposes).** Helping at a cost goes with other-oriented concern, not with a disposition to escape one's own distress. That supports care as a drive over the partner's *need*, not as relief of the agent's own aversive load (V). But the trial-by-trial learning signal in people *is* the partner's visible reaction: seeing their pain lowers the next shock.

## What it means for the designs
1. **Concern, not distress, should be what care relieves.** The ledger design has both: C (concern, 𝒟 at b̂_p) and V (aversive load from being the target of the partner's aversive state). Personal distress is the V-like motive, and it predicts nothing here. Its circuit is the withdrawal one. If V can be lowered by acts, the cheapest act is escape or placation (ending the topic, getting a smile). So **no act-credit may flow through V at all.** Let V raise arousal and the urge to withdraw (an honest cost), and let only C's relief credit helping.
2. **Humans do learn from the partner's face, but that face is honest.** The β = −0.26 lagged effect means people adjust help using the partner's displayed pain. That is safe here because the face was a fixed, graded function of the shock: there was no way to change the face without changing the harm. In a real dyad the face *can* be moved without the need: politeness smiles, contagion from the agent's own smile, a partner masking for the agent's sake. That is why Principle 6 forbids the face as a target even though people use it. The design should take the face as *response* evidence (when to care) and get *credit* (did it help) from channels where display and need come apart less easily.
3. **The approach circuit tracks outcomes of one's own costly help, so care should feel good when it works.** The VTA and caudate response in high-concern people when they see the outcome of giving is the "empathic joy" side. This bears on the critic's item (c), satiable positive drives. Relief of C when inferred need falls *for reasons the agent's act caused through need* (not through display) is a positive event, so the agent has positive affect from successful care without a smile reward.
4. **Escape-ease as a test.** The design can import the logic of the escape paradigm as a falsifier. Offer the agent an easy exit (end the topic, change subject) and a costly help act. If care is concern-based, help rate should not fall when exit is made easy. If it is distress-based (V-driven), help rate falls. Run it at equal R in the H-tests.
