# Murray & Trevarthen 1985/1986, the double-video live vs replay paradigm (with Rochat, Neisser & Marian 1998 and later replications)

- Murray, L. & Trevarthen, C. (1985). Emotional regulation of interactions between two-month-olds and their mothers. In T. M. Field & N. A. Fox (Eds.), Social perception in infants (pp. 177-197; Nadel et al. cite pp. 101-125). Norwood, NJ: Ablex.
- Murray, L. & Trevarthen, C. (1986). The infant's role in mother-infant communications. Journal of Child Language 13(1):15-29, doi:10.1017/S0305000900000271.
- Rochat, P., Neisser, U. & Marian, V. (1998). Are young infants sensitive to interpersonal contingency? Infant Behavior and Development 21(2):355-366, doi:10.1016/S0163-6383(98)90012-1.

## The paradigm
Mother and infant sit in separate rooms. Each sees a full-face, life-size image of the other, with cameras behind one-way mirrors so eye contact is possible (Henning & Striano describe a build "similar to that used by Murray and Trevarthen"). Live: real-time transmission. Replay: the partner's image is replaced by a recording of that same partner from an earlier live stretch of the session. Form, content and affect are kept; responsiveness to the viewer's current behaviour is removed.

## Claims and evidence
- Infant side (1985, as described by Striano et al. 2005): "Infants between 6 and 12 weeks of age responded with more positive behavior such as gazing and smiling in the live compared to the replay period." Nadel et al. 1999 add that M&T infants "became upset during the replay". Sample size and statistics not read.
- Mother side (1986 abstract): mothers saw either live video of their infant or "the same sequence replayed some minutes later. The mother's babytalk differed consistently between live and replay sequences. Since the infants' acts were identical in form in the two conditions, the only difference being the infant's potential responsiveness to the mother, these results are evidence for the infant's active role." So the adult detects whether the infant is contingent on her, not only the reverse.
- Objections and failures:
  - The fatigue/order objection: the original order was live then replay, so infants may simply get fussier over time (Nadel et al. 1999 abstract).
  - Rochat, Neisser & Marian 1998 (read; see its note): M&T tested only 4 infants (18 sessions), always Live before Tape, with Live run until an active interchange was reached. With order counterbalanced and fixed 60-s intervals (N = 10, 2-3 months) gaze was 75.8% Live vs 75.3% Tape (p = .94), and smiling differed in the opposite direction (0.59% vs 3.33%, p < .03). After a still face (N = 21, 8-25 weeks) the still face cut gaze (F(2,34) = 9.94, p = .0004), but Live and Tape still did not differ. M&T's own means, as reproduced there: gaze 89.3 vs 63.4%, frowning 0.5 vs 25.4%.
  - Hains & Muir 1996: 5-month-olds did not discriminate live from replay with their mother but did with a stranger (via Striano et al. 2005).
- Replications and refinements:
  - Nadel et al. 1999: a live–replay–live replication at 2 months, n = 10 (abstract).
  - Bigelow & DeCoste 2003 (abstract): 2-, 4- and 6-month-olds, mothers and strangers, three orders of two contingent episodes and one replay. Infants were "more visually attentive to contingent interactions than to the noncontingent replay when contingent interactions occurred prior to the replay", and replay-level attention carried over into later contingent episodes. Effects held at 4 and 6 months (attention and smiling); at 2 months only effect sizes for attention suggested a beginning.
  - Soussignan et al. 2006 (abstract): 2-month-olds, three seamless 30-s episodes vs a 90-s all-live control. Grimacing rose only in the replay period relative to control. Infants who smiled in the first live episode reacted most negatively to replay. Late declines in gaze in the control were attributed to fatigue.
  - Stormark & Braarud 2006 (abstract): 2- to 4-month-olds. Replay was given either to the infant or to the mother. Low-negative-affect infants looked more at mother in live than in replay; high-negative-affect infants did not.
  - Henning & Striano 2004 (read): only timing was manipulated, with live vs a 1-s delay of the partner's image and voice in 2-min seamless counterbalanced segments. Infants (3 months n = 34; 6 months n = 29) gazed more at the live mother: Live 57.9% vs Delay 41.9% of time; F(1, 34.3) = 5.08, p = .031 at 3 months; F(1, 27.0) = 4.38, p = .046 at 6 months; GLMMs controlling for maternal behaviour. Infant smiling and vocalizing did not differ (ps > .16). Mothers (n = 33 at 3 months, 26 at 6 months) did not change behaviour when their infant was delayed by 1 s (ps > .08).

## Mechanism
None is formalised. The interpretation shared across these papers is that from about 2-3 months infants form an expectancy that the partner's behaviour depends on their own, and detect its absence even when the partner's form and affect are unchanged. Two dissociations matter:
1. Attention vs affect. A 1-s delay lowered gaze but not smiling; replay lowered both at 2 months in some labs. Contingency timing seems to drive attention more than affect, unless the break is large.
2. Asymmetric sensitivity. Mothers did not detect a 1-s infant delay (Henning & Striano) but did change their speech with a minutes-old replay (M&T 1986). The adult's window for "the other responds to me" is wider or more content-based.

## What it implies for an engine learning a partner model and its self–other face map from live contingency
- The double video is the design pattern for breaking the confound the critique found. It removes exactly one causal arrow while keeping all marginal statistics: the partner's form, rate and affect stay the same, but the partner's dependence on the viewer is cut. The engine can run both directions internally, with no new data:
  - Partner-replay (the infant condition): feed the engine a recorded stretch of the same partner. A correct partner model's "responds to me" estimate (lagged dependence of partner on the agent's exogenous acts) should collapse toward zero. An estimator that regresses the partner on the agent's own state trace will NOT collapse, because the agent still follows the replayed partner and the following is still being read as the partner's response (critique C13). This makes replay an automatic test of the estimator.
  - Self-replay (the 1986 mother condition): show the partner a replay of the agent's earlier face while the agent keeps sensing the live partner. Any remaining "partner responds to me" estimate measures the confound directly, because the partner cannot be responding to what it no longer sees. This needs the operator's consent, since it deceives the person, so use it as an offline evaluation with consenting testers, not in daily life.
- Use a delay probe as a finer instrument. A 1-s delay of the partner stream keeps everything except the timing of the relation. If the agent's learned response lag to the partner is real, the estimate should shift by 1 s. If it is an artefact of autocorrelation, it will barely move.
- ABA with a second live period, counterbalanced order, and a no-switch control are required. Otherwise drift in the agent's own state (or fatigue in the person) mimics a contingency effect.
- The attention/affect dissociation suggests that a broken contingency should first lower the engine's orienting or attention to the partner, and only with larger or longer breaks change its affect. That is a testable prediction for the engine's behaviour.
- Infants show these effects only from about 2-4 months, and only when engaged. An engine needs a period of accumulated interaction before its contingency expectancy is meaningful, and its contingency estimate should not be trusted while its own state is far from baseline (high distress).

## Verdict: adopt the paradigm as an evaluation and identification probe; watch the infant findings (primary texts unread, 2-month results contested)
The live–replay–live and 1-s delay manipulations are the right tools to test that a learned partner model encodes the partner's dependence on the agent rather than the agent's following. Do not cite M&T 1985 results beyond what is stated above until the chapter is read; Rochat et al. 1998 is read and argues the 1985 effect may be an order-and-peak artefact.
