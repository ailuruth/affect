# Messinger & Fogel 2007, "The interactive development of social smiling" (in R. V. Kail, ed., Advances in Child Development and Behavior 35:327-366, Elsevier), with Messinger, Dondi, Nelson-Goens, Beghi, Fogel & Simion 2002, "How sleeping neonates smile" (Developmental Science 5(1):48-54)

Source read: the chapter's proof PDF from the first author's teaching site, sections I-V read in full (pp. 327-352: theory, neurophysiology, blind infants, neonatal smiling, emergence of social smiling, smile types, smiles as continuous indices, interactive development, smile-gaze coordination). Sections VI-VIII (referential smiling, atypical development, outcomes, conclusions) were NOT read. The 2002 neonatal report: abstract and results figures skimmed, not read in full. Messinger, Fogel & Dickson 2001 ("All smiles are positive, but some smiles are more positive than others", Developmental Psychology 37:642-653) was NOT read in the original; its findings are taken from the chapter's account of it.

## Claims
- Neural route: "Voluntary or deliberate smiling (being asked to produce a smile) is thought to originate in the cortical motor strip and travels to the nucleus via pyramidal tracts (Rinn, 1984). However, most social smiling in infancy is spontaneous", via an extrapyramidal pathway (basal ganglia, amygdala). Afferent feedback from the smiling face (trigeminal) is a possible basis for facial feedback (p. 330). Meta-analyses find no consistent neural signature of "joy", which they read as weak support for a dedicated affect program.
- Blind infants: social smiles appear (to a familiar voice), become more frequent from 4 to 12 months, but are "less frequent and briefer"; deficits appear after 2-3 years. So "the emergence of social smiling is experience expectant, occurring even in the absence of visual feedback. The interactive intensification of smiling depends, however, on visual engagement" (pp. 332-333).
- Neonatal smiles: in REM sleep and drowsiness, "an average rate of one smile per 5 min"; a microcephalic case suggests subcortical origin; premature infants smile more and spontaneous smiling declines with age, suggesting "developing cortical inhibition" (p. 333). In the 2002 study (25 sleeping full-term neonates, 6 min each, Baby FACS): "One-half of the neonates showed bilateral Duchenne smiles", one-quarter at "a mature level of intensity", while "open-mouth bilateral smiles occurred in less than one-tenth". Stronger lip-corner raises went with stronger cheek raises: "an early, apparently neuromuscular, synergy" (p. 334).
- Sequence of elicitors (Wolff 1987): no reliable smiling to stimuli in week 1; to the human voice by the end of week 2 in half the infants; to human and non-human sounds by week 4; voice plus face best at 5 weeks; by 8 weeks "infants smiled only after making visual contact with the mother's face" (p. 334). Onset of social smiling tracks postconceptional age, not postnatal weeks (p. 335).
- Early social smiling grows out of attention: it is "frequently preceded by a 3- to 20-s period of brow knitting and visual fixation on the mother's face" after which the brows relax and the smile appears (Oster 1978; Lavelli & Fogel 2005) (p. 336).
- Dyad-specific contingency by 2 months: "2-month-olds smile less at a stranger who is either more or less contingently responsive than the infant's mother (Bigelow & Rochat, 2006)" (p. 337).
- Smile types: simple, Duchenne (eye constriction, AU6), play (mouth opening, AU26/27), and "duplay" (both). Mean amplitude rises in that order. Eye constriction is "recruited" as amplitude rises, a muscular synergy. Mouth opening indexes arousal for both smiles and cry-faces; eye constriction indexes intensity for both (the cry-face with strong eye constriction is read as distress rather than anger). Mapped onto a valence-arousal circumplex, with extreme arousal erasing valence (pp. 337-342).
- Continuous, not categorical: "a single infant or mother smile can ebb and flow in amplitude over periods of 20-30 s", and within a smile eye constriction tracks amplitude, so a single smile can move in and out of being "Duchenne"; "It seems unlikely that some of those periods are emotional while others are not." Hence "all smiles are positive, but some smiles are more positive than others" (pp. 343-346).
- Infants and mothers do not copy each other's smile form: infant smiles couple mouth opening to amplitude, mothers do not (they open the mouth to talk and exaggerate). "Infants and mothers are not precisely imitating the form of each other's smiles" (p. 350).
- Contingency structure (pp. 347-348): between 2 and 5 months, maternal displays are "typically necessary" to elicit infant smiles but "not sufficient"; an infant smile "is typically sufficient to elicit a mother smile" but not necessary. Mothers respond to infant smile onsets "within 2 s" and rarely end mutual smiling first. "Thus the infant's prototypical experience of smiling is smiling with another." Infants smile in about 20% of face-to-face time. Infant-initiated smiles become pronounced at 6-9 months.
- Development of duplay smiles: from 1 to 6 months, they become increasingly concentrated in moments when the infant gazes at the mother and she smiles, while simple smiles stay non-specific (Messinger et al. 2001, Figure 6).
- Regulation: 3-month-olds begin and end smiles within a gaze at the parent; by 6 months they gaze away during intense smiles, regulating their own arousal (Yale et al. 2003) (pp. 351-352). Smiles co-occur with raised heart rate and hand-mouthing.
- Recognition of a smile-voice match at 3.5 months works only for the mother's face, not a stranger's (Kahana-Kalman & Walker-Andrews 2001).

## Evidence
Numbers above as reported; the 2002 neonatal study: n = 25, 52% with bilateral Duchenne smiles, 24% at mature intensity, open-mouth bilateral smiles in under 10% (2/25 vs 21/25 for a related contrast, p < 0.01, in the results).

## What it implies for the engine
- The innate face is a set of muscle synergies, not emotion templates: at birth, lip-corner pull and cheek raise are already coupled (Duchenne form in sleep), with no social trigger. The engine's innate body should hold synergies of this kind (AU12 recruiting AU6 with intensity; mouth opening with arousal) and let a single scalar-like drive produce a graded, continuous expression.
- Smiles are graded and continuous; a classifier of "smile types" would cut a continuum. This supports Principle 1 and suggests reading the engine's own face and the operator's as continuous AU intensities, with eye constriction and mouth opening as intensity and arousal channels.
- What becomes social is the trigger and its timing, learned over the first two months: voice, then face, then mutual gaze; and dyad-specific contingency levels by 2 months. The engine's smile reflex should be innate, its coupling to the operator's face and gaze learned.
- The contingency asymmetry (mother's smile necessary but not sufficient; infant's smile sufficient but not necessary) is a measurable dyadic signature. It can be computed on engine-operator data with the sufficiency/necessity indices in gergely-watson-1996-social-biofeedback.md.
- Arousal regulation by gaze aversion during intense smiles appears around 6 months: an innate-body candidate for regulating the engine's own arousal during high coupling, and a behaviour that should emerge rather than be scripted.
- Blind infants show the innate floor (smiles occur) and the learned ceiling (interactive intensification needs visual feedback).
