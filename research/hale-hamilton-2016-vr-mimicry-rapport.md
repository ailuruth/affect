# Testing the relationship between mimicry, trust and rapport in virtual reality conversations (Hale & Hamilton, Scientific Reports 2016)

Hale, J., Hamilton, A. F. de C. "Testing the relationship between mimicry, trust and rapport in virtual reality conversations." Scientific Reports 6:35295, 2016. doi:10.1038/srep35295. Open access, UCL Discovery PDF.

## What was read
- Full main text: introduction, Study 1 (method, results, discussion), Study 2 (pre-registered; method, results, discussion), general discussion, conclusions, methods. I did not read the supplementary tables (S3, S4) the text refers to for full Bayes factors.

## Claims
- "We conclude that being mimicked does not always increase rapport or trust".
- Study 1: avatars mimicked head and torso at a 1 s or 3 s delay during a photo-description conversation. "Rapport was higher towards mimicking avatars, with no effect of timing."
- Study 2 (pre-registered, 40 participants of European or East Asian background, avatars of each appearance): "We found no effects of mimicry on rapport or trust ratings or implicit trust behaviour in a novel maze task, and no effects of group status or interactions."

## Evidence
- Detection depends on delay. Study 1: 11 of 36 (30.5%) detected mimicry at 1 s, 1 of 26 (3.8%) at 3 s; χ²(1) = 6.9, p < 0.01. Study 2: 8 of 48 (16.6%) detected it at 3 s. All detectors were excluded before analysis.
- Study 1 rapport: main effect of mimicry p = 0.02, but "the Bayes factor for this effect was BF01 = 0.34, which is only weak evidence." Split by delay, the effect was significant at 3 s (t(24) = 2.15, p = 0.04, d = 0.49) and not at 1 s (BF01 = 2.01, favouring the null).
- Study 2: "our data favour a null main effect of mimicry on ratings of liking, rapport and trust, as well as trust behaviour in the virtual maze (all BF01 > 1.6)"; all mimicry × group interactions BF01 > 30.
- Co-presence 4.45 (Study 1) and 4.75 (Study 2) on a 1-7 scale, which the authors call comparable to other VR studies.
- The authors' interpretation includes the possibility that "'positive' mimicry effects may actually be driven by negative reactions to an absence of mimicry".

## Failures documented
- **Short-lag copying is noticed.** Mimicry at 1 s was detected eight times as often as at 3 s (30.5% vs 3.8%), and closer temporal coupling did not buy more rapport.
- **The rapport benefit of avatar mimicry did not replicate** under pre-registration (Study 2 null, Bayes factors favouring no effect).
- Implication drawn by the authors: the response to being mimicked may track general contingency or predictability rather than copying as such. They say the present studies cannot settle this.
