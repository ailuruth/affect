# Murray, De Pascalis, Bozicevic, Hawkins, Sclafani & Ferrari 2016, "The functional architecture of mother-infant communication, and the development of infant social expressiveness in the first two months" (Scientific Reports 6:39019, doi:10.1038/srep39019, PMC5155249)

Source read: the full text of the publisher PDF (results, discussion, methods). The Supplementary Methods (coding scheme) and supplementary tables were NOT read. Note that Murray and Ferrari are co-authors of meltzoff-2018-reexamination-oostenbroek.md.

## Claims
- Two accounts compared. Contingency account (Watson): parental contingency, "regardless of its form", shapes infant social behaviour (smiling and cooing rise even with non-social contingent feedback). Functional architecture account: infants and parents are prepared to respond in specific ways to specific forms of the partner's behaviour, notably "mirroring" and "marking" (highlighting with attention-attracting cues such as eyebrow flashes).
- Result 1: infant social expressiveness (smiles, vocalisations, "pre-speech" mouth movements) rose steeply from 1 to 9 weeks, "particularly after 3 weeks" (Age chi^2(4) = 679.8, R^2 = .747), and formed one cluster distinct from non-social mouth movements, which declined from 3 to 9 weeks.
- Result 2: contingency was low. Responses to the infant were only 9-15% of maternal behaviours; mothers responded to 20-33% of infant behaviours (27.6% overall), within 2 s.
- Result 3: responses were form-specific. Of mothers' mirroring and positive marking (marking with a smile), 60.1% and 63.7% went to infant social behaviour; negative responses went mainly to infant negative affect (46.6%); neutral marking (no smile) mainly to biological events such as sneezes (44.3%). Mirroring followed 15.5% of infant social expressions vs under 8% of other behaviours.
- Result 4: overall contingency did not predict development. "Levels of maternal contingency, even when considered specifically in relation to infant social behaviours, were unrelated to future infant social development." Within a session, any response shortened the interval to the next infant social act (6.86 vs 8.81 s), but only positive marking did so specifically, and controlling for it removed the contingency effect.
- Result 5: form predicted development (weeks 5-9, time t to t+2 weeks, controlling for prior rate). More maternal mirroring of infant social behaviour (but not of non-social behaviour) predicted more infant social behaviour two weeks later (chi^2(1) = 11.6, p < .001, R^2 = .12). Positive marking also predicted increases, with standardised coefficients about half as large, whether it followed social or non-social behaviour. Neutral marking and negative responses predicted relative decreases.
- Interpretation: because mirroring helped only when it mirrored social behaviour, its effect is not generic motivation; "maternal mirroring enhanced infant control over the production of mirrored social expressions". Only 15% of infant social expressions were mirrored, yet this had "a notable impact" two weeks later. Infants "capitalize on a limited corpus" of specific responses. "This is not to say that contingent associations are irrelevant - parental responses that fostered the development of social expressiveness were, by definition, contingent. Rather, our data show that the form of parental response is critical."
- Infants may expect responses within a range of intensity, valence and form; violations (e.g., "exaggerated maternal laughter in response to infant pre-speech") may disrupt engagement.

## Evidence
n = 20 dyads; home visits at 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 weeks; 3 minutes of face-to-face interaction per visit, event-coded on a 1-s base; maternal contingent response = within 2 s; multilevel Poisson and binomial models. Small sample and short sessions (15 minutes per dyad in total).

## What it implies for the engine
- Learning by living from very sparse data is what infants actually do: about a quarter of their acts get any response, and 15% of social expressions get mirrored, yet that sparse mirroring drives development over two weeks. This is evidence that the brief's "little real data, lean on innate structure" constraint is the human situation too, provided the learning rule weights the right kind of response.
- Generic contingency (any response within 2 s) is not the signal; the form of the response matters, specifically matching (mirroring) and positive marking. This sharpens the Gergely & Watson and Heyes accounts: the engine's update for "expressing" should be driven by the partner's matching or smiling response to that expression, not by response probability alone. Contingency is necessary, not sufficient.
- It also implies something innate on the receiving side: the infant is prepared to treat being mirrored and being smiled at as special (a "functional architecture"). For the engine, the innate body can include a prior that a partner's matching response and a partner's smile carry more weight than other contingent events. That is a design choice to state explicitly, since ASL (ray-heyes-2011-wealth-of-the-stimulus.md) needs no such prior.
- Marking with a smile reinforced social expression regardless of what it followed, as a generic social reward. Principle 6 forbids rewarding the engine for the user's smiles. This study shows that infants' social expressiveness is shaped by exactly that. The engine needs a satiable form of it (or an explicit decision to exclude it) to avoid the flattery failure the brief warns against.
- Mis-attuned or exaggerated responses disrupt engagement: an expectation window on the partner's response that the engine could have and could violate.
