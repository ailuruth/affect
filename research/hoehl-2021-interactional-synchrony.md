# Hoehl, Fairhurst & Schirmer 2021, "Interactional synchrony: signals, mechanisms and benefits" (Soc Cogn Affect Neurosci 16(1-2):5-18, doi 10.1093/scan/nsaa024)

Source read, 2026-11-25: full text, open access, PMC7812629 (fetched as HTML and converted to text). Read: all sections from the abstract to the conclusions, and the Figure 1 caption (Breska & Deouell). The Figure 2 framework diagram was not viewed, only its title. The reference list was not read.

## Claims
- **Signals:** synchronizing power rises with stimulus complexity. "Auditory, multimodal, emotional and somewhat variable and adaptive sequences" synchronize best. "Positive emotions enhance, whereas negative emotions impede alignment."
- **Mechanism, two stages:**
  - Temporal regularity, not meter or periodicity, is represented "fairly automatically" by striato-cortical loops.
  - Whether one actually aligns is gated by socio-emotional value, computed in the posterior superior temporal cortex / TPJ.
  - "We represent temporal regularity fairly automatically ... whether or how closely we adapt depends on a rhythm's perceived socio-emotional significance."
- **Selectivity:** "we adjust our own temporal rhythms to external ones in a more selective manner". The socio-emotional processes "serve as a filter that prevents us from synchronizing randomly in ways that may be detrimental".
- **Working definition:** interactional synchrony is "an active process in which one or more individuals adjust to each other rather than adjusting independently to stimuli in the environment". Comparisons should be between comparable processes (voluntary with voluntary), and the phase precision should be "scaled to the process of interest".
- **Benefits:** predictability (lower prediction error, freed working memory) and affiliation, bidirectionally. Rapport increases synchrony and induced synchrony increases rapport.
- **Limits — when more is worse:**
  - "In situations involving a high level of negative arousal, physiological synchrony might result in empathetic distress rather than empathetic concern."
  - Empathic concern needs "a clear self-other distinction as well as functional emotion regulation", and "a high degree of affective synchrony and self-other overlap ... may be detrimental".

## Evidence (as reported in the review)
- **Temporal contingency matters:** Nadel et al. 1999 replaced live maternal video with a replay of "good maternal behavior", and infant affect decreased. Manera et al. 2013 found detection of an interacting partner fell when a 667 ms phase delay was introduced.
- **Relationship gates synchrony:**
  - Konvalinka et al. 2011: closeness to fire-walkers predicted spectators' heart-rate synchrony.
  - Reindl et al. 2018: children aged 5-9 "synchronized brain activities with their mother, but not with an unfamiliar female".
  - Miles et al. 2010: people aligned more with a punctual than a late confederate.
  - Sun et al. 2019: more neural synchrony under a power framing than a trust framing.
- **Synchrony emerges even when it costs:** Naber et al. 2013, "Whac-a-Mole", where synchrony between players emerged "despite the fact that such synchrony impairs individual performance".
- **Asymmetric roles in neural coupling:** Jiang et al. 2015 found TPJ coupling "for leader–follower but not follower–follower pairs".
- **The optimum is mid-range, and too much coupling goes with risk:**
  - Beebe's "optimum midrange model" of caregiver-infant contingency: "both disengaged parenting and too intrusive parenting and overstimulation have been associated with insecure attachment outcomes".
  - Saxbe et al. 2017: "correlations of cortisol levels were higher in parent–child dyads with less behavioral coordination".
  - Wass et al. 2019: greater co-fluctuation of arousal with an anxious caregiver. "Non-anxious parents responded with arousal primarily to peaks in their infant's arousal, anxious parents responded also to small fluctuations."
  - Galbusera et al. 2019: more whole-body movement synchrony went with less affective self-regulation.
- **Models of human-like coordination (cited, not detailed):**
  - Dahan et al. 2016 add "a model term that increases with ongoing zero-phase synchrony and when crossing some threshold calls for a random force that allows the model to exit from synchrony". This reproduced human variability better than models without it.
  - Washburn et al. 2019: a small feedback delay lets an artificial agent anticipate chaotic human behaviour ("anticipatory synchronization").
- **Prosody (via Kleinbub 2017, not this review):** Jaffe et al. 2001 found extreme high and low synchrony both predict insecure attachment.

## Formal models
None given; the review is verbal. It points to:

- the striatal beat-frequency model (Buhusi & Meck 2005);
- predictive processing (Friston, Kilner);
- Dahan et al. 2016, synchrony-triggered noise to escape lock;
- Washburn et al. 2019, delay-coupled anticipatory synchronization.

None of their equations are in the review.

## What it implies for a dyadic affective core
- **Two separate factors set how much the partner gets in, and they differ in kind:**
  - an automatic perceptual tracker of the partner's regularities, always on;
  - a valuation gate that decides whether to align, set by relationship, rapport, valence and role.

  In a one-gain model the gain does both jobs. Here tracking is unconditional and alignment is selective, so a partner can be fully modelled without being copied. This is the separation the twelve sketches lacked: each of them used the same map for reading the partner and for being moved by the partner.
- **Selectivity by sensitivity threshold, not gain:** the anxious caregiver responds to small fluctuations, the non-anxious one only to peaks (Wass 2019). The difference is in which partner events trigger coupling, a threshold or nonlinearity, not in a multiplicative gain.
- **An exit mechanism is part of human-like coordination:** Dahan et al. 2016 needed a synchrony-accumulating term that forces escape from lock. That is a built-in anti-copy dynamic, not a lower gain.
- **Self-other distinction is required for concern rather than distress.** High affective synchrony under negative arousal is predicted to degrade the agent's regulation. A core needs a separate self state that is not overwritten by the partner during the partner's distress (relevant to Principle 3: reading through one's own body must not become becoming the other).
- **What is NOT shown:**
  - A narrative review; no meta-analytic numbers.
  - Most cited benefits are correlational or from induced motor synchrony in short tasks.
  - The two-stage neural framework is the authors' synthesis, "a potential theoretical framework".
  - Nothing specific to facial affect at sub-second scales beyond the 667 ms phase-delay result.

## Verdict: adopt (as a design constraint)
- **Constraint 1:** perception of the partner's dynamics and the decision to align are separate stages, and the second is gated by relationship and valence.
- **Constraint 2:** the evaluation should not reward maximal synchrony. Mid-range contingency and the escape from lock are the human pattern, which agrees with Principle 6.
- **What to read next:** Dahan et al. 2016 and Washburn et al. 2019 (cited, not read) for formal escape-from-synchrony and anticipation models.
