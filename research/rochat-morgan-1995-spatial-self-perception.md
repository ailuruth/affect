# Rochat & Morgan 1995, "Spatial determinants in the perception of self-produced leg movements by 3- to 5-month-old infants" (Developmental Psychology 31(4):626-636); with Rochat 1998, "Self-perception and action in infancy" (Experimental Brain Research 123:102-109) and the delay study in Rochat & Striano 2000, "Perceived self in infancy" (Infant Behavior & Development 23:513-530)

Source read:

- Rochat & Striano 2000: the full article was extracted, but only the sections on spatial and temporal determinants (1.6-1.8) and the conclusion were read closely.

## Claims
- With temporal contingency held perfect and identical in both views (both are live), 3- and 4-5-month-olds still tell two views of their own legs apart by spatial structure. They look longer, and kick more, at the view whose direction of movement is left-right reversed relative to what they feel. Orientation alone (feet up vs feet down, direction kept congruent) is not discriminated. "Directionality of movement was the crucial variable" (1995, p. 633).
- The authors suggest spatial determinants may come before temporal ones: their 3-month-olds discriminated by direction, while Bahrick & Watson's 3-month-olds showed no group preference by contingency. They flag the confound that their setup added a contingent rustling sound to every kick (p. 634-635).
- Rochat 1998, from Morgan & Rochat 1997: swapping which leg appears on which side (each leg's own orientation and direction kept) reduced looking and kicking at 3 and 4-5 months. With bulky socks hiding the knee and ankle bending, the swap had no effect (n = 10 per age). Read as an early "calibrated intermodal space of the body", a body schema.
- Rochat 1998, from Rochat & Morgan (then in press; 3-4-month-olds): with no object, infants looked and kicked more under the reversed view. When a kickable object that made the sound was on screen, the preference flipped to the congruent ego view. Rochat reads this as two modes, "doing" (use the familiar calibration to reach a goal) and "probing" (explore the violated calibration).
- Rochat 1998, from Rochat & Striano (then submitted): a pacifier gave sound either as an analog of the pressure applied (pitch tracked pressure online) or as a contingent but non-analog 2-s trill of random pitch. Two-month-olds sucked differently in the two conditions: lower amplitude, more presses just at threshold, more long presses in the analog condition. Newborns did not differ. So by 2 months infants discriminate "contingent and matching in form" from "contingent only".
- Rochat & Striano 2000, delay study: groups of 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 months (n = 10 each) saw two live views of their legs, one delayed by 0.5, 1, 2 or 3 s (1 min each). There was no systematic preference at any age or delay. They conclude that infants detect predictability and intermodal form, not exact temporal coincidence, and cite Watson (1984) that perfectly contingent feedback up to about 3 s of delay still specifies self.

## Evidence with numbers (Rochat & Morgan 1995)
- Apparatus: 60° reclined seat, so the legs are seen only on a 25-in. split screen 2 m away. Striped socks. A microphone under the feet played a rustle for every kick from a speaker on the TV. 5-min test. Looking coded from a face camera (reliability r > .92). Leg activity: sound above 1/3 of max counted as a 2-s bout. Measures: looking (s, %), leg activity as % of looking time per view, gaze switches within 1 s.
- Exp. 1, observer view (inverted orientation and reversed direction) vs ego view; n = 10 + 10 (3 m 13 d; 4 m 18 d); 6 excluded. Looking %: main effect of view, F(1,18) = 34.95, p < .0001. 9 of 10 at each age looked longer at the observer view (binomial p < .02). Looking seconds: Age × View F(1,18) = 6.86, p < .02, stronger at 3 months (Tukey p < .01) than at 4-5 months (p < .06). Gaze switches fell from 14 in minute 1 to 6 in minute 5 (F(4,72) = 7.83). Leg activity higher while looking at the TV than away, F(1,18) = 64.9, and marginally higher for the observer view, F(1,18) = 4.11, p < .057.
- Exp. 2, ego vs left-right reversed ego view (direction only); n = 10 + 10; 5 excluded. Looking %: 58% vs 42% (3 months), 59% vs 41% (4-5 months); view F(1,18) = 5.39, p < .03. 8 of 10 per age preferred the reversed view (p < .06). Leg activity per looking time higher for the reversed view, F(1,18) = 5.88, p < .03. Leg activity rose over minutes, F(4,72) = 4.13, p < .005.
- Exp. 3, ego vs reversed observer view (orientation only, direction congruent); n = 10 + 10; 9 excluded (6 looked less than 15%). No preference: F(1,18) = 1.56, p < .23. Split 6/4 and 4/6. No difference in leg activity, F(1,18) = 0.11.

## Mechanism
No formal model. What is detected is an intermodal invariant between felt and seen movement: timing, direction, and (from the sucking and bulky-sock studies) the form of the movement. Violating the direction mapping draws more looking and more self-generated movement while looking. The authors call this active exploration of the mismatch.

## What it implies for an engine that learns a partner model and its own self–other face map from contingencies in live interaction
- Learn the mapping, not just the timing. Infants register the sign and geometry of the map from their own motor space to the seen display, not only whether something moves when they move. For the face map: the engine's own-face channel and the partner's face should be compared through a learned map with sign and structure (left/right, which muscle moves which landmark), and a temporally contingent but structurally wrong reflection must count as a mismatch. A contingency score that ignores form (Watson's indices on event times alone) would treat the reversed view and the true view as equal.
- Self-generated variation is the probe. Infants kicked more while looking at the incongruent view. That is the behaviour an identification strategy needs: when the mapping is uncertain, emit more of one's own variation and watch the consequence. The engine should raise its spontaneous micro-movement when a channel's mapping is uncertain or violated, and should learn the mapping from those spontaneous movements. Because they are internally generated, they are not caused by the partner, so they can serve as the instrument that the critique (C13) asks for.
- Two modes, doing vs probing: with a goal, infants preferred the familiar calibration; without one, they explored the violated one. The engine should probe (vary, test the map) in low-stakes moments and rely on the learned map when acting toward the partner, rather than probing during an exchange that matters.
- Delay tolerance: perfectly predictable feedback delayed up to 3 s was treated like live feedback from 1 to 5 months. The engine's self-channel test should look for a perfectly predictable relation at some fixed lag, not for zero lag, and its estimate of its own efferent-to-afferent delay can be learned. Conversely, a partner who copies the engine with a fixed short lag would look like "self" by this test. The partner model must therefore check imperfection (Bahrick & Watson) and the partner's own dynamics, not lag alone.
- Caveat for the engine: none of these paradigms has a partner who also responds. The infant is always the only driver, so they show self-detection under clean intervention, not identification under mutual following.
