# Scherer 2003, "Vocal communication of emotion: A review of research paradigms"

Speech Communication 40(1-2):227-256. doi:10.1016/S0167-6393(02)00084-5.

Read in full: section 1 (the Brunswikian lens model), section 2 (encoding, decoding, inference, transmission and representation studies), section 3 (definition of emotion and design-feature table, choice of theory with the component-patterning predictions in Table 5, how to obtain samples, how to measure inferences, which parameters to measure, new paradigms), section 4 (conclusion). ## Claims
- Frame: a Brunswikian lens. Speaker state -> distal cues (measured acoustics; their correlation with the state is "ecological validity") -> transmission -> proximal cues (the listener's percept, which Scherer extends to the neural representation, e.g. perceived pitch rather than F0) -> inference (cue utilization) -> attribution. Communication fails at a identifiable link: no reliable externalization, degradation in transmission, or a flawed inference rule. Example from his personality work: extraversion is both encoded and decoded through the voice; "emotional stability" is decoded consistently (resonant, warm, low-pitched voices) but not encoded at all: a shared stereotype with no kernel in the speaker.
- Three ways to get emotional speech (natural, induced, portrayed); each flawed. Portrayals are more intense and prototypical and influenced by stereotypes; induction gives weak affect and different appraisals in different people; broadcast "natural" speech is performed and its underlying state unknown. Scherer asks for experimental induction with manipulation checks, within-speaker designs, and comparison of portrayed and natural speech (none existed in 2003).
- The voice is not only an arousal channel. The impression that it is came from measuring only F0, energy and rate, and from lumping emotion-family members (hot vs cold anger, elation vs quiet happiness). F0, energy and rate may index arousal while valence acts more on source and spectral characteristics.
- Recognition from content-free voice: about 60% in ~30 studies before the 1980s, 55-65% for actor portrayals generally, about five to six times chance, versus about 75% for faces. Cross-culturally 66% across 9 countries with German portrayals, falling as the listener's language is more dissimilar from German: universal inference rules plus language-specific paralinguistic patterns. In the voice, sadness and anger are recognized best, then fear; joy, near-perfect in the face, is hard in the voice; disgust is barely above chance. Explanation offered: the voice evolved for signals that must carry over distance (warning, threat).
- Resynthesis (Ladd et al. 1985; Tolkmitt et al. 1988): manipulating F0 level, contour, range, intensity, duration and accent on natural utterances gave strong, mostly additive main effects with few interactions. F0 range had the largest effect: narrow range read as sadness, wide range as high-arousal negative (annoyance, anger). High intensity read as negative or aggressive; fast speech as joy, slow as sadness. Speaker and content effects were minor.
- Transmission and hearing reshape cues: telephone band-limiting lowers perceived pitch; perceived loudness tracks one or a few harmonics more than overall intensity; listeners judge vocal effort from the spectral balance of harmonics and noise even when loudness is equalized (a soft voice at six times the intensity of a loud one is still heard as soft, Klasmeyer 1999). Speakers adapt production to the channel (shouting changes many laryngeal parameters together). Scherer recommends perceptual measures (Bark bands, perceived loudness and pitch).
- Affective state types by design features (Table 4): emotion (brief, synchronized across subsystems, event-focused, appraisal-driven), mood, interpersonal stance, attitude, trait. Everyday speech is coloured mostly by stress, mild mood and interpersonal stance, not by the intense emotions studied in the lab, and intense emotions are the most regulated.
- Componential (appraisal) theory gives mechanism-level predictions per check (Table 5, after Scherer 1986):
  - Novelty: interruption of phonation, sudden inhalation, ingressive sound with glottal stop.
  - Intrinsic pleasantness: "wide voice" (faucal and pharyngeal expansion, relaxed tract walls, mouth corners up: more low-frequency energy, F1 falling) vs "narrow voice" (constriction, tensed walls, corners down: more high-frequency energy, F1 rising, F2 and F3 falling, narrow F1 bandwidth).
  - Goal/need significance: "relaxed voice" (F0 low in range, low-moderate amplitude) when consistent; "tense voice" (F0 and amplitude up, jitter and shimmer, more high-frequency energy) when discrepant; combined with wide or narrow by conduciveness.
  - Coping potential: control -> tense; no control -> "lax voice" (hypotonus: low F0, restricted range, low amplitude, weak pulses, very low high-frequency energy, spectral noise, neutral formants, broad F1 bandwidth). Power: high -> chest register, deep forceful respiration, low F0, high amplitude ("full voice"); low -> head register, raised F0 ("thin voice").
- Standardize the parameter set across labs (the call that GeMAPS later answered).

## Evidence with numbers
Review, so numbers are others': ~60% mean recognition (Scherer 1989, ~30 studies); 55-65% voice vs ~75% face; 66% over 9 countries (Scherer et al. 2001); Banse & Scherer 48% over 14 categories (55% families collapsed).

## What it implies for an engine whose voice is driven from an internal state through an innate vocal body
- Table 5 is close to a specification for the innate vocal body: a small set of physiological settings (respiration depth, laryngeal tension, register, glottal pulse strength, pharyngeal width, lip-corner retraction) whose combinations produce the acoustics. These are body variables, not emotion names, so they fit Principle 2: the engine's latent state drives tension, width, register and breath; "wide/narrow/tense/lax/full/thin" are settings, and any category is only the listener's reading.
- The settings combine additively in the theory and, in resynthesis, mostly additively in perception. That justifies a vocal body with independent actuators whose effects sum, and makes a factorial ablation possible (remove pharyngeal width control; listeners should lose valence cues but keep arousal cues).
- Lip-corner retraction appears inside the voice predictions: the face and the voice share an actuator (smiling shortens the tract and raises resonances). The engine's face and voice bodies should be coupled through shared articulators, not driven as two channels.
- The lens model fits Principle 3. The engine's perception of the operator's voice is the proximal side; the engine's own production is the distal side. An agent that infers the other's state by inverting its own vocal body is a lens model whose cue-utilization weights are the agent's own encoding weights. The lens also names what to test: ecological validity of the engine's output cues (do they track its state?) and cue utilization by the operator (does the operator respond to them?).
- Use perceptual, speaker-relative features as input (perceived pitch in semitones, loudness, spectral balance), and remember the operator's microphone is a transmission channel that biases spectral cues.
- Everyday affect is mostly mood and stance at low intensity. The engine's voice should mostly carry slow, small shifts of stance, with large excursions rare; an engine whose voice swings like actor portrayals would be miscalibrated.
- Novelty has a vocal reflex of its own (phonation interrupted, inhalation): a candidate innate vocal reflex for the engine, like a startle.
