# Seibt, Mühlberger, Likowski & Weyers 2015, "Facial mimicry in its social setting" (Frontiers in Psychology 6:1122, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01122)

Source read: full article PDF from Frontiers (open access). I read in full the sections on the setting and social interaction, perceiver state (sad and fearful mood), the sender (gaze, dynamic expressions), the relationship (familiarity, attitudes, interdependence including Table 1, fairness, power, group membership, social exclusion), responses to happy and angry expressions, general conclusions and measurement. I skimmed the perceiver-trait sections (empathy, attachment, social anxiety, gender) and the methods table. The primary studies below are reported as this review reports them; I read Weyers 2009, Bourgeois & Hess 2008, Hess & Bourgeois 2010, Fischer et al. 2012, Hofman et al. 2012 and Carr et al. 2014 myself (separate notes), but not Likowski et al. 2011a, Seibt et al. 2013, Häfner & IJzerman 2011, Lanzetta & Englis 1989, Heerey & Crossley 2013 or Sims et al. 2012.

## Claims
- General conclusion, in the authors' words: "this moderation is not just a matter of more or less mimicry, but that the intensity and type of facial response shown to facial displays also depend on the facial expression and the gaze direction." They add that predicting every combination of setting, perceiver, relationship, dynamics, expression, muscle and gaze may be "akin to trying to predict the exact verbal reply to a particular statement".
- Happy expressions evoke the most robust response (zygomaticus and orbicularis up, corrugator down). Even competitors and sad-mood participants still show corrugator relaxation to smiles; what drops out is the zygomaticus smile.
- Anger mimicry is "puzzling" if mimicry serves affiliation. Proposed reasons it appears in labs: corrugator also indexes effort, disapproval and surprise; less social settings permit it; shared anger at a common opponent; deterrence. In live settings anger is rarely mimicked.
- Among intimates, "smiles in response to smiles and to other emotional expressions seem to regulate the relationship"; only two publications measured mimicry with long-standing partners (Fischer et al. 2012; Häfner & IJzerman 2011).

## Evidence (as reported by the review)
- Interdependence, Table 1 (three experiments with women: Weyers et al. 2009; Seibt et al. 2013; Likowski et al. 2011a). In all three, no zygomaticus activation to happy faces under competition. Under competition, corrugator relaxed (not contracted) to sad and angry faces in Seibt 2013 and Likowski 2011a, read as a positive reaction to a competitor's setback; in Likowski 2011a this was mediated by self-reported joy, and the loss of zygomaticus to happy faces was mediated by lower state cognitive empathy. Under explicit cooperation (Likowski 2011a), zygomaticus activated to sad faces, mediated by the goal of a smooth interaction ("encouragement"); under subliminal cooperation priming (Seibt 2013), zygomaticus and orbicularis activated to angry faces ("soothing smile").
- Häfner & IJzerman 2011, Study 1: increased zygomaticus to the anger expressions of romantic partners toward whom participants had a communal orientation.
- Likowski et al. 2008: after one pairing of avatars with trait words, zygomaticus to happy faces of positive and neutral but not negative characters; congruent corrugator to sad faces of positive characters, incongruent corrugator deactivation to sad faces of negative characters.
- Sims et al. 2012 (conditioning): zygomaticus to happy faces was a positive linear function of the reward value previously paired with that face. Aguado et al. 2013: after neutral faces were followed by that person's happy or angry face, participants later showed the differential pattern to the neutral faces themselves.
- Heerey & Crossley 2013: Duchenne smiles were reciprocated earlier than polite smiles, "with muscle contractions even starting before the onset of an expected Duchenne smile" (EMG study); same direction in FACS-coded live conversation.
- Kawamoto et al. 2014: more zygomaticus to happy faces after Cyberball exclusion. Likowski et al. 2011b: sad mood left almost no facial reactions except corrugator relaxation to happy faces.
- Measurement: studies differ in window (first second vs whole 6-s means); "An important next step will be to study the time course of facial mimicry modulations in more detail."

## What it implies for the design
- The review's own summary rejects the single-gain picture: relation and goals change which channel responds and with what sign. The minimal structure consistent with Table 1 is a context-dependent linear map from partner channels to own channels (off-diagonal and negative entries allowed: smile to the partner's anger or sadness, brow relaxation to a rival's distress), not a scalar times the identity.
- Some components are robust across contexts (corrugator relaxation to smiles); others are the ones that move (zygomaticus return, response to negative faces). An agent can keep a stable valence-congruent core and learn context-dependent terms on top.
- Relation values can be learned fast from experience (one pairing; conditioned reward; partner reputation), which is what Principle 2 needs: no labelled relation types, only history with this person.
- Anticipatory smile return to expected genuine smiles means the response depends on prediction of the partner, not only on the current frame.

## Verdict: adopt (as the map of the field, with primary sources checked separately)
Many of its central studies come from the authors' own lab, all-female samples, avatar stimuli and small N. Treat the Table 1 pattern as replicated within one lab.
