# Friston, Thornton & Clark 2012, "Free-Energy Minimization and the Dark-Room Problem" (Frontiers in Psychology 3:130; PMC3347222)

Source read: the full text on PubMed Central (PMC3347222), from the abstract through the final exchange, the conflict-of-interest statement and the acknowledgments. Read on 2026-11-25. Not read: the reference list. The later active-inference papers that add an explicit epistemic (information-gain) term to policy selection, for example Friston et al. 2015 "Active inference and epistemic value", were not read and are not reported here.

## Claims
- The problem, as stated by the Theorist (Thornton): if surprise is computed from raw frequencies of sensory data, "reduction of surprise dictates blocking out sensory data altogether. Alternatively but equally absurdly, agents should proceed directly to the least stimulating environment and stay there. That is to say, they should take up position in the nearest 'dark room' and never move again."
- The answer, as stated by the Physicist (Friston), is that surprise is relative to the agent's model, not to sensory entropy alone: "the resolution of the Dark-Room Problem rests on the fact that average surprise or entropy H(s | m) is a function of sensations and the agent (model) predicting them. Conversely, the entropy H(s) minimized in dark rooms is only a function of sensory information."
- Its condition: "a dark room will afford low levels of surprise if, and only if, the agent has been optimized by evolution (or neurodevelopment) to predict and inhabit it. Agents that predict rich stimulating environments will find the 'dark room' surprising and will leave at the earliest opportunity." Dark-room agents exist: "Troglophiles have evolved to model and navigate environments like caves".
- The framing is homeostatic: "The free-energy principle says that we harvest sensory signals that we can predict ... ensuring we keep to well-trodden paths in the space of all the physical and physiological variables that underwrite our existence."
- The Philosopher's (Clark's) summary: "The dark-room scenario cannot then obtain unless staying in the dark room is itself the way to minimize surprise relative to the expectations implicitly defined by that entire, and importantly niche-reflecting, model. That means that for creatures like us, the dark room is simply not an attractor. The explanatory burden then shifts to the question how we became creatures like us in the first place."

## Evidence
None. A philosophical dialogue modelled on Eddington's prolog, with no data, simulation or equations beyond H(s | m) vs H(s). Two objections remain open in the text. The Theorist: "If the critical question of how probabilities are determined is left unsettled, there is no limit to the ways we can envisage agents determining probability distributions", illustrated by four "cambots" (MIN1-MIN4). Under different probability models they crash into a barrier, do nothing, wander to a tartan flag, or flee. The Philosopher: the principle "may, in at least one important sense, turn out to be a little bit too accommodating". The authors close without agreement: "No doubt the debate will continue."

## Verdict: watch (as the free-energy side's official answer), reject (as a mechanism for curiosity)
- The answer moves the dark room out of the objective and into the priors. The agent leaves the dark room only if its model already expects a rich, changing world. For us that means the anti-dark-room property would have to be put in by hand as a prior over sensory states. It does not follow from the objective. That conflicts with brief.md Principle 10 (components must follow from the core's equations) unless the prior is itself derived.
- It does nothing for the noisy TV. Surprise minimization under any model avoids unpredictable input; the paper's own MIN4 "vigorously seek[s] out a more predictable environment".
- On satiability it is the opposite pole from Oudeyer and Schmidhuber: surprise minimization is homeostatic ("well-trodden paths in the space of ... physiological variables") but has no curiosity term. Schmidhuber 2010 (§IV.D) answers that under learning progress dark rooms "get boring as soon as they are predictable". A set-point learning-progress variable (D62) would join the two: homeostatic in form, as here, with the regulated quantity being progress, as in Oudeyer and Schmidhuber. No source we read does this; it is our construction.
- If the active-inference candidate keeps an expected-free-energy formulation, read the epistemic-value papers before claiming it avoids the dark room. This note does not support that claim.
