# Synthesis: how the face is driven from inside, and how infants come to express and read emotion

## 1. The face has more than one driver: the two-route structure

Clinical neurology has known for a century (Monrad-Krohn 1924) that voluntary and emotional facial movement dissociate. The evidence is double dissociation:
- Motor cortex or corticobulbar lesions: patients cannot smile on command but smile normally at a joke; the emotional smile can even be exaggerated on the weak side (matsumoto-lee, topper, gothard).
- Basal ganglia, thalamus, hypothalamus, or anterior cerebral artery (midcingulate) lesions: voluntary movement is intact, spontaneous emotional expression is lost ("amimia") (matsumoto-lee, gothard).
- Facial nerve anastomosis restores voluntary movement but not emotional expression; pseudobulbar palsy produces laughing or crying with no matching feeling (matsumoto-lee, reporting Rinn).
- The two streams stay separate down to the upper pons: a pontine infarct of the corticobulbar fibres gave a volitional palsy while laughing stayed symmetric (topper, n = 1).

Anatomy (macaque tract-tracing as summarised by gothard): five cortical face motor areas per hemisphere converge on one final common path, the facial nucleus.
- Deliberate route: M1 and ventrolateral premotor cortex go to the lateral facial nucleus (lower face, contralateral). SMA goes to the medial nucleus (upper face) and handles self-initiated movement. Premotor cortex initiates externally cued movement, so the partner's face could be the cue.
- Affective route: the midcingulate face areas. M3 projects bilaterally to the upper face and to brainstem autonomic centres, so it drives the face and the viscera together. M4 projects to the upper lip and is needed for emotional but not voluntary smiling. The amygdala projects directly to the premotor, SMA, M3 and M4 face areas. It seems to select which expression fits the social context rather than to form it.
- Reflex and pattern-generator level: periaqueductal gray, hypothalamus and brainstem tegmentum produce stereotyped bilateral displays such as laughing, crying, defensive faces and orofacial taste responses. The emotional route reaches the facial nucleus polysynaptically through lateral-tegmental interneurons (topper).
- Region prior: lower face mostly contralateral and cortically driven; upper two thirds bilateral and more limbic (gothard, topper, matsumoto-lee).

Three properties matter for design:
1. The routes interact. The cortical route continuously inhibits and reshapes the affective one. Evidence: release of the emotional smile after cortical lesions; more spontaneous smiling in premature infants, declining as the cortex matures (messinger-fogel); learned display rules that become automatic by mid-to-late childhood (matsumoto-lee). A masked smile has a signature: the mouth (zygomaticus) without the eyes (orbicularis oculi).
2. The loop is closed. Amygdala neurons fire after EMG onset, to the sensory consequence of one's own expression. Amygdala and cingulate respond both to seen and to produced expressions, and facial feedback may modulate feeling (gothard, matsumoto-lee, messinger-fogel). The face is an input to the affective state as well as an output.
3. Expression is gated by social context. Duration of eye contact predicts whether an expression is reciprocated in monkeys and humans. Amygdala "eye cells" fire during mutual gaze (gothard).

Matsumoto & Lee add that a voluntary/involuntary split is too crude for adult faces. They propose three dimensions: display-rule modification, degree of learning and automatisation, and conscious effort. An adult's "spontaneous" social smile is often an automatised learned regulation acting on the affective route.

## 2. What is innate

Good evidence:
- Muscle synergies, not emotion templates. Half of sleeping neonates show bilateral Duchenne smiles (lip-corner pull with cheek raise), a quarter of them at mature intensity, with no social trigger. Smile strength and eye constriction are already coupled (messinger-fogel). Newborn smiles appear in REM sleep, about one per 5 minutes, and probably come from subcortical structures.
- A small set of reflexive displays: the smile, the cry-face (eye constriction carries intensity, mouth opening carries arousal), and disgust and crying faces. Disgust and crying appear even in infants without forebrain structures above the midbrain (matsumoto-lee, citing Steiner 1973, secondary). Congenitally blind infants show the spontaneous repertoire (matsumoto-lee, messinger-fogel).
- Arousal and exploration-driven orofacial responses. Newborns protrude the tongue to many arousing stimuli: lights, music, an approaching pen, a tongue model. These responses fade by about 2-3 months (jones, ray-heyes). Jacobson's alternative is an innate releasing mechanism, an approaching nipple-like object eliciting a feeding response (ray-heyes).
- Perceptual and motivational preparedness. Newborns prefer faces, direct gaze and happy expressions (murray, citing others). Infants detect contingency and are rewarded by it (gergely-watson, bahrick-watson). Infants are also especially sensitive to specific partner responses: mirroring and marking with a smile (murray).
- Developmental timing. Social smiling tracks postconceptional age, not postnatal experience (messinger-fogel).

Not innate, on current evidence:
- Adult-prototype discrete negative expressions. Izard's infant "anger", "disgust" and "sadness" configurations do not match the FACS adult formulas (Oster et al. 1992: only 3 of 19 negative expressions agreed). Judges read infant negative faces as "distress". At 11 months, anger and fear situations produce the same facial patterns even though the infants' behaviour differs, in three cultures (r = .50 between the two situations' expression rank orders, Camras et al. 2007). Situational specificity is met only for happiness at 4 months, and for happiness and disgust at 12 months (camras-shutter).
- A general visual-to-motor correspondence for faces (neonatal imitation). Here the evidence is contested:
  - Only tongue protrusion survives review (Ray & Heyes: 18 gestures in 37 experiments; Anisfeld: 35 studies). Jones and Ray & Heyes argue that even tongue protrusion is non-specific.
  - Oostenbroek et al. 2016 (n = 106, 11 models, 4 ages) found no imitation. Meltzoff et al. 2018 re-analysed the same data against the mean of 10 arousing controls and found tongue protrusion at every age, d = .24 to .40. The reply to that re-analysis was not read.
  - Even the favourable reading gives one orofacial act with small effects, and no innate matching of emotional expressions.
  - Ray & Heyes judge reported neonatal matching of emotional expressions to be a scoring artefact. Jones allows that species-typical emotional displays may be "reflexively matched". This point is unresolved.
- Facial mirror neurons. Cook et al. state that the claim that face mirror neurons are innate while hand mirror neurons are learned is untested. Facial self-imitation improves only with visual feedback (Cook, Johnston & Heyes 2013, secondary).

## 3. What is learned, and from which contingencies

Learning shapes four things.
- The triggers and timing of innate expressions. The smile's eliciting stimulus shifts from the voice (week 2) to voice plus face (week 5) to mutual gaze with the mother's face (week 8). By 2 months infants smile less at strangers whose contingency is higher or lower than the mother's (messinger-fogel).
- Coupling to the partner. From 1 to 6 months, high-intensity "duplay" smiles concentrate in moments when the infant gazes at the mother and she smiles. Mother and infant do not copy each other's smile form (messinger-fogel).
- Social expressiveness itself. It rises steeply from 3 to 9 weeks. Its growth is predicted by the form of the mother's responses, not by their rate (murray; details under "Which contingencies").
- Imitation and the self-other map. Real imitation of facial gestures appears in the second year, act by act. Visible (transparent) actions are imitated before opaque ones, and actions with sounds before silent ones (jones, ray-heyes). The mechanism is contingency-sensitive associative learning between seeing and doing (ray-heyes, cook):
  - The link forms only if seeing x is more probable while doing x than otherwise (contingency), not from mere co-occurrence.
  - It can be abolished or reversed in adults by 50-90 minutes of crossed training.
  - Learned links are gated by context, as in Bouton's renewal.
  - Twin data put 30% of the variance in imitation at 2 years on genes and 70% on environment. Infants whose parents rarely imitate them imitate less (ray-heyes).

Two further things are learned later:
- Differentiated negative expressions. Infants show emotion-specific behaviour (fighting the arm restraint, avoiding the growling toy) before they show emotion-specific faces. Adult-like forms probably emerge after 12 months, shaped by socialisation (camras-shutter).
- Regulation. Infants avert their gaze during intense smiles by 6 months (messinger-fogel). Display rules are learned through childhood (matsumoto-lee).

Which contingencies:
- Being imitated. In the first year, mothers imitate infants about once a minute, roughly five times as often as the reverse; 79% of imitation episodes are mother copying infant. These imitations are marked (exaggerated, rewarded) (ray-heyes, jones).
- Mirroring of the infant's social expressions. In Murray et al., only 15% of these were mirrored, yet mirroring predicted expressiveness two weeks later. Overall contingency did not (murray).
- Marked, contingent affect-mirroring. Through it the infant learns its own internal states (gergely-watson). Self versus other is told apart by perfect versus high-but-imperfect contingency (bahrick-watson). *[Corrected 2026-11-25: the primary, now read, shows 5-month-olds prefer the noncontingent display, while 3-month-olds split bimodally with no group preference; see bahrick-watson-1985-proprioceptive-visual-contingency.md and contingency-causal-synthesis.md.]*
- Self-observation: own hands directly, the face only in a mirror. In Amsterdam's study, 85% of 6-12-month-olds treated the mirror image as a playmate (ray-heyes).
- Synchronous reactions to shared events. In 55% of spoon-feeding sequences, mother and infant both opened their mouths.
- Acquired equivalence: a word or sound paired at one time with seeing an act and at another with doing it (ray-heyes).
- The partner's withdrawal. The still-face drop in infant positive affect and gaze is the standard test that the infant's expression depends on the partner's contingent response (mesman-2009).

There are two views on what the infant brings, and a design must state which it adopts:
- Associative (Heyes, Cook): domain-general learning and no prior toward matching.
- Functional architecture (Murray, also Meltzoff): the infant is prepared to treat mirroring and smiling responses as special.
Both agree that contingency is necessary. Murray's data say that contingency alone is not sufficient.

## 4. Implications for the engine's innate body and for learning by living

Innate body. These are proposals, each with a source-grounded ablation prediction.
1. A muscle layer with two or three separately ablatable input routes converging on it:
   - (a) a deliberate route that can address individual muscles, used by the conceptual layer or a learned policy, strongest on the lower face;
   - (b) an affective route driven by the latent affective state and interoception, recruiting synergies, bilateral and reaching the upper face;
   - (c) brainstem pattern generators for reflex displays (cry, laugh, startle, gape, arousal-driven orofacial exploration).
   Ablation predictions follow the clinical pattern. Cut (a): posed expressions fail, spontaneous ones survive or are exaggerated. Cut (b): spontaneous expression goes flat while posing survives. These are the volitional and emotional palsies, with a century of human data to compare against. (gothard, topper, matsumoto-lee)
2. Innate synergies rather than emotion presets: AU12 recruiting AU6 with intensity, mouth opening with arousal, and a cry-face whose eye constriction and mouth opening scale with distress. There are no innate anger, fear or surprise templates (camras-shutter, messinger-fogel). This matches Principle 2 and argues against six basic-emotion presets (see mimo.md).
3. Cortical inhibition of the affective route as a learned, initially weak regulator. Its absence early in life gives more spontaneous expression; its growth gives display rules and masking (matsumoto-lee, messinger-fogel). The conceptual layer reaches the face mostly by modulating the affective route, not by writing expressions. This is consistent with Principle 4, in which Claude never drives frames.
4. Proprioceptive feedback from the face into the affective state, so the loop is closed (gothard). Without it, facial-feedback effects and self-monitoring are impossible. Ablation prediction: removing it should weaken the coupling between expression and state.
5. Gaze and context gating of expressive responses: mutual gaze raises reciprocation (gothard, messinger-fogel).
6. A contingency detector, and possibly a prior that weights a partner's matching or smiling response. That prior is an explicit choice between the Heyes and Murray positions. It is testable: an engine without it should still learn mimicry from contingency, more slowly.

Learning by living:
7. The visual-to-motor face map starts weak and non-selective. It is trained only by contingent see-x/do-x pairs, with a prediction-error (Rescorla-Wagner) rule, not Hebbian co-occurrence (cook, ray-heyes). In conversation, people smile at the same moments by chance. A contiguity rule would bind everything to everything.
   - Falsifiable prediction: if the partner's displays are systematically crossed with the engine's own acts, the engine's "mimicry" should reverse into counter-mimicry. If it does not, the map was not learned.
   - Context gating should make the map operator-specific.
8. Sources of pairs for an artificial agent:
   - The engine's own rendered face seen while issuing muscle commands: a perfect-contingency mirror, available for free. It should be marked as self, using the perfect-contingency signature from Bahrick & Watson.
   - The operator's spontaneous matching.
   - Shared reactions to the same event.
   - Words: Claude naming "frown" both when the operator frowns and when the engine does.
   A risk to record: an adult operator will not mirror the engine once a minute with exaggerated marking, as mothers mirror infants. Measure the contingency actually available in sessions before expecting mimicry to form. Do not quietly replace missing data with an innate copy map.
9. Expression develops in stages, and evaluation should look for that order: reflexes and synergies first; then social triggering of the smile; then dyad-specific contingency expectations (a still-face violation response); then differentiated negative faces, after differentiated coping behaviour.
10. Tension with Principle 6. Infants' social expressiveness is reinforced by the partner's smiles (positive marking), the very signal Principle 6 forbids rewarding. If this pathway is included, it must be satiable. Excluding it is a decision with a predicted developmental cost: less social expressiveness.

Evaluation borrowed from the infant literature:
- Matching tests with controls that are equally arousing and matched for motion and location (meltzoff-2018's tube and box controls). A mimicry claim must beat these, not just a still face.
- Sufficiency and necessity indices of who drives whom. The mother-infant asymmetry was: infant smile sufficient but not necessary for a mother smile; mother display necessary but not sufficient for an infant smile (messinger-fogel, gergely-watson).
- Still-face and reunion effects, per mesman-2009.
- Facial EMG-style mimicry onset at about 500 ms (dimberg-thunberg).
