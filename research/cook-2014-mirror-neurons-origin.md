# Cook, Bird, Catmur, Press & Heyes 2014, "Mirror neurons: from origin to function" (Behavioral and Brain Sciences 37(2):177-192, doi:10.1017/S0140525X13000903)

Page wording may differ from the published version.

## Claims
- Thesis: mirror neurons (MNs) "are forged by domain-general processes of associative learning in the course of individual development", and so "do not necessarily have a specific evolutionary purpose or adaptive function". Four lines of evidence: MNs do not consistently encode goals; contingency and context sensitivity explain all MN properties; infants get enough sensorimotor experience ("wealth of the stimulus"); MNs "can be changed in radical ways by sensorimotor training".
- Mechanism (Figure 1): before learning, visual neurons in STS that encode seen actions are weakly and non-selectively connected to premotor and parietal motor neurons; "the links between sensory and motor neurons coding similar actions are not consistently stronger than other, nonmatching links". Correlated experience of seeing and doing the same action strengthens matching links, and "motor neurons become MNs". A face example in the text: "when an adult imitates an infant's facial movements, there might be correlated excitation of neurons that are responsive to the observation and execution of lip protrusion".
- Contingency, not mere contiguity: learning requires "that the event provoking firing of one predicts the event provoking firing of the other". Where one event "is equally likely to occur in the presence and absence of the other event - contiguous pairings produce little or no learning" (Rescorla 1968). Human mirror effects change with contingent but not non-contingent sensorimotor experience (Cook, Press, Dickinson & Heyes 2010). Contiguity-only (Hebbian) learning would produce "junk associations".
- The hierarchy of visual and motor codes explains strictly congruent MNs (low-level contingencies, e.g. self-observation of one grip) and broadly congruent MNs (high-level contingencies, e.g. many grasps during group feeding). Non-matching contingencies (you release, I grasp) produce "logically-related" MNs; action sounds produce audiovisual MNs; pliers produce tool-use MNs.
- Contextual modulation: as with Bouton's renewal (a tone that meant shock in one context and food in another elicits freezing again in a novel context), MN responses that look like goal coding (grasp-to-eat vs grasp-to-place, depending on a cup) can be context-gated associations.
- Infants: human neonates match only tongue protrusion, and that effect is non-specific (lights, music; and greater to a mechanical "tongue" or disembodied mouth, Soussignan et al. 2011). Newborn monkey lip-smacking matching (Paukner et al. 2011) seems driven by low lip-smacking in the static-face control rather than more lip-smacking to the model. Infant EEG alpha suppression is not evidence of mirroring. Associative learning ability at 1 month predicted imitation 8 months later (Reeb-Sutherland et al. 2012). With high contingency, infants learn action-effect associations "in just a few trials".
- Training studies in adults: brief sensorimotor training can "enhance", "abolish", "reverse" and "induce" mirror responses, measured by fMRI, repetition suppression, MEPs and automatic imitation. Examples: ~50 min of opening the hand to a robotic pincer opening made automatic imitation of the pincer as strong as of a human hand 24 h later (Press et al. 2007); ~90 min of counter-mirror finger training reversed muscle-specific MEPs (Catmur et al. 2007).
- Faces specifically: a "hand / face hybrid" (Casile et al. 2011) says facial MNs are innate while hand MNs are learned. Its prediction (face MNs less modifiable) is untested, and "a recent study of improvement in facial imitation suggests that face MNs are as susceptible to modification by sensorimotor experience as hand MNs (Cook, Johnston, & Heyes, 2013)" ("Facial self-imitation: objective measurement reveals no improvement without visual feedback"). So the hybrid "stands as an intriguing but essentially ad hoc hypothesis".
- Self-recognition of one's own movement without visual experience (avatar facial motion) depended on temporal (rhythm) cues, not spatial form (Cook, Johnston & Heyes 2012), so it is not evidence for an innate mirror mapping.

## Evidence
Theory and review in a target article. The numbers above (50 min, 90 min, 24 h) are reported training durations. No pooled effect sizes are given in the parts read.

## What it implies for the engine
- The engine's "perceive others through its own motor pathways" (Principle 3) can be built from a learned observation-to-motor map that starts weak and non-selective and is shaped only by contingent seeing-and-doing. That is the brief's learning-by-living, and it keeps the mapping out of hand-coded correspondences.
- The learning rule must be contingency-sensitive (Rescorla-Wagner style, prediction error), not Hebbian co-occurrence. In a conversation, the operator and engine smile together often by chance; contiguity-only learning would bind everything to everything. This is a concrete requirement on the formal core: the update should depend on how much the engine's act x raises the probability of seeing x relative to its base rate.
- Context gating comes for free in associative learning and gives a mechanism for "this person, this situation" specificity: the same seen frown can map to different motor and affective responses in different relational contexts. That matches the brief's demand for needs and states structured over people and episodes.
- The model predicts that the engine will learn non-matching mappings when the environment supplies them (operator frowns when the engine smiles), so "mimicry" is not guaranteed; what is learned is whatever the dyad's contingencies are. Complementary responses (laugh when teased) fall out of the same rule. This makes mimicry a testable emergent outcome, as Principle 10 requires.
- Facial self-observation matters: improvement in facial self-imitation required visual feedback. An engine that sees its own rendered face while issuing muscle commands gets the feedback humans get only from mirrors and partners.

## Verdict: adopt (as the learning rule and the case against innate facial mirroring)
Caveat: the commentaries include strong objections (e.g. from mirror-neuron researchers) that were not read. Read Cook, Press, Dickinson & Heyes 2010 (contingency) and Cook, Johnston & Heyes 2013 (facial self-imitation) before using their specifics.
