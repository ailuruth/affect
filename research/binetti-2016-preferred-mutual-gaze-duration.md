# Binetti, Harrison, Coutrot, Johnston & Mareschal 2016, "Pupil dilation as an index of preferred mutual gaze duration" (Royal Society Open Science 3:160086, doi:10.1098/rsos.160086)

Source read: full text from PubMed Central (PMC4968459, via the PMC OAI full-text record): abstract, introduction, methods and results, discussion. The electronic supplement was not read.

## Claims
- There is a typical comfortable duration for being looked at directly by a stranger, about 3.3 s, with a spread of under a second across people.
- It does not depend on gender, personality (Big Five) or the actor's rated attractiveness, dominance or trustworthiness; it is shorter for actors rated more threatening.
- People who prefer longer direct gaze show a faster pupil dilation in the first 500 ms of seeing the face, and already before the face appears.

## Evidence with numbers
- 498 London Science Museum visitors (11–79 years, 56 nationalities); 428 with acceptable psychometric fits analysed.
- Task: chin rest, video of one of eight actors who looks straight at the viewer for 0.1–10.3 s (in 300-ms steps), with 500 ms of downward-averted gaze before and after. After each clip: "too short" or "too long" relative to comfortable, imagining a silent encounter with a stranger on the underground. 40 trials per person, durations chosen by two interleaved QUEST staircases.
- **Preferred gaze duration (PGD): 3295 ± 706 ms** (50% point of a cumulative Gaussian per person); mean psychometric s.d. 703 ms. No gender-group difference (F(3,424) = 1.45, p = .23).
- PGD correlated with psychometric s.d. (r = .43, the scalar property of timing); with age only in men watching women (r = .23); with actor threat rating r = −.13 (p = .005). No personality correlations.
- Pupil: the long-PGD group dilated faster in the first 500 ms (first PCA component; d from .35 to 1.08 as the groups were drawn further from the mean). The difference was present 200 ms before face onset (anticipatory). PC1 score vs PGD: r = .16 (anticipatory window), r = .18 (first 500 ms).
- The authors cite earlier dyadic estimates of 2.95 s and 4.66 s, consistent with their 3.3 s (those papers were not read here).
- Stated limits: one-way video, no conversation, no reciprocal gaze; the 'stranger on the underground' framing; the staircase confounds PGD with difficulty (they argue against this from the timing of the pupil effect).

## What it implies for the design
- A sustained direct look from the agent passes a stranger's comfortable duration after roughly 3 s (±0.7 s). The design's look of concern holds gaze and brow together for minutes; on this evidence the gaze part alone becomes uncomfortable within seconds unless it is broken.
- The tolerable duration shortens for faces judged more threatening. A lowered or tense brow combined with direct gaze is likely to shorten it further (inference, not tested here with expressions).
- The comfortable limit is a property of the viewer, timed with scalar variability. A per-partner limit can be learned from the partner's own gaze breaks, but the default should sit around 3 s with breaks jittered (s.d. ~0.7 s), not at a fixed period.
- Caution: this is silent, one-directional video between strangers. In conversation (rogers-2018-dual-eye-tracking-mutual-gaze.md) mutual face gaze is shorter still (~2.2 s). An intimate partner may tolerate more; this paper does not test that.
