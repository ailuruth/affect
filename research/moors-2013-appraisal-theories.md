# Appraisal theories of emotion: state of the art and future development

Agnes Moors, Phoebe C. Ellsworth, Klaus R. Scherer and Nico H. Frijda, *Emotion Review* 5(2):119–124, 2013, doi:10.1177/1754073912468165. Read from the published version in the University of Geneva open archive (unige:97856). © the authors (SAGE).

## What was read
The whole article, extracted with pdftotext, from the introduction to the conclusion. I skimmed the references. It introduces a special section; the other papers in that section were not read.

## Claims
- **Emotions are processes, not states.** "Emotion" is shorthand for an emotional episode.
- **The theories are componential.** The components are:
  - appraisal;
  - motivation (action tendencies and action readiness);
  - somatic (peripheral physiology);
  - motor (expressive and instrumental behaviour);
  - feeling.
- **The process is continuous and recursive.** "Changes in one component feed back to other components". "Several emotional episodes may run in parallel". Under **immediate efference**, a component need not finish before it changes later components.
- **Appraisal "detects and assesses the significance of the environment for well-being"**, which means the satisfaction or obstruction of **concerns**: "needs, attachments, values, current goals, and beliefs". It is therefore "inherently transactional".
- **Core appraisal variables** (substantial agreement): goal relevance, goal congruence, certainty, coping potential (control or power) and agency. Some theories add novelty, intrinsic pleasantness, urgency, norm compatibility and others.
- **Mechanisms.**
  - Two-mode views (rule-based plus associative) and three-mode views (adding sensory-motor) are both held.
  - Appraisal "often proceeds automatically"; practice automatizes it. It "often involves the recognition of action affordances", and it is not mainly abstract cognition.
  - Whether checks run sequentially or in parallel is unresolved.
- **How appraisal reaches the other components.** There are two proposals:
  - appraisal values form a pattern, which works like an emotion representation and then drives the components (Lazarus);
  - each appraisal value directly drives components with no emotion representation between them (Ellsworth, Scherer). In one variant this goes through the motivational component first (Frijda, Scherer).
- **Differentiation follows the appraisals available.** "If only a few appraisals yield results, the emotional experience is relatively undifferentiated and global." Also, "the generalized distress experienced by infants differentiates into sadness and anger when the infant is able to perceive differences in agency." Expectancy-based surprise depends on a cognitive restructuring "around 9 months".
- **Stimulus–emotion relations vary, but appraisal–emotion relations are stable.** "The same appraisals lead to the same emotions; different appraisals lead to different emotions."

## Evidence
A summary article. The examples are cultural differences in agency appraisals (Japanese versus Americans; Imada & Ellsworth 2011) and developmental timing (Scherer, Zentner & Stern 2004).

## Mechanistic commitments
1. Evaluation is **relative to concerns**. With no concerns there is no emotion. In the brief's terms, concerns are the agent's body variables and the goals learned from them.
2. **A small set of evaluation dimensions.** Their combinations give differentiated emotions.
3. **Recursive coupling** among components.
4. **Developmental differentiation**: new emotions appear when the agent becomes able to compute a new appraisal dimension.

## What it implies for a single formal core
The concerns clause is the bridge to a homeostatic core. If concerns are the core's satiable body variables (Principle 6), goal relevance and congruence are derivable as the magnitude and sign of an event's effect on predicted need.

**Agency** and **coping potential** are the dimensions a homeostatic core does not supply by default:
- agency needs a model of **who caused** the change (self, partner, world). This is a causal attribution in the agent's generative model;
- coping potential needs the **controllability** of value, roughly the spread of value across available actions.

Both can come out of a model-based core (a learned world model plus planning). That makes them derivation obligations, not modules.

The developmental claim is the sharpest prediction for an engine that starts from an infant body: a **distress that is not yet differentiated** should split into a protest-like approach mode and a withdrawal mode **only after** the agent's model separates partner-caused from world-caused changes.

## What an ablation could separate
- **Agency.** Train the agent's world model without partner-versus-world attribution, for example by merging the partner's action channel into "world". The prediction: no split between protest and withdrawal after partner-caused versus world-caused losses. Against an intact agent, this is a developmental double dissociation.
- **Coping potential.** Remove the agent's actions that affect the outcome, by making the loss uncontrollable. The negative state should shift from active to passive, the appraisal-theory analogue of learned helplessness.
- **Concern dependence.** Same stimulus, different need state. Responses should differ, so appraisal relative to concerns is distinct from stimulus–response. If the response does not change with need, the core is not appraising.
- **Direct efference versus a pattern representation.** Check whether the face's response to a mixed appraisal is the sum of the single-dimension responses (direct efference) or snaps to the nearest prototype (pattern representation).

## Verdict: adopt (appraisal dimensions as derivation targets, especially agency and coping potential; developmental-differentiation test)
It defines the family more than it gives a mechanism. It is the clearest list of evaluation dimensions a core must produce.
