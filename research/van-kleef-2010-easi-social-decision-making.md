## Claims
- **Two premises.** (1) People use others' emotions to make sense of "fuzzy" social situations. (2) The cooperative vs competitive structure of the situation "fundamentally changes the meaning and social consequences of emotional expressions."
- **Two routes, as in Van Kleef 2009.**
  - Inferential: strategic inferences about the other's appraisal and intentions.
  - Affective: contagion, then affect infusion ("how do I feel about it?") and mood management (relieve my own caught sadness by helping).
- **Two moderators, with signs given in Fig. 1:**
  - Perceived competitiveness: + to the inferential path, − to the affective path.
  - Epistemic motivation: + to the inferential path, − to the affective path.

  The authors argue that the two are empirically independent: competitive predisposition correlates only weakly and non-significantly with need for cognition and closure (De Dreu, Koole & Oldersma 1999).
- **Contagion is itself context-dependent.** It is "more prevalent in cooperative rather than in competitive situations." Evidence cited:
  - Totterdell 2000: cricket players' moods track their own team, not the opposing team.
  - Lanzetta & Englis 1989: expecting cooperation gives congruent facial EMG and autonomic responses to pleasure and distress; expecting competition gives "counter-empathic," opposite-signed responses.
- **Table 1: each emotion class carries a social signal, and its effect flips by setting.** Responses use Horney's move toward / away / against:

| Expression class | Signal | Cooperative setting | Competitive setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Happiness, joy | opportunity, affiliation | move toward (cooperate) | move against (exploit: "they are satisfied, I can demand more") |
| Anger, frustration | dominance, aggression | move against (less cooperation) | move toward (concede to avoid impasse) |
| Sadness, disappointment, worry | supplication | move toward (help) | move away / ignore, or against |
| Guilt, regret, embarrassment | appeasement | move toward (repair sustained) | move against (they will concede, so I wait) |

- **Valence does not predict the response.** Happiness and guilt both invite competition. Anger and guilt are both negative, yet anger tends to draw cooperation and guilt invites exploitation.
- **Mediation differs by setting.** In cooperative settings the evidence points to affective mediation (contagion, then affect infusion). "Few studies have found evidence of a role for inferential processes," and those few concern anger with high epistemic motivation. In competitive settings almost every study shows inferential mediation or moderation by epistemic motivation.
- "Moving away" is predicted but almost never measured.

## Evidence (primary numbers I read)
Van Kleef, De Dreu & Manstead 2004a. Students negotiated with a simulated buyer over six rounds of offers on three issues. The opponent's emotion was given as typed statements ("this negotiation pisses me off"), not as faces.

**Exp. 2.1 (N = 118, three conditions)**
- Demands fell from round 1 (M = 629) to round 6 (M = 462).
- Opponent emotion affected mean demand: angry 490, non-emotional 540, happy 553; F(2,115) = 5.66, p < .005.
- Concession ("distance travelled"): angry 210, non-emotional 173, happy 122.
- Contagion occurred in feeling. Participants facing anger reported more anger, F(2,115) = 8.01. Those facing happiness reported more happiness, F(2,115) = 3.36. Their own emotions did not predict their behaviour in the contagion direction.
- Impressions of the opponent: happy 3.55, neutral 2.75, angry 1.68; F(2,115) = 43.73.

**Exp. 2.2 (about 93 participants; 2 emotions × 3 opponent concession sizes)**
- Concession: angry 222 vs happy 131, F(1,87) = 18.48.
- The emotion effect depended on the opponent's own behaviour:
  - small opponent concessions: F(1,87) = 18.08;
  - moderate: F(1,87) = 4.05;
  - large: F(1,87) = 1.21, ns.
- Estimated opponent limit (1–15 scale): angry 8.27 vs happy 7.29, F(1,87) = 8.38. This effect also held only in the small-concession condition.
- Mediation in the small-concession condition: emotion → limit β = .40; limit → concession β = .37; Sobel Z = 2.67. The mediation was partial.

**Studies reported by the chapter (not read in the original)**
- 2004b: the anger-vs-happiness effect on demands appears under low time pressure, low need for closure and low power, and is absent under the opposites (Fig. 5).
- Steinel et al. 2008: anger at the offer brings more concession than happiness; anger at the person brings less.
- Van Dijk et al. 2008:
  - Anger leads to more generous ultimatum offers when rejection is costly.
  - When rejection is cheap or the bargainer is powerful, anger leads to *less* generous offers. That reversal is mediated by the bargainer's own anger.
  - Anger also leads to more deception.
- Van Kleef et al. 2006a, Exp. 3: disappointment produced concession when the opponent was described as cooperative and no effect when described as competitive (Fig. 4).
- Van Beest et al. 2008: in coalition formation, people conceded more to an angry party when they had to include it, and excluded it when they could.
- Krumhuber et al. 2007: authentic smiles raise trust-game cooperation over fake smiles or none (Fig. 2; the bar values cannot be read off the manuscript figure).

**Limits of the evidence**
- Most negotiation studies use typed emotion words from a simulated opponent, with Western student samples.
- No facial measures of the participant are taken. "Contagion" here is self-reported feeling, not facial mimicry.
- The facial evidence for sign flips is Lanzetta & Englis 1989, cited, not tested here.

## Formal model
None, verbal only. Fig. 1 gives qualitative signs (+/−) for each moderator on each path. Table 1 gives a qualitative sign table of expression class × setting. No functional form is stated.

## What it implies for the design
- **Per-expression sign flips come from interdependence, not from a gain.**
  - Table 1 is the clearest statement in the literature of why mimicry-context-synthesis.md's table shows different rows moving in different directions. Each expression says something different about the partner's appraisal. What that information means for *my* outcome depends on whether our outcomes are positively or negatively linked.
  - My formalisation, not the paper's: let ρ ∈ [−1, 1] be the inferred correlation of the agent's and the partner's outcomes. Let m(x) be what the partner's expression x reveals about the partner's appraisal and next act. The agent's response is then the change in its *own* expected need trajectory given m(x) and ρ.
    - Happiness ("partner's goal is being met"): good for me if ρ > 0, bad if ρ < 0. Sign flips with ρ.
    - Anger ("partner blocked, blames someone, may act"): it predicts a costly act by the partner, so conceding avoids loss in either setting. In a cooperative setting it also signals a strained relationship. This gives a different ρ-profile from happiness.
    - Supplication: it matters only if the partner's loss is my loss (ρ > 0). This gates rather than flips, matching the "ignored" cell.
    - Appeasement: it predicts that the partner will concede. Under ρ < 0 that is exploitable.
  - Four expressions with four different ρ-profiles from one interdependence variable means the response is not a gain on a copy. Copying is also not on the path for any ρ: it appears only where the agent's concern-relative appraisal happens to coincide with the partner's (shared goals, ρ ≈ 1, and an outcome that befalls both).
- **This is exactly the human finding.**
  - Contagion is "more prevalent in cooperative situations."
  - Under a social-appraisal reading, convergent feeling is what inference produces when concerns are aligned, not a separate transfer channel.
  - The design should derive the reciprocal case from the inference (shared concerns lead to the same appraisal) and not add a contagion path with its own weight.
- **Does a hidden dial remain? Yes, two candidates. Neither is flat-to-copy, if built as above.**
  - (a) **ρ.** It is a scalar that, if fixed globally, sets every row at once. The data need ρ per partner and per episode (Hofman 2012: one unfair offer flips smile return at once), and inferred jointly from the same expressions. Van Kleef & Côté 2022 report that observers infer the interdependence structure *from* the expressions (Van Doorn et al. 2012).
  - (b) **Epistemic motivation.** In Bayesian terms it reads as the weight given to the partner's expression relative to the prior over the situation. Exp. 2.2 shows this weight is not a trait-like gain. The expression moved behaviour only when the opponent's concessions left the outcome uncertain (F = 18.08 vs 1.21 ns). That is a value-of-information gate, not a constant.
  - **Risk.** If "low epistemic motivation" is implemented as falling back to a contagion route, the old dial returns: low motivation leads to copying. The 2004a data argue against that fallback. Even when inference was weak, the act did not follow the caught feeling (Exp. 2.1).
  - Van Dijk 2008's reversal (cheap rejection or high power: own anger drives meaner offers) is the one case where behaviour followed the caught feeling. There the reciprocal feeling acted through the agent's own appraisal (I am being pushed around), which is itself concern-relative.
- **Moving away matters for the agent.** Disengagement (turning away, withholding the face, ending the exchange) is a predicted response class that the field barely measured. For a body-first agent it is a cheap, distinct output: gaze aversion and reduced expressivity. It should be a first-class option, not a zero of the gain.
- **Labels.** As with EASI 2009, the classes are emotion labels. A label-free version replaces "anger signals dominance" with a learned predictive map: partner face → partner's next act → change in the agent's own needs, conditioned on the shared episode.

## Verdict: adopt (Table 1 as the target pattern; the moderators as a warning)
The best source for the claim that the sign of the response depends on the expression × interdependence, and for the primary demonstration that caught feeling and chosen act can diverge (2004a Exp. 2.1). Do not implement its two moderators as a mixing weight between a contagion route and an inference route, because that weight is the flat-to-copy dial again. All facial-response claims still need the facial studies (Lanzetta & Englis 1989; Seibt 2015).
