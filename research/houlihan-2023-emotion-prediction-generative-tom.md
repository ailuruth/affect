## Claims
- Observers predict a person's emotional reaction to an event by (1) inferring the person's preferences and beliefs from their action by inverse planning, (2) computing how the person would appraise the outcome against those inferred contents, and (3) mapping appraisals to emotion concepts. Tested on a public one-shot prisoner's dilemma ("Split or Steal", Golden Balls).
- Observers attribute **social** preferences (inequity aversion, both advantageous and disadvantageous) and **second-order reputational** preferences (caring what others will infer about one's values). These are needed for nearly every emotion, not only "social" ones: observers predict similar joy for a player who split (half the pot) and one who stole (whole pot).
- The same outcome yields different predicted emotions depending on the player's own action: after the opponent steals, a cooperator is predicted to feel envy and contempt, a defector regret; after the opponent splits, a cooperator relief and gratitude, a defector guilt.
- Sparse personalising information (a photo and a job description) shifts inferred preferences and thus predicted emotions (the engineer who split is predicted to feel more envy than the councilman who split, because he is inferred to have stronger disadvantageous-inequity aversion).
- The learned appraisal-to-emotion map is distinct for each of 20 emotions.

## Formal model
- Base utility (Fehr & Schmidt 1999 form, prospect-theory value ν, reference point π_Money):
  E[U_base(a1)] = Σ_{a2} π_{a2} · [ ω_Money · ν(Money − π_Money) − ω_AIA · ν(AI) − ω_DIA · ν(DI) ]   (3.1)
  AI = how much more player 1 got than player 2; DI = how much more player 2 got.
- Reputation (an embedded inverse-planning loop: what a rational observer would infer about my base weights from my action):
  E[U_base+repu(a1)] = E[U_base(a1)] − ω^repu_Money · ν(E[ω_Money | a1]·pot) + ω^repu_AIA · ν(E[ω_AIA | a1]·pot) + ω^repu_DIA · ν(E[ω_DIA | a1]·pot)   (3.2)
- Action: P(a1 | ω, π_{a2}) ∝ exp(λ · E[U(a1)]) (3.3); inversion P(ω, π_{a2} | a1) ∝ P(a1 | ω, π_{a2}) P(ω, π_{a2}) (3.4).
- Computed appraisals (19 dimensions) over each utility term: achieved utility AU, prediction error PE = AU − EU, |PE| of the opponent's action, and counterfactuals CF_a2 (if the opponent had chosen otherwise, weighted by belief it would) and CF_a1 (if I had chosen otherwise, weighted by P(¬a1 | ω, a2)).
- Emotion concepts: a sparse linear transformation β (Laplace prior) from the joint distribution of computed appraisals to 20 rated emotions, fitted on generic players, scale cross-validated on specific players.

## Evidence
- Training (GenericPlayers): n = 554 observers, 8 trials each, 20 emotions; each outcome expectation from 1,108 judgements. Test (SpecificPlayers): n = 1,512 observers, 20 players, 12,096 emotion predictions per model comparison; pots $2 to $207,365.
- Overall fit, Lin's concordance: ComputedAppraisals ccc = .854 [.844, .859]; InversePlanningLesion (preferences from the prior, not the action) .762; SocialLesion (money only) .663.
- Personalisation (difference between specific and generic predictions): ComputedAppraisals ccc = .439, r = .471; InversePlanningLesion ccc = .089; SocialLesion ccc = .116. So inverse planning and social preferences are needed to adjust predictions to the individual, even where the lesioned models fit average intensities.
- Surprise was poorly identifiable (observers rated all outcomes surprising); the model learned surprise as a function of PE on disadvantageous inequity, and the inverse-planning lesion could not capture surprise at all.

## What it implies for the design
- **The relation enters as a utility over the joint outcome, not as a gain on the partner.** In the Fehr-Schmidt form, the partner's gain enters my utility with a sign that depends on which side of equality I am: disadvantageous inequity (the partner got more) is a loss, advantageous inequity (I got more) is also a loss for an averse person, and the partner's absolute welfare can be added with either sign. This is exactly the structure that produces the human sign flips: a rival's distress is my relative gain; a partner's anger at a shared foe is aligned with my concern; a cooperating partner's anger is a threat to the joint project that calls for soothing. None of these is a scalar on the partner's face.
- **Each concern is a separate term with its own weight** (ω_Money, ω_AIA, ω_DIA, and their reputational versions). That gives several weights, each acting on a different appraisal, not one dial. Per-expression gating arises because different expressions are evidence about different terms (the partner's satisfaction, the partner's view of me, the partner's expectation).
- **Reputation is a second-order loop**: what the partner will infer about me from my action. The agent's facial response to the partner is itself an action the partner will read, so the same machinery (the agent models the partner's inverse model of the agent) gives a reason for display rules, for withholding mimicry to signal distance (Fischer & Hess 2017 in the mimicry synthesis), and for polite smiles.
- **Prediction error and counterfactuals are the appraisal primitives**, and they are concern-relative differences: computable on the agent's own body variables (achieved change in a need, change relative to expectation, change relative to what would have happened if the partner had acted otherwise). That last one is directly a contingency quantity, which earlier sketches (contingency operators) computed without this interpretation.
- **Hidden scalar check.** λ (softmax rationality) is a single scalar; it sets how sharply an action is evidence about preferences. It moves confidence in inferred preferences, not the sign or shape of the agent's response. The weights ω define the shape of the response and are a vector. A design built on this would hide a flat-to-copy dial only if it collapsed the concern vector to one "how much I care about the partner's welfare" weight w and made the response proportional to w × (partner's inferred appraisal). That collapse is exactly the empathy-as-gain model; the paper's model comparison (SocialLesion, ccc .663 vs .854) is evidence that observers do not use it.
- **What does not transfer.** The game is fully specified (actions, payoffs, pot), utilities are hand-chosen from behavioural economics, emotion labels are human ratings (against Principles 1-2), and the model predicts emotions from events without any face. For the agent the open problem is the outcome space: in conversation there is no payoff matrix, so the "shared outcome" must be represented in the agent's own need coordinates and learned.

## Verdict: adopt (the utility structure as the mechanism for sign flips; reject the labelled concept layer)
The most fully specified generative model of social appraisal available. Its lesion results show that inferred social concerns, not the outcome alone, drive predicted reactions; its Fehr-Schmidt utility shows how a relation can be a vector of signed concern weights rather than one gain.
