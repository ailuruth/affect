# Teyler & Rudy 2007, "The hippocampal indexing theory and episodic memory: Updating the index" (Hippocampus 17(12):1158-1169; doi 10.1002/hipo.20350)

Source read: **the abstract only** (via Europe PMC, PMID 17696170). The full text is closed access. Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar and Unpaywall list no open copy, and the publisher PDF returned a paywall page. No unofficial copy was sought. Read on 2026-11-25.

To place the paper I also used two secondary sources, which are not substitutes for reading it:
- McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly 1995 (read in full; see mcclelland-1995-complementary-learning-systems.md), section "Binding", which discusses the original Teyler & DiScenna 1986 proposal at length.
- Goode, Tanaka, Sahay & McHugh 2020, "An integrated index: engrams, place cells, and hippocampal memory" (Neuron; PMC7486247). I read its first ~100k characters through an automated summary, not line by line.

Treat every claim below that is attributed to Teyler & Rudy beyond the abstract as unverified.

## Claims (from the abstract)
- The 1986 theory "offered an account of episodic memory based on the intrinsic organization of the hippocampus, its synaptic physiology and its anatomical relationship to other regions of the brain."
- "the hippocampus was functionally designed and anatomically situated to capture information about neocortical activity generated by the individual features of behavioral episode."
- "because the hippocampus projects back to these neocortical regions the information it stored could serve as an index to the pattern of neocortical activity produced by the episode. Consequently, a partial cue that activated the index could activate the neocortical patterns and thus retrieve the memory of the episode."
- Conclusion: the theory "has aged very well. Its core ideas can be seen in many contemporary theories and there is a wealth of data that support this conceptual framework."

## Context from secondary sources (not Teyler & Rudy's own text)
- McClelland et al. 1995 summarize Teyler & DiScenna 1986 as holding that the hippocampus "does not store the memory itself but, rather, stores only a list of addresses of or pointers to the diverse locations in the neocortex where the memory itself is stored". The local cortical content is laid down by fast plasticity in cortex at the time of learning. CLS reconciles this by reading the hippocampal "addresses" as compressed codes, and by suggesting that the encode and decode pathways, and possibly superficial cortical layers, belong to the fast or stable side.
- Goode et al. 2020 restate indexing as: the hippocampus "generates a code or 'index' that binds neuronal activity patterns underlying an experiential event", the memory being "stored across distributed neocortical (and potentially subcortical) modules". Their quantitative points come from other labs' work: about 50% of CA1 pyramidal cells are place cells and about a quarter of those are c-Fos engram cells (Tanaka 2018); engram labelling captures about 20% of CA1 pyramidal cells; reactivating "2–3%" of labelled DG granule cells reproduces context-appropriate behaviour (Liu 2012); only about 15% of place cells are stable over weeks (Ziv 2013). Behaviour driven by DG engram reactivation "reflects the valence of the reinforcing stimuli". They "favor ... a time-dependent shift in the indexing function from the hippocampus to cortical traces."

## Evidence
None verified from the paper itself. The abstract asserts "a wealth of data" without numbers.

## Mechanism (as stated in the abstract)
- At encoding, the hippocampus stores a conjunctive, sparse pointer (the index) to the co-active neocortical feature patterns. Plasticity is in the index, not necessarily in the content.
- At retrieval, a partial cue activates part of the index; the index completes and, through return projections, reinstates the full cortical pattern.
- No equations given.

## Implications for the engine (our inference)
- **Store keys and content separately.** The engine's buffer can hold a compact key per episode (a time, a partner and state tag, a low-dimensional latent summary) that points to stored feature streams on disk. Principle 7 already requires features, not raw media. Replay then means: select keys by weight, load the referenced feature segment, and re-encode it with the current encoder. This also answers Kumaran 2016's "index maintenance" question for S6's model upgrades. If keys are derived from the current encoder, they must be recomputed after an upgrade; if they are tags plus timestamps, they survive.
- **Partial-cue retrieval while awake is a second replay channel.** When the operator's face or voice partially matches a stored key, the index completes to the episode and reinstates its state context during the live conversation. That is state-dependent recall, and per McClelland 1995 it is the kind of reinstatement that should refresh the trace. It is ablatable: disable online retrieval and keep offline replay.
- **Valence lives in what the index reactivates, not in the index.** If Goode's summary is right that engram reactivation reproduces the valence of the original reinforcer, then the state tag need not be a label stored with the key. It can be whatever body and interoceptive state the reinstated pattern drives. This fits Principle 2 (no hand-named emotion variables), and it is our reading.

## Verdict: watch (unread)
Only the abstract was read. The index idea is fully represented in the two CLS papers read for this topic, which explicitly absorb it. Upgrade the verdict only if the full text is obtained and contains specific mechanisms beyond CLS, for example on how semantic memory forms when the index is lost, or on context and fear-conditioning data.
