# Jones 2009, "The development of imitation in infancy" (Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 364(1528):2325-2335, doi:10.1098/rstb.2009.0045, PMC2865075)

Source read: the full text from PubMed Central (sections 1-7, all body text). The reference list was not read. It is a review by the author of the arousal account of neonatal tongue protrusion; her own studies (Jones 1996, 2006, 2007) are described here in summary, not read in the original.

## Claims
- Abstract: "The widely accepted view that newborn infants imitate lacks supporting evidence. Instead, existing data suggest that infants do not imitate others until their second year, and that imitation of different kinds of behaviour emerges at different ages." Imitation is "not an inherited, specialized module, but is instead the emergent product of a system of social, cognitive and motor components, each with its own developmental history".
- Matching is not the same as imitation: "imitation is only one possible interpretation of behavioural matching".
- Only tongue protrusion holds up. Anisfeld (1996) reviewed 35 newborn studies, 32 of which used tongue protrusion, and found "only tongue protruding was reliably matched by newborns in different studies and in different laboratories". Mouth opening was not reliably matched.
- Arousal account: newborns protrude the tongue to many arousing non-face stimuli: touches on the palm (Humphrey 1970), a looming pen or ball (Jacobson 1979), a box with a blue lining opening and closing (Legerstee 1991), and 20-s segments of the Barber of Seville overture in the standard imitation paradigm, with a pattern that "matched the pattern in imitation experiments very closely" (Jones 2006). Infants looked longer at a tongue-protruding face than a mouth-opening face (Jones 1996). So tongue protrusion to a tongue model is "infants responding as they usually respond to an interesting/arousing stimulus" and the match is "nothing more than a coincidence".
- The arousal account does not predict diffuse arousal; it predicts a specific increase in tongue protrusion, which she relates to oral exploration: tongue protrusion to dangling toys stopped "in the same week ... in which he first successfully reached and grasped a toy" (two infants followed weekly from 3 weeks).
- Other reported newborn imitations (pouting, finger sequences, head rotation, index-finger extension, sideways tongue) are unreplicated and explicable by arousal. Meltzoff & Moore 1977's judge data counted the first two of four rankings as correct, so chance was 50%. In Nagy et al. 2007, the girls who matched finger movements more also had higher heart rates.
- Primates: newborn chimpanzees matched tongue protrusion and mouth opening only during weeks 1-8 (n = 2); rhesus infants matched lip smacking or tongue protrusion only on day 3, not days 1, 7 or 14. Adult chimpanzees reproduce modelled actions "only about 5 per cent of the time". All matched behaviours in all three species are mouth behaviours.
- What true newborn imitation would require (built-in knowledge of own body parts without vision, their action capabilities, the mapping to another's body; plus vision, memory, intermodal matching, and "the motivation (some reason) to imitate"). Meltzoff & Moore's AIM (active intermodal mapping) model posits these; "research has not yet established the psychological reality of the AIM mechanism". Meltzoff & Borton's (1979) touch-to-vision shape transfer failed to replicate with added controls (Maurer et al. 1999).
- An innate mirror system for newborns would need its sensory side inherited, since the newborn has never seen others do the act; monkey mirror neurons respond to goal-directed acts, and adult monkey mirror neurons "learn" to respond to new stimuli (Ferrari et al. 2005).
- Static emotional faces: newborn matching of emotional expressions (Field et al. 1982) involves "species-typical, apparently innate behaviours" that are "readily but reflexively matched", so imitation is not the only mechanism.
- After the newborn period: matching of tongue protrusion and mouth opening declines after month 2 (Fontaine 1984; Heimann et al. 1989, gone by 3 months). In parent-infant interaction, it is the adults who imitate: parents matched infant vocalisations "about two to three times in each 10 min session", infants matched parents "about once in every 20-30 min" (Kokkinaki & Kugiumutzakis 2000, 2-6 months); Pawlby 1977 found the same over 4-10 months.
- Elicited imitation, 6-20 months (Jones 2007): only "Aaah" met the criterion before 12 months; the other seven behaviours met it between 12 and 18 months; tongue protrusion only at 18 months and finger sequences at 16 months. Masur: under one apparent imitation per session at 10 and 13 months, four or five at 17 and 21 months. At 15 months, modelled production exceeded spontaneous production for 8 of 22 acts (Abravanel et al. 1976). Horne & Erjavec 2007: 1-2-year-olds trained to match four actions did not generalise to four untrained ones.
- Many "imitation" results in the first year may be emulation (learning what the object affords) rather than copying actions.

## Evidence
A review. Numbers above are as reported by Jones. The strongest direct data are her own: tongue protrusion to music and toys in the standard paradigm, its disappearance at reach onset, and the 6-20-month elicited imitation study.

## What it implies for the engine
- Do not hard-wire a copy-what-you-see mapping. The reliable neonatal phenomenon is an innate arousal/interest-driven oral response (tongue protrusion) that happens to match one model; it is an innate reflex tied to arousal and exploration, not an innate self-other correspondence. The engine's innate body can include such a reflex (an arousal-gated orofacial exploration response) without implying mimicry.
- The early mirroring in the dyad is done by the partner: parents imitate infants several times more often than infants imitate parents. In living with the operator, the engine is in the infant's seat, but the operator is not going to mirror it like a parent. The engine therefore cannot rely on the human to supply the dense contingent mirroring that infants get; a substitute is self-observation (seeing its own face rendered) plus whatever mirroring occurs. This is a design risk to record.
- Facial imitation that is real appears in the second year, piecemeal, behaviour by behaviour. A learned mimicry map in the engine should be expected to grow act by act, with each correspondence acquired separately, not switched on as a whole.
- Rapid matching of emotional expressions (not arbitrary gestures) may be a separate, reflexive matching of species-typical displays. This is the one place Jones leaves room for something innate in facial matching, and it is not imitation. Compare Heyes's account, where emotional mimicry is also learned (see ray-heyes-2011-wealth-of-the-stimulus.md).
