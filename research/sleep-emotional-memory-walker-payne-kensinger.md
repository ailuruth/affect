# Sleep and emotional memory: Walker & van der Helm 2009 vs Payne & Kensinger (2008, 2018)

Three sources, read as one note because they give two different answers to the same design question: when an affect engine replays tagged experience offline, should replay keep the affect, or remove it?

- Walker, M. P. & van der Helm, E. (2009). "Overnight Therapy? The Role of Sleep in Emotional Brain Processing." *Psychological Bulletin* 135(5), 731-748. doi:10.1037/a0016570.
- Payne, J. D., Stickgold, R., Swanberg, K. & Kensinger, E. A. (2008). "Sleep preferentially enhances memory for emotional components of scenes." *Psychological Science* 19(8), 781-788. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02157.x.
- Payne, J. D. & Kensinger, E. A. (2018). "Stress, sleep, and the selective consolidation of emotional memories." *Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences* 19, 36-43. doi:10.1016/j.cobeha.2017.09.006.

## Claims

### Walker & van der Helm: "sleep to forget, sleep to remember" (SFSR)
- Emotional experiences are encoded and consolidated more robustly than neutral ones because of "the autonomic neurochemical reactions that are elicited at the time of the experience". Yet "later recall ... tends not to be associated with anywhere near the same magnitude of autonomic (re)activation". The "affective blanket" is removed while "the information contained within that experience (the memory) remains."
- The decoupling "preferentially takes place overnight, such that we 'sleep to forget' the emotional tone yet 'sleep to remember' the tagged memory of that episode".
- They give REM as the place for it, for three reasons. (1) Limbic structures reactivate in REM ("amygdala, the hippocampus, and the anterior cingulate" are elevated). (2) Theta synchrony allows "integration ... of recently experienced emotional events in the context of pre-existing neocortically stored semantic memory". (3) Most important, this happens "within a brain that is devoid of aminergic neurochemical concentration", in particular without locus coeruleus noradrenaline. The result is "depotentiating ... the autonomic-arousing charge originally acquired at the time of learning (the emotion)".
- Wake and sleep are two mechanisms. In wake, consolidation is "predominately by adrenergic mechanisms". In REM it happens "by way of cholinergic modulation, in the absence of adrenergic influence". The qualitative difference between them is the whole point.
- Repetition until it succeeds: "if this process of divorcing emotion from memory is not achieved across the first night ... a repeat attempt ... will occur on the second night, as the strength of the 'emotional tag' ... will remain high." Repeated failure is their account of PTSD nightmares.
- Pathology predictions. Excess or early REM (as in depression) "may disproportionately amplify the strength of negative memories". Sleep loss biases encoding toward negative memories. Together these yield "a perceived autobiographical history dominated by negative life events".

### Payne et al. 2008: selective consolidation of the emotional centre
- "Sleep ... led to a preservation of negative objects, but not their backgrounds". "Time spent awake led to forgetting of the entire negative scene."
- Interpretation: scene components "become 'unbound' during sleep, allowing sleep to selectively preserve only what is calculated to be most salient". Sleep "may preferentially promote memory for gist over detail".
- Sleep did not benefit neutral scenes. The authors suggest that "mixed presentation of neutral and emotional stimuli biases processing toward enhancing only the emotional information" (untested).

### Payne & Kensinger 2018: tag at encoding, execute in sleep
- Arousal neuromodulators "(e.g. norepinephrine, cortisol) present during and after learning help set molecular 'tags' that designate specific traces ... to be prioritized for consolidation." Sleep's high-frequency events ("sharp wave ripples, sleep spindles, theta rhythm") are "essential for linking these distributed tags into the integrated memory trace".
- Both conditions are needed: "cortisol benefits effective tagging ... sleep enhances the efficiency with which those tags are executed."
- Two timescales of tag: "cortisol has a sluggish timecourse ... there must be a faster signal that denotes salience on a trial-by-trial basis". Trial-by-trial skin conductance is the candidate fast signal.
- From a meta-analysis (Shields et al. 2017, as summarised in Box 2), stress helps memory most when it "closely precedes learning", is "related to encoded content", and occurs "in the same context as learning".

## Evidence
### Payne et al. 2008 (primary data)
- N = 88 students, in four groups. Wake-delay: 24, study at 9 AM, test at 9 PM. Sleep-delay: 24, study at 9 PM, test at 9 AM after 7-8 h of sleep. Two 30-min controls: 20 at AM and 20 at PM. No polysomnography was recorded, so sleep stage is not measured in this study.
- Stimuli: 64 scenes, each studied for 5 s. 32 had a negative arousing object (arousal 5-7 and valence below 3 on 7-point scales) and 32 a neutral object, all on neutral backgrounds. Test: objects and backgrounds shown separately, each judged same, similar or new. "General recognition" = similar/(1 − same). False alarms were below 5% ("same") and below 20% ("similar").
- Trade-off present at both delays: Valence × Component F(1,86) = 68.7, p < .0001, ηp² = .44.
- Sleep vs wake on general recognition: the 3-way interaction F(1,46) = 4.7, p = .03, ηp² = .10. Negative objects: 68% after sleep vs 44% after wake, t(46) = 3.4, p = .001. Specific recognition: 68% vs 58%, t(46) = 2.7, p = .01. Backgrounds: 38% vs 38%.
- Change from the 30-min baseline. Wake: negative objects −16%, their backgrounds −9%. Sleep: backgrounds −9%, objects +8% (n.s.). Neutral scenes ended near 45 ± 4% under both sleep and wake.
- The object-background disparity "more than doubled" after sleep compared with the 30-min and wake conditions. AM and PM 30-min groups did not differ, and alertness was 3.0 ± .30 vs 3.3 ± .28 (p = .60), which argues against a circadian account.

### As reported by Walker & van der Helm 2009 (secondary to me)
- Encoding after sleep deprivation (Walker & Tharani 2009; the text says 36 h, the figure caption 38 h). Overall there was a "40% deficit". Positive and neutral items showed a "59% retention deficit", while negative items had "a markedly smaller and nonsignificant impairment".
- Consolidation window (Graves et al. 2003, mice): sleep deprivation 0-5 h after training impaired contextual fear memory, while deprivation at 5-10 h did not.
- Nap study (Nishida et al. 2009). After a 90-min nap, negative memory improved selectively. The gain correlated with REM amount (r = .63, p = .026) and with right-minus-left prefrontal theta in REM (r = .88, p < .001, peak at 5.75 Hz).
- Late-night (REM-rich) sleep favoured emotional over neutral texts (Wagner 2001), and the benefit persisted at 4 years (Wagner 2006).
- Reactivity after sleep loss (Yoo et al. 2007): amygdala response to aversive pictures was 60% greater, recruited volume was threefold, mPFC-amygdala connectivity was lost and amygdala-locus coeruleus connectivity rose.
- Acetylcholine in REM is "quadruple the levels seen during NREM and double those measured during quiet waking". Between 75% and 95% of dreams contain emotional content.
- Direct support for SFSR (Sterpenich et al. 2007): participants deprived of sleep on the first night showed lower recall at 72 h and no reduction in amygdala reactivity on re-exposure.
- Contrary evidence that the authors themselves cite (Wagner, Fischer & Born 2002): unpleasantness ratings increased after REM-rich late sleep relative to new pictures. There was no increase relative to the same pictures' pre-sleep baseline, and arousal fell numerically but not significantly. The authors leave the discrepancy unresolved ("remains unclear").
- Clinical associations. REM latency under 65 min predicts antidepressant response and relapse. One night of sleep deprivation helps 40-60% of depressed patients. Both are correlational and do not test the mechanism.
- The authors' own scope warning: "the majority of studies have principally examined negative and arousing emotional stimuli".

### As reported by Payne & Kensinger 2018
- Higher pre-encoding cortisol and larger trial-wise SCR predicted better later memory for negative items, but not neutral ones. These relations held "only in those who slept (and not those who remained awake)" (their Figure 2). The effect sizes are in figure panels only, and I did not extract numbers from them.
- Box 1: REM amount often correlates with emotional memory. SWS in a nap relates to selective emotional consolidation. Noradrenaline blockade during SWS disrupts temporal memory for emotional information. The authors also consider sequential SWS→REM models.

## Mechanism (as stated; no equations are given in any of the three)
1. **Encoding tag.** A salience tag is set on each trace by arousal neuromodulators close in time to the event. It has a slow component (cortisol, background stress level) and a fast one (sympathetic or SCR response to the specific item). Tags are set per component, not per episode: the negative object is tagged, the neutral background is not.
2. **Offline selection.** During sleep, reactivation favours tagged traces. Untagged components decay at the wake rate, and tagged ones are held or strengthened. The result is gist plus the emotional centre, with context lost.
3. **Offline transformation (SFSR only).** The selected traces are re-run under a neuromodulatory condition that differs from encoding: low noradrenaline and high acetylcholine in REM. The content association is strengthened and integrated with neocortical knowledge. The trace's bound autonomic response is depotentiated.
4. **Iteration.** The tag persists until the decoupling succeeds, so the same trace is selected again on later nights. Failure gives repetitive replay and persistent reactivity.

The two positions agree on steps 1-2. Payne and Kensinger say nothing about whether the affect attached to the kept trace weakens, so they neither assert nor test step 3. The conflict between the positions is therefore narrower than it looks. Selective consolidation answers *which content survives*. SFSR adds a claim about *what happens to the affective response attached to the content that survives*. The only direct test of that second claim in these sources goes both ways: Sterpenich 2007 supports it, and Wagner 2002 points the other way on valence ratings.

## Implications for the engine (our inference, not the authors')
**What gets replayed.** Replay is selected by a tag that the core produces at encoding time. It is not a label (Principle 2). Following Payne & Kensinger, the tag should have two time constants:
- a fast per-moment term: the magnitude of the engine's own arousal or interoceptive change at that frame, the analogue of SCR;
- a slow multiplicative context term: a leaky integral of recent need or stress, the analogue of cortisol.

A candidate is tag_t = g(s̄_t) · |Δa_t|, where a is the engine's arousal readout and s̄ is the slow stress integral. The tag should attach to segments within an interaction, not to whole sessions. That reproduces the object/background split: the partner's salient facial or vocal event is kept, and the surrounding neutral stretch decays. Under Principle 10 the tag cannot be a free-standing module. It has to be a quantity the core already computes (for a homeostatic core, for example, |Δ drive| or the costate magnitude; see the Schaul note on |δ| as priority and its bias).

**When.** Consolidation has a window: in Graves 2003, deprivation during the first 0-5 h after training mattered and deprivation at 5-10 h did not. That favours an offline phase soon after each daily session, not a weekly batch. The operator's routine of a few hours of contact a day followed by an overnight offline phase fits the biology's schedule.

**Two phases with different gains.** If SFSR is adopted, offline replay has two passes over the tagged buffer:
- (a) a content pass (the SWS-like phase), which trains the slow model to predict the replayed partner and context features;
- (b) an affect pass (the REM-like phase), which re-runs the same episodes through the affect pathway with the arousal or neuromodulatory gain clamped low. This makes the learned mapping from cue to autonomic response extinguish toward baseline, while the cue-to-content prediction keeps its strength.

The affect pass also sets the tag update. A natural rule is tag ← tag · r_replay / r_encode, where r is the arousal response the replay elicits. Episodes whose replayed response stays high keep their priority and return on the next night (SFSR's iteration), so a cap or decay is required to avoid a PTSD-like loop.

**Temperament risk.** Two effects compound. The reviews report that negative material survives degraded states better (the encoding asymmetry). Arousal-weighted replay also over-trains the slow store on the tagged minority (in Payne 2008, neutral material gained nothing from sleep). The slow store's prior, which is where a temperament would sit, could therefore drift negative. That is exactly the depression mechanism Walker and van der Helm propose. The engine needs:
- the importance-sampling correction from the Schaul note whenever the slow store's outputs are read as affect;
- a monitored valence balance of what gets replayed.

**Scarce data.** Selective replay is how few hours a day become enough. Each salient partner moment is replayed many times offline, and the neutral bulk is allowed to fade. The cost is overfitting to a handful of events. Replay of the untagged bulk at a low base rate (α < 1 in Schaul's terms) keeps the slow store calibrated.

**Partner specificity.** Under this scheme, the tagged segments are the ones that carry the partner's high-arousal contingencies: what their face did when the engine's state moved. Gist-over-detail consolidation predicts that the relationship stored in the slow store is the regularity of those central moments, not the background of each session.

**Ablations this gives (derived from the mechanism, to be re-derived from whichever core is chosen):**
1. Uniform replay (no tag weighting). Prediction: the Payne pattern disappears, and salient partner events decay at the same rate as neutral context.
2. Affect pass without the gain clamp, or with no affect pass. Prediction: the engine's arousal response to recalled partner events does not fall across nights, which is the Sterpenich deprivation result.
3. No offline phase. Prediction: uniform decay of content, as in Payne's wake group.
4. Fast tag without the slow context term, or the reverse. Prediction: weaker selectivity (Payne & Kensinger's two-signal claim).

## Verdict: adopt (encoding-time tag → selective offline replay); watch (SFSR affect depotentiation)
- **Adopt:** a tag set at encoding from the core's own arousal signal, with fast and slow components, used to weight offline replay at sub-episode granularity. This is well supported by primary data (Payne 2008) and by mechanism (see the synaptic tagging note). It meets Principle 10 only if the tag is a quantity the core already computes.
- **Watch:** the claim that offline replay under low arousal gain removes the affective charge while keeping content. It is attractive because it gives a falsifiable two-pass design and a pathology (repetitive replay) for free. But the direct evidence in these sources is split (Sterpenich 2007 for, Wagner 2002 partly against). Walker and van der Helm call the model "heuristic", and the work is almost entirely on negative stimuli. Build it as an ablatable switch, not as a foundation.
- **Reject:** importing the clinical claims (REM latency, antidepressants) as design constraints, because they are correlational.
