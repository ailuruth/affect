The old railway line that runs along the valley was built in the eighteen sixties, mostly by hand. The workers cut the route through soft clay in the lower fields and through hard grey stone near the top of the hill. Where the ground was too wet, they laid thick timber beams across the mud and covered them with crushed rock. Some of those beams are still there today, buried a metre below the path that walkers use now.

The line carried coal down from the mines and wool and timber from the farms higher up. A small station stood at every village, usually a single brick building with a ticket office, a waiting room and a coal store. Most of the stations have gone, but two remain. One is now a cafe that serves soup and bread to cyclists. The other is a private house with a bright red door and a garden full of vegetables.

If you walk the path from the bottom of the valley, the first thing you notice is how gently it climbs. Trains cannot cope with steep slopes, so the engineers kept the gradient low by curving the route around the side of each hill instead of going straight over it. That is why the path is twice as long as the road that runs beside it. On a bicycle it feels almost flat, even though you rise more than two hundred metres from start to finish.

About halfway up there is a tunnel. It is roughly four hundred metres long and slightly curved, so for a short stretch in the middle you cannot see daylight at either end. Lamps were fitted along the walls a few years ago. They switch on when they sense movement and switch off again a minute later. The air inside stays cool all year, and in winter the brick roof drips steadily onto the gravel.

Beyond the tunnel the valley opens out. The river below is wide and shallow here, and in dry summers you can see the old stone piers of a bridge that was washed away in a flood more than a century ago. A new bridge was built a little further upstream, made of iron instead of stone. It still carries the path across the water, and it rings faintly when a heavy cart rolls over it.

Near the top of the line is the junction where a short branch once led to a quarry. The quarry closed in the nineteen fifties and slowly filled with rainwater. It is now a deep green lake with steep walls on three sides. Swimming is not allowed, because the water is very cold and the bottom drops suddenly, but people come to sit on the flat rocks at the edge and eat their lunch.

The last station on the line sits at the head of the valley, where the hills meet the open moor. From here the trains used to turn round on a large iron turntable, which two men could push by hand once the engine was balanced on it. The turntable pit is still visible, a round hollow in the grass about fifteen metres across, with a ring of stone blocks around its rim.

Weather on the moor changes quickly. A clear morning can turn to low cloud within half an hour, and the wind is often strong enough to make cycling hard work. Most visitors check the forecast before they set off and carry a waterproof layer even in summer. The cafe at the old station keeps a board by the door with the latest report written in chalk.

The line itself closed to trains in the nineteen sixties, when the mines shut and lorries took over most of the freight. For twenty years the track bed was left alone, and trees grew up between the old sleepers. Then a group of local volunteers began clearing it, one short section at a time, with saws, shovels and a borrowed tractor. It took them eleven years to open the whole route as a path.

Today the path is looked after by a small trust. It repairs the surface after storms, clears fallen branches, and maintains the lamps in the tunnel and the railings on the bridge. Each spring it counts the number of people who use the path by fitting a small sensor beside the track for a month. Last year the count was a little over sixty thousand, about twice the number of ten years ago.

If you plan to walk the full length, allow about five hours, and remember that the last bus back down the valley leaves the top station at six in the evening.
